The names A marsh, a quail and a town that moved ten kilometres: the names here are older than the languages that use them.
Siracusa is the Greek Syrakousai, usually traced to Syrako, a marsh beside the first settlement. The Corinthians who founded it in 734–733 BC built on the island first, for the harbour and the spring; that island is Ortigia, Greek Ortygia, conventionally read as quail island. Noto is the Roman Netum, moved ten kilometres after the earthquake of 1693; the ridge where it stood is still called Noto Antica. Modica's chocolate has a name protected since 2018 — and its oldest maker is not allowed to use it.

The soul of Siracusa — the essay
The market before nine, a temple inside a cathedral, a harbour that swallowed a fleet, and the baroque towns that rose after 1693
THE ESSAY

It is a quarter to eight at the Ortigia market and the swordfish is already on the slab. The red prawns go first. By nine the cheese shop at the end of the street has a queue, and by noon the fishmongers are hosing down. Walk to the end and you are at the Temple of Apollo, one of the oldest Doric temples in Sicily, with the morning traffic going round it.

This is a city built in layers, and the layers are still in use. The Corinthians landed on Ortigia in 734–733 BC because it had a great harbour and a freshwater spring a few steps from the sea; the spring is still there, the Fonte Aretusa, with papyrus growing wild in it. The cathedral on Piazza Duomo was built into the Temple of Athena, and you can see the Doric columns in its walls. Caravaggio painted the burial of the city's saint here in 1608, while on the run. Cicero called it the greatest of the Greek cities and the most beautiful of all, and in 413 BC its harbour swallowed the entire Athenian fleet.

The greatest of the Greek cities, and the most beautiful of all.Cicero, Against Verres, 70 BC

The stage is still the stage. The Greek theatre is cut into the rock of the Temenite hill, and every spring since 1914 it has played Greek tragedy again, at dusk, to thousands — the 2027 season opens on 7 May. The puppeteers of Ortigia still carve and armour the knights they fight with.

Then the Val di Noto. After the earthquake of 11 January 1693 the towns of the south-east were rebuilt almost at once, in a honey-coloured late baroque: Noto ten kilometres from its ruins, Modica down two ravines, Ragusa Ibla on its spur. That is where the sweet table of this coast is most serious — Corrado Assenza's almond in Noto, Modica's chocolate worked cold — and where the best restaurant in the south-east, Ciccio Sultano's two-star Duomo, sits in Ibla. Southward the coast ends at Marzamemi, a village built round a tuna trap, where the fish still comes home as bottarga.

Come in October, when the summer crowd has gone and the sea is still warm enough to swim; or on 13 December, when Siracusa carries Santa Lucia's silver statue through the streets and eats boiled wheat instead of bread.

From Mithaecus to the red guide
The first Greek cookbook author, the Arab ice, the convent pastry, the baroque rebuilt in one go — and the stars that came after
2,500 years

Every plate on this coast is a stratum. Sicilians name the layers fluently — Greek, Arab, Norman, Spanish, convent — and the honest version is that some dishes can be dated and most cannot. What follows keeps the line between the two.

The Greeks wrote it down first. Siracusa had a reputation for its tables before Rome had one. Plato, in the Gorgias, names Mithaecus as the author of a treatise on Sicilian cookery, the first cookbook writer in Greek whose name survives; fragments of his fish recipes are quoted by later writers. The city that fed him was, Cicero said, the largest and most beautiful of the Greek cities. What survives of that table on today's plates is the olive, the wheat, the wine and the fish — and the habit of treating all four as a subject for argument.

The Arab layer is the one everyone claims. Muslim rule from the ninth to the eleventh century reorganised Sicilian agriculture, and citrus, sugar cane, rice, almond confectionery and the sherbet that became granita are routinely traced to it. Some of that is documented; much of it is tradition repeated until it hardened. The granita at least has a clear mechanism: sweet syrups, the Arab sharbat, cooled with snow carried down from Etna and stored in pits on its flanks, the neviere, until ice machines made the trade obsolete in the twentieth century.

Spain and the convents made the sweets. Chocolate reached Modica under Spanish rule, and the town still works it cold, without conching, so the sugar stays crystalline in the bar; whether the method itself came from Mexico is a story Modica tells, not one historians have settled. The monasteries and convents of the Val di Noto turned almonds, ricotta and candied fruit into a devotional pastry of their own — the cassata, the cannolo, the pasta reale moulded into fruit — and sold it through the turning wheel in the convent wall.

1693 rebuilt the stage. The earthquake of 11 January 1693, the worst in Italian history, destroyed some forty-five towns in the south-east and killed tens of thousands. Noto was refounded ten kilometres from its ruins and built in one golden stone; Catania rose again in black lava; Ragusa, Modica and Scicli were rebuilt in the same late baroque, and UNESCO listed eight of these towns together in 2002. The aristocratic kitchens of that rebuilt world employed French-trained cooks the Sicilians called monsù, from monsieur, and their timballi and gelatine are the ancestors of the island's fine dining.

The red guide came late, and then all at once. Ciccio Sultano's Duomo in Ragusa Ibla holds two MICHELIN stars and Gambero Rosso's highest Sicilian score for 2026; St. George by Heinz Beck in Taormina holds two; twelve more rooms between Marina di Ragusa and Linguaglossa hold one, including Cortile Spirito Santo, Siracusa's first star, awarded in late 2023, and Zash, which serves its tasting menu inside a restored nineteenth-century palmento on Etna. Read the list for what it says about the produce: almost every one of those menus is written in pistachio, almond, citrus, Etna wine and fish from this coast.

The market

Before nine for the fish.

Ortigia marketMarketSwordfish on ice, lemons by the crate, and men calling prices across Via De Benedictis.

The market runs along Via Emanuele de Benedictis from the Temple of Apollo, a Doric ruin from the 6th century BC, so you buy your fish beside the oldest temple of its kind in Sicily. The old sailors' harbour — true stories of this sea

War in the harbour

Siracusa's two great sieges, and the admiral who watered at the spring.

The harbour that ate a fleet413 BCAthens lost the war in the bay you can see from the Lungomare Alfeo.

In 415 BC Athens sent the largest armada of its age against Syracuse. Two years later it was trapped in the Great Harbour. Thucydides records how the Syracusans rebuilt their bows short, solid and braced, to ram head-on in water too crowded for Athenian tactics. On 27 August 413 BC an eclipse of the moon made Nicias wait 'thrice nine days' before retreating. The Syracusans used the time to close the mouth, nearly a mile wide, with ships moored broadside. About 110 Athenian ships failed to break the boom. The army fled overland and was destroyed; at least seven thousand survivors went into the stone quarries on half a pint of water and a pint of corn a day, for eight months.

★ The old hand's point: Sea-room is a weapon. A fast fleet shut in a closed basin trades its advantage for the enemy's; decide where you will fight before you are forced to.

Archimedes' claw, and the mirrors nobody saw214–212 BCAn iron hand on a beam that lifted Roman ships by the bow and dropped them.

Marcellus came at the sea walls with sixty quinqueremes, some lashed in pairs to carry sambucae, scaling ladders raised by pulleys. Archimedes, then in his seventies, answered with engines graded by range: catapults for distance, small scorpions through loopholes for close work, and beams swung out over the wall. From one hung an iron hand on a chain. It gripped a prow, the counterweighted beam came down, and the ship was stood on its stern and dropped, to capsize or fill. Marcellus joked that Archimedes was using his ships to ladle seawater into his wine cups. The city fell anyway in 212 BC, by treachery during a festival, and Archimedes was killed by a soldier.

★ The old hand's point: A galley's weakness is its bow: long, light, all buoyancy aft of the ram, easy to lever up by the stem under a wall with deep water close in.

Touch it today

Watering at Arethusa1798Nelson filled his casks at Ortigia's spring, then sailed to the Battle of the Nile.

In July 1798 Nelson had chased Bonaparte to Alexandria, found it empty, and beaten back to Sicily without frigates or news. Naples was neutral and had resolved to refuse him; through Lady Hamilton, Southey writes, secret orders reached the Sicilian governors, and at Syracuse he got 'everything which he wanted'. His water was drawn from the spring of Arethusa, a few metres from the sea. He wrote to the Hamiltons: 'surely watering at the fountain of Arethusa, we must have victory... I will return either crowned with laurel or covered with cypress.' He sailed on 25 July, learned at Coron that the French had gone to Egypt, and on 1 August found them in Aboukir Bay.

★ The old hand's point: A fleet's range is its water. The admiral who finds water and fresh food keeps the initiative; a galley chef provisioning for a long passage does the same arithmetic.

The tuna and the wreck

A village built round a trap, and the Christmas wreck the state ignored.

MarzamemiTonnaraA whole village built to serve a net that stood across the bluefin's path each spring.

Marzamemi, probably from the Arabic marsa al-hamam, 'bay of the doves', existed for one reason: the tonnara, a fixed labyrinth of nets anchored each spring across the route of the bluefin running up the coast. Sources rank it second in Sicily after Favignana. The village grew like a company town: palazzo, church, warehouses and fishermen's houses around the courtyard that is now the piazza. The fish were driven through chambers of net into the camera della morte and killed in the mattanza under the rais, the master of the trap, while the crews sang the cialoma. Overfishing was noted even under the Bourbons; after wartime bombing the plant was abandoned, and the bombed church has been restored as a Museo del Mare.

★ The old hand's point: A tonnara bets on a migration route that never moves. When purse-seiners took the bluefin offshore, the fish were not gone; they were intercepted.

The Christmas wreck off Portopalo, 19961996For four years the sinking of the F174 was treated as rumour. A fisherman's net ended that.

On the night of 25–26 December 1996, about 19 miles off Portopalo di Capo Passero, some three hundred people from India, Sri Lanka and Pakistan were being moved in a heavy sea from the freighter Yohan to a wooden boat 18 metres long, registered F174. The two collided and the F174 sank. At least 283 people died. Officials called the wreck a rumour. Then a Portopalo fisherman, Salvatore Lupo, hauled up the identity card of a seventeen-year-old Tamil, Anpalagan Ganeshu. In June 2001 Giovanni Maria Bellu published it in La Repubblica, and a remote camera filmed the hull at 108 metres. An organiser was sentenced to 30 years in 2008. The wreck was never raised.

★ The old hand's point: Fishermen know what lies on their grounds before anyone else. What broke the silence was one man who kept what his net brought up and handed it over.

Seasonal
What each part of the year is for around Siracusa — the blood orange, the tragedies, the granita and the harvest
4 seasons
October to DecemberOctober: the Etna harvest finishes, the Ottobrata fills Zafferana on Sundays, and the sea is still warm enough to swim (around 23 C). Eat: porcini and other mushrooms from the Etna woods, chestnuts, prickly pears, fresh mostarda (grape-must jelly), new olive oil from late October. November: San Martino (11 Nov) opens the new wine with roast chestnuts; first rains. December: Santa Lucia (13 Dec) in Siracusa, cuccìa instead of bread; Christmas with buccellati (fig-filled pastries) and presepi in the churches.
January to MarchJanuary and February are the season of the blood orange from the plain of Catania (Arancia Rossa di Sicilia IGP; the cold nights near Etna develop the red pigment). Sant'Agata (3-5 Feb) brings minne and olivette. Almond trees blossom in February. Carnival in Acireale is one of the island's oldest, with papier-mache and flower floats. In March, wild fennel and asparagus; the INDA box office is busy before the May season. Etna often has snow and skiing above Nicolosi and Linguaglossa.
April to JuneEaster brings processions in every town. INDA tragedies run in Siracusa from April or May to late June (2027: from 7 May). Noto's Infiorata is held mid-May. Fresh broad beans, artichokes, and wild greens fill the markets; ricotta is at its best in spring. In May-June the swordfish season begins in the Strait of Messina and the bluefin tuna pass. The sea warms to swimming temperature by June.
July to SeptemberSummer means granita and brioche for breakfast, concerts in Taormina's Teatro Antico, and long evenings in the piazzas. Sant'Agata's summer feast is on 17 August. Etna summer paroxysms can bring ash and crowds to the slopes (2026 brought both). Late summer: the grape harvest begins on the coast, figs and prickly pears ripen, and in odd years Bronte harvests pistachios (late August-September). The Ortigia Film Festival is in late September.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The stories behind the canon, and where they are argued rather than settled.

#1
DISH

Cioccolato di Modica

Modica works chocolate at low temperature without conching, so the sugar does not dissolve and the bar breaks with a grainy snap. The method is said to descend from the Aztec metate, brought by the Spanish; the town tells that story, historians have not settled it. The name is an IGP since 2018 — and Antica Dolceria Bonajuto, founded in 1880 and the most famous maker, left the consortium the same year, so its bars legally cannot carry the name.
WhereAntica Dolceria Bonajuto, Modica · Maps →
#2
DISH

Bottarga and the tonnara

For centuries the tuna came past this coast and the tonnare caught them in a chamber of nets, hauled in the mattanza to the crews' work-song. Marzamemi grew around one. The trap is gone — industrial purse-seining killed the tonnare — but the village still cures the fish: bottarga from the roe, ventresca from the belly, mosciame from the fillet.
WhereCampisi and Taverna La Cialoma, Marzamemi · Maps →
#3
DISH

The almond of Noto

The Pizzuta d'Avola almond is grown in the countryside around Noto and Avola and protected not by a DOP but by a Slow Food presidium, the Mandorla di Noto. It is the almond in Assenza's granita and in the convent pastry of the baroque towns — and the reason the purist's breakfast here is almond, not pistachio.
