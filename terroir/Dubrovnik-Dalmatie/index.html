The name Two names for one town: Ragusa, the Latin of the rock, and Dubrovnik, the Slavic of the oak wood.
The refugees from Roman Epidaurus, at Cavtat, settled the rocky islet they called Ragusa; across a marshy channel, under the hill, grew a Slav settlement named for the holm oak, dub. The channel was filled in and became the Stradun. The Republic signed its treaties as Ragusa until 1808; the city has answered to Dubrovnik ever since. The details of the founding are argued over; the two names are not.

The soul of the place — the essay
A republic with one word on its flag, a town rebuilt to one rule, and a coast that feeds it
THE ESSAY

Dubrovnik is a city that once was a country. For four and a half centuries it governed itself as the Republic of Ragusa, a small, clever maritime state that slipped free of Venice in 1358 and kept its independence until Napoleon’s marshal dissolved it in 1808.

It bought that survival the way it bought everything else: with diplomacy, tribute to whichever empire was nearest, and a merchant fleet that flew its own flag in every Mediterranean port. On that flag it wrote one word, LIBERTAS, and it meant it more than most: in January 1416 the Great Council banned the slave trade through the city and threatened any captain who carried slaves with prison — one of the earliest such bans in Europe, made while the trade was thriving all around it.

The constitution was built to stop any one man from rising; its oddest rule has a card of its own. The city it governed fits inside a ring of walls, which have their own walk. When the earthquake of 1667 flattened the town, the walls stood and the street was rebuilt to a single rule, which is why the Stradun looks the way it does. The siege of 1991–92 is remembered in two quiet rooms of the Sponza.

The quintessence. The table answers the coast, not the walls. An hour north, in the brackish bay of Mali Ston, the flat oyster has been farmed since Roman times; on the Pelješac slopes the native Plavac Mali ripens on stone; in Konavle lamb cooks for hours under an iron bell. Inside the walls the city keeps a short canon tied to its feasts — the ‘dirty macaroni’ of St Blaise, pašticada with gnocchi, a black risotto — and opens a meal, in the truest Dalmatian gesture, with a small glass of travarica.

Where it comes from

The city began, by the usual account, in the seventh century, when Greco-Roman refugees from Epidaurus — today’s Cavtat — fled Slav and Avar raids and settled the rock. Venice held it for a century and a half; the Treaty of Zadar released it in 1358, and Ragusa paid tribute first to Hungary, then to the Ottomans, and governed itself.

A republic this compact bred an improbable density of minds: a playwright the country still names first, and a Jesuit whose physics ran a century ahead of its time; both have cards. It also bred a habit of caution: a quarantine on the shore for travellers and goods from the east, whose stone halls still stand outside Ploče.

Marmont abolished the Senate in 1808; the city passed to Napoleon’s Illyrian Provinces and in 1815 to Habsburg Austria. The twentieth century brought the hotels, the Summer Festival in 1950 and, in 1979, the UNESCO listing; then the siege, the long restoration, and a second fame as a film set. The stones are the same; the crowds are new.

Where the money sits

Dubrovnik lives on visitors, and it is honest to say so. The Old Town has emptied of residents as the apartments became holiday lets, and on a summer cruise day several thousand day visitors pass through Pile Gate between breakfast and the afternoon; the city has capped the ships it lets in, and the old streets still fill.

That is the line a visitor can taste. The menus on Prijeko are written in eight languages for people who will not come back; the side lanes, Lapad, Gruž and the country konobas cook for people who will. The value is in the market before nine, in a cellar door on Pelješac, and in an oyster eaten on the quay where it was lifted, at a fraction of its price inside the walls.

And the clock is the season. From Easter to October the city is full and the island boats run; from November the cruise ships thin out, several kitchens close until spring, and the city gets its own streets back — cold, quiet, and serving the winter green stew. Whoever comes in May, June or September gets warm water and the Old Town at both ends of the day; whoever comes in February gets St Blaise.

1333 — the salt of Ston and its guarding walls
Why the Republic bought a peninsula, walled a hillside and lived on the white crust of a few shallow pools.
THE STORY

In 1333 Dubrovnik took Ston and the Pelješac peninsula, and what it wanted was the salt. The shallow pans at the head of the Ston channel became the Republic's most profitable export, and the walls that still climb the hill above them were built to keep that salt in Ragusan hands for nearly five centuries.

Salt was made at Ston long before Ragusa: one source mentions the settlement with salt in 167 BC, and others trace the working back to Roman times. What changed in 1333 was ownership, when Dubrovnik acquired Ston and Pelješac, bought from the local lords in the tourism accounts; some guides say 1334. UNESCO's tentative-list entry calls salt the Republic's most important and most profitable export, and ties the layout of the pans to the town's planning.

The walls cross the narrowest neck of the peninsula from Ston to Mali Ston: more than 7 km at first, about 5.5 km today. Their start is argued: one source gives building from 1333 to 1506, Wikipedia gives 1358, after the Pelješac purchase. How much the salt earned is argued too. Tourism sources repeat that it brought about a third of the Republic's income, and one gives 15,900 ducats a year, but no scholarly source for either figure was found.

The pans are still worked. Solana Ston has 58 pools in five groups, nine of them for crystallization, and makes about 500 tonnes of salt a year. The harvest is done by hand in the dry weeks of late summer, mostly late July to September, and rain can push it back, as late-July rain did in one reported year. No 2026 harvest report was found.

Two tables in this guide sit on the bay the walls were built to guard: Bota Šare and Vila Koruna, at Mali Ston, a walk from the pans.

How the Republic ran

A constitution against ambition, a wind with legal weight, and a street rebuilt to a single cornice.

A ruler for one monthcuriosityThe Republic of Ragusa feared one man more than any fleet, so it gave its head of state a month

For four and a half centuries, from the Treaty of Zadar in 1358 to Marmont's decree of 1808, Ragusa ran itself on suspicion of ambition. Every noble entered the Major Council at eighteen; it chose a Senate of patricians over forty, the standard count is 45, for a year at a time; a seven-member Minor Council handled the day. On top sat a Rector who served exactly one month, had to wait two years before he could stand again, and lived shut inside his palace for the whole term, leaving it only on state business. Nobody was meant to get used to power.

Why go. It explains the whole city: a merchant aristocracy that trusted no single man, and kept its independence that way.

The wind that excused a crimecuriosityDubrovnik swears its old Senate stopped work when the jugo blew: a lovely story with no paper behind it

The jugo comes up from the south-east, warm and wet, piling the sea against the rocks below the walls and pressing on the head like a hand. Dubrovnik tells a story about it: that the Republic's councils would not sit or pass sentence while it blew, and that a crime done in a hard jugo earned a lighter penalty, because the wind was known to unsettle judgement. Travel writers repeat it as law. Look for the statute, though, and it is not there: the story lives in folklore collections and guidebooks, not in any archive anyone cites. People still blame the jugo for headaches and short tempers.

Why go. Treat it as what Dubrovnik feels about its weather, not as Republic law.

Touch it today

Why every façade matchescuriosityThe Stradun looks designed in one go because, after 1667, it was: a rebuild run by rule from Rome

On the morning of 6 April 1667 the ground shook for a few seconds and the medieval city came down; fires burned for days after. The Rector was among the dead. The man who saved the shape of the place never saw it: Stjepan Gradić, a Ragusan priest who ran the Vatican Library, lobbied the Pope and Europe's courts for money and pressed the Senate to rebuild rather than move. What rose was Baroque and regulated: the Stradun's houses at one height, shops below with the door and the counter window under a single arch, flats above. That sameness is policy, not chance.

Why go. Look at the ground floors: the door-and-counter arches repeat, house after house.

Two citizens

The playwright and the physicist the town still claims first.

Marin DržićplaywrightThe Renaissance comic who mocked Dubrovnik's grandees, and later plotted to overthrow them

Marin Držić was born in the city in 1508, trained as a priest, and spent his life broke, restless and very funny. His Dundo Maroje, first staged in 1551, sends a miser to Rome after his spendthrift son and lets the servant Pomet run rings round everyone: Croatian theatre still starts there, and the Summer Festival keeps staging it. Late in life he wrote to the Medici in Florence asking for help to topple the patrician government that had laughed at his plays. Nothing came of it. He died in Venice in 1567. His house on Široka is now a small museum of his stage world.

Why go. See a Držić play in Croatian if one is on: you will follow Pomet without a word of the language.

Ruđer BoškovićpolymathA Jesuit from these lanes guessed in 1758 that matter is points held apart by forces

Ruđer Josip Bošković was born in the Old Town on 18 May 1711, joined the Jesuits in Rome, and became one of the great scientific minds of his century: astronomer, mathematician, surveyor, diplomat for his Republic. His Theoria Philosophiae Naturalis, printed in Vienna in 1758, pictured matter not as hard little balls but as points with forces between them, repulsive up close and attractive further off. Two centuries later physicists recognised atomic and field ideas in it, and Heisenberg is credited with calling him the Croatian Leibniz. He was a Fellow of the Royal Society in London and died in Milan in 1787.

Why go. Stand at the foot of the Jesuit Stairs: the square there carries his name.

The city on foot

The ring of the walls, the street and its squares, and the mountain the city was shelled from.

The city wallsthe full ringA one-way walk of 1,940 m round the rooftops, from the Pile bastions to Minčeta and back to the gate

Climb from Pile Gate past Bokar, Michelozzo's two-storey casemate of 1461-63, and follow the sea wall east above the rocks, the swell sounding below, terracotta roofs and washing lines. The ring turns north round the harbour and up to Minčeta, the round tower of 1464 by Michelozzo and Juraj Dalmatinac: at 25 m the highest point. Across the cove Lovrijenac sits on its own 37 m rock, its seaward walls 12 m thick, its city-facing ones 60 cm, so the city could bring it down if it were ever lost. Over its door: Non bene pro toto libertas venditur auro. Your ticket covers it; Hamlet plays there in summer.

Why go. From Minčeta you see the whole Stradun from above, the view that explains how the city is laid out.

Out of town

The long walls of Ston above the salt pans, and a cold river with its mills.

The walls of Stoneurope's long wallThe Republic walled a whole isthmus to guard its salt: climb from Ston over the hill to Mali Ston

Ston came under Dubrovnik in 1333, and the Republic set out to wall off the whole isthmus of Pelješac. Between the two towns the walls climb over a steep hill, linking Ston's walls on the salt-pan side with Mali Ston on the bay. Climb the stepped ramparts to the high towers and you look down on the square ponds of the Ston salt pans on one side and the oyster lines of the bay on the other. On a still day you can hear the gulls from both. Coming down to Mali Ston you arrive at the oyster tables of Bota Šare.

Why go. From the top tower you can see why the walls were built: salt on one side, the sea on the other.

Konavle was the Republic's grain field, and the Ljuta ground its flour. Along this short spring-fed river there were once eight flour mills, two oil mills and three fulling mills for cloth, fed by channels and three small aqueducts. There is no beach on the seaward side of the Old Town, only the rock the walls stand on. Go past the cathedral and through one of the small openings in the south wall by the Buža bars and you come out on flat shelves of pale limestone just above the water. A boat away

The island off the Old Port, and the sand of the Elaphiti.

Lokrumthe island reserveA wooded reserve ten minutes off the Old Port, with peacocks, a salt lake and a rule: everyone leaves by night

Lokrum sits about 600 m off the Old Port and nobody lives on it: overnight stays are banned, and the last boat takes everyone back to town in the evening. Benedictines founded an abbey here in 1023; Archduke Maximilian of Habsburg bought the island in the 1850s, built a summer house on the monastery ruins, laid out gardens and brought peacocks, whose descendants still strut the paths and scream from the pines. The island has been a protected reserve since 1964. Walk from the harbour to the cloister, where a replica Iron Throne recalls the Game of Thrones filming, then on to the Mrtvo More, the 'Dead Sea', a small salt lake fed by the sea through the rock, warm, sheltered and shallow enough for children. The southern shore is bare rock dropping into deep water.

Why go. The quietest swim within sight of the walls, and a pine-shaded walk when the Old Town is full.

The Republic in stone

The palace, the customs house and memorial, and the quarantine halls on the shore.

The Rector's Palacepalace · museumA palace twice blown up by its own gunpowder, rebuilt with a Renaissance portico and a sailor's bust

The Republic kept its gunpowder in the building where it kept its head of state, and paid for it twice. An explosion in 1435 wrecked the old fortified residence; Onofrio della Cava, the Neapolitan who built the city's fountains, raised a late-Gothic palace in its place. A second blast in 1463 tore it again, and Michelozzo of Florence and Juraj Dalmatinac worked on the repair, which is why Gothic windows sit over a Renaissance portico of carved capitals. Inside the arcaded atrium stands the bust of Miho Pracat, the ship-owner from Lopud, the one private citizen the Republic honoured that way. Upstairs, the Cultural History Museum fills the state rooms.

Why go. Linger in the atrium: in summer it is one of the Festival's concert halls, and its acoustics are why.

Sponza and the Memorial Roompalace · memorialThe Republic weighed its trade in this courtyard; today one room holds the faces of those who defended it

Paskoje Miličević built the Sponza between 1516 and 1522 as the Republic's customs house, with its mint and treasury; every bale landed at the port passed under its arch, where a Latin line warns that the weights do not allow cheating, for God weighs with the weigher. It came through the 1667 quake and now keeps the Dubrovnik State Archives. In one wing is the Memorial Room of the Defenders: photographs of the men killed in the siege of 1991-92, which began on 1 October 1991. On 6 December 1991 alone the shelling killed 19 people and wounded 60. Most visitors pass in under a minute. Stay longer.

Why go. Read a few names and ages in the Memorial Room: most of them were barely twenty.

Lazaretiquarantine · cultureTen stone halls outside Ploče Gate where caravans and their cargo waited out the plague before entering

In 1377 Ragusa's Major Council made travellers from infected places wait outside the city before they entered. The halls beyond Ploče Gate came much later, dated 1627 by the World Monuments Fund and to work between about 1590 and 1642 by other accounts: a line of stone warehouses with walled yards where traders off the Ottoman roads, their goods and their animals sat out their days under guard. Today the halls are a cultural centre. In summer the Linđo Folklore Ensemble dances here under the vaults, a dance-master on the lijerica calling the steps. The season ended in early October.

Why go. Come back in summer for a Linđo night: the old quarantine yard is the right stage for it.

This is red-wine country with two great whites an island away. Learn three names; then drink them at the wall, or in a bar that pours nothing but Croatian.

Ask for it by name

The native red, its great slope, and the white of Korčula.

Plavac Malithe redThe little blue grape of Pelješac, and a DNA hunt that found Zinfandel's family tree in Dalmatia

Plavac Mali means little blue, and it is the red of every Dalmatian table. For years Californians, Mike Grgich among the believers, said it was Zinfandel. In 1998 Carole Meredith of UC Davis began testing with Ivan Pejić and Edi Maletić of the University of Zagreb and found it was not the same grape, but related. In December 2001 Pejić brought her a near-extinct vine from Kaštela called Crljenak Kaštelanski: it matched Zinfandel and Primitivo exactly. The team's 2004 paper showed Plavac Mali to be the child of that grape and Dobričić, a variety from the island of Šolta. So Zinfandel is Plavac's parent, not its twin.

Why go. Order it with brodet or lamb from the peka: big, dark, dried-fig fruit and alcohol that often passes 14%.

Dingač is a strip of near-vertical vineyard between Trstenik and Podobuče on the south face of Pelješac, and in 1961 it became the first protected wine name in Yugoslavia. Why go. Drive the Potomje tunnel and come out above the open sea and the steepest vines in Croatia.

The other glasses

The one-village white, the sun-dried sweet wine, and the herb rakija that opens a meal.

Grkone villageA white that grows only in Lumbarda's sand, from a vine that cannot pollinate itself

Grk means the Greek, and it grows only around Lumbarda, at the eastern tip of Korčula, in sandy soil between the stone walls. Greek settlers from Issa, today's Vis, did found a colony here in the 3rd or 4th century BC, but nobody can prove they brought this vine. The botany is proven: Grk has functionally female flowers, so growers plant Plavac Mali between the rows to pollinate it. Even then the bunches set unevenly and yields are tiny. Fewer than ten estates bottle it. The wine is dry, bitter-almond, saline and herbal.

Why go. Taste a vine that needs a red grape beside it to bear fruit at all.

Live, the real thing

The Konavle dance with its lyre, and the a cappella of Dalmatia in stone rooms.

The lijerica is a small pear-shaped fiddle with three strings, held upright on the knee and bowed. Its player sits in the middle of the floor and beats time with his foot on the stone while a caller shouts the figures in rhyme, and the dancers around him turn, swap partners, speed up and stop on command. The Linđo Folklore Ensemble, founded in the mid-1960s, keeps it alive and stages it on summer nights in the Lazareti, the long row of stone halls just past the Ploče Gate, in full Konavle costume.

Why go. Watch the lijerica player's right foot: the whole dance hangs on that beat.

Sokol grows out of a rock above Dunave, high in Konavle; it is hard to see where cliff ends and masonry begins. Bronze Age, Roman and Byzantine finds show the rock was held long before Ragusa. Ivan Marinov Gučetić began the summer villa and garden at Trsteno in 1494, and his family, the Gozze, kept adding to it for four centuries: Renaissance beds, a pergola walk, a baroque grotto and Neptune fountain. Ragusan captains brought seeds home. In 1948 Vito Gozze gave the estate to the Croatian Academy of Sciences and Arts. In early October 1991 shelling from the sea set much of it alight, and in the drought summer of 2000 a forest fire burned some 120,000 square metres. Two giant oriental planes in the village square, said to be over 500 years old, survived both.

Why go. The two plane trees in the village square, then the cliff path down to the little harbour.

Read the plate

The oyster that travelled, and the truffle that does not belong here.

Oysters inside the wallsthe warningThe oysters on Old Town menus come from Mali Ston, an hour up the coast: eat them there instead

Dubrovnik's oysters are not from Dubrovnik. They are farmed in the bay at Mali Ston, about an hour north-west by road, and reach the Old Town by van. An oyster keeps, but it does not improve: a plate inside the walls may have spent days in transit and cold storage, especially in the shoulder season when restaurants buy less often, and it is served at Old Town prices. At Mali Ston the same shellfish comes off the rafts in the bay you are looking at. The oyster itself, its protected name and its season, have their own card under the table.

On the plate

Mandarins picked by boat in the Neretva, oysters back on the Mali Ston quay, and the first winter stew.

The mandarin harvest in the Neretva deltamandarinsLate September to mid-November: the only place, they say, where mandarins are harvested by boat

An hour and a half north of Dubrovnik, around Opuzen, the Neretva delta is Croatia's mandarin country, averaging about 43,000 tonnes a year between 2010 and 2024 according to a Croatian agricultural paper. Visit Dubrovnik gives the harvest as late September to mid-November, with October and November the peak. Some plantations sit on islands between the channels and are reached only by the lađa, the flat wooden boat of the delta. Agencies sell days that combine a boat ride, picking with a basket to take home and a tasting, often with brudet, fried eels and mandarin liqueur. This year's early fruit is reported smaller after a dry, hot summer.

Why go. Go before mid-November, and buy a crate from the roadside stalls on the way home.

In the calendar

The food festival has just ended, the clocks go back, two public holidays fall in November and the winter festival opens at the end of the month.

Clocks back and the November holidaysdates to knowClocks go back on Sunday 25 October; All Saints on 1 November and Remembrance Day on 18 November are holidays

Three dates change the shape of the weeks ahead. On Sunday 25 October the clocks go back an hour, as across the EU, and sunset jumps to late afternoon. Sunday 1 November, All Saints, is a public holiday: families visit the graves, and the cemetery at Boninovo fills with candles in the evening. Wednesday 18 November is Remembrance Day, the public holiday for the victims of the war and of Vukovar, and shops and offices close. The tourist board's Saturday Winter Mornings, with themed walks and folklore at St Blaise's Church, began on 8 November last year; the 2026 programme was not yet published.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates and their honest passports: which are the city’s own, which are the coast’s, and which the hinterland sends down.

ORIGIN

Šporki makaruli

Hand-cut macaroni under a deep beef ragù cooked slowly with bay and cloves. The name means ‘dirty macaroni’: the story goes that patrician households ate the meat and poured what was left of the sauce over pasta for the servants. The city kept the dish and dropped the hierarchy, and eats it above all in the week of St Blaise, early February. Origin. Dubrovnik’s own

Eat it — Kopun, Old Town
ORIGIN

Pašticada

Beef larded with pancetta, garlic and cloves, marinated in vinegar and Prošek, then braised for hours with prunes and dried figs until the sauce turns sweet and sour. It comes with gnocchi, always. A two-day dish: ask before you order. Origin. Dalmatia-wide

ORIGIN

Mali Ston oysters

The European flat oyster, Ostrea edulis, not the cupped Pacific: farmed in the brackish bay of Mali Ston since Roman times and protected by an EU designation since 2020. Eaten raw with lemon, at the bay, with a glass of Pošip. Origin. Mali Ston, an hour north

Eat it — Bota Šare or Vila Koruna, Mali Ston
ORIGIN

Crni rižot

Risotto made black with cuttlefish and its ink, with garlic, white wine and parsley; the ink goes in at the end so the rice stays glossy. Venice cooks one too; Dalmatia says its own is no copy, and the argument is part of the dish. Origin. The whole Adriatic shore

Eat it — Proto, Old Town
ORIGIN

Janjetina ispod peke

Lamb, veal or octopus with potatoes and rosemary under an iron bell buried in embers, heat from above and below, nobody lifting the lid for hours. It has to be ordered ahead — a country dish, cooked to the guest’s arrival. Origin. Konavle and the hinterland

Eat it — Konoba Dubrava, Bosanka, ordered ahead
ORIGIN

Brodet

The fishermen’s stew: at least three kinds of fish with tomato, onion and a glass of red, never stirred — the pan is shaken so the fish stays whole. Polenta or potatoes on the side. Origin. Coastal Dalmatia

ORIGIN

Zelena menestra

The winter stew of the city: cabbage and kale in a smoked-pork broth with dried mutton, sausage and potatoes. Dubrovnik writings record it in the fifteenth century; Konavle sets it at the centre of the Christmas table. Origin. Dubrovnik and its hinterland

ORIGIN

Rožata

The city’s custard, baked in a caramel and turned out like a crème caramel — but scented, in the Dubrovnik way, with a home-made rose liqueur that makes it taste of here and nowhere else. Origin. Dubrovnik’s own
