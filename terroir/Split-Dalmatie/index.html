Split

Dalmatia · Croatia
"An emperor built himself a palace to die in; his neighbours moved into it and never left. Seventeen centuries later the market still trades at its gate."
The name Split is Spalatum, and the Romans’ word for the place was probably a shrub, not a palace.
The old story says Spalatum comes from palatium, the palace. The likelier root is the Greek and Latin name of a spiny broom that grew on this shore, aspalathos, recorded for the bay before Diocletian built anything. The Venetians wrote Spalato; the Croats say Split. The sources still argue; the palace is the better story, and the shrub is probably the truer one.

The soul of the place — the essay
An emperor’s retirement home that became a town, a market at its gates, and a coast of stone, oil and wine
THE ESSAY

Split began as one man’s retirement. Around the year 300 the emperor Diocletian, a Dalmatian by birth, built a fortified palace on the bay to live out his days in, and abdicated in 305 to grow cabbages in it — or so he is said to have told the men who begged him to come back.

Three centuries later the Avars and Slavs sacked Salona, the Roman capital a few kilometres inland, and its people fled to the empty palace and stayed. They built houses into the arcades, chapels into the walls, and a cathedral into the emperor’s tomb; its story, and the palace’s plan, have cards of their own. What matters at table is that the town grew inside a building, and the market grew at its doors: the green market against the east wall, the fish hall a few steps from the west gate.

Venice held the city from 1420 to 1797 and left a vocabulary in the kitchen: brodet is a cousin of brodetto, pašticada of a slow Venetian braise. Then came a short French interlude and a century of Habsburg rule, and with it coffee houses and cakes. In 1501 Marko Marulić finished Judita here, the first great poem in the Croatian language; the city still calls him its father.

The quintessence. The kitchen is poor-soil and sea. The limestone that built the palace drains too fast for wheat but suits the vine, the olive and the herbs that go into travarica; so the table is fish, oil, greens, wine and a little lamb. The canon is short and seasonal: grilled fish and squid, a black risotto, the Hvar stew without tomato, pašticada on Sundays and in winter, peka when someone has ordered it the day before. And before lunch, the marenda: the mid-morning plate of the working city, still served in the canteens by the harbour.

Where it comes from

The fetivi — the born-and-bred Splićani — will tell you the palace is not a museum, and they are right: people still live in it, hang washing in it and argue about parking outside it. The Golden Gate, the Peristyle and the cellars are on every itinerary; the side alleys, Varoš behind them and the hill of Marjan are where the town lives.

The twentieth century brought a sculptor, Ivan Meštrović, whose bishop stands at the Golden Gate and whose house is a gallery under Marjan; a football club the city treats as a religion; and in 1979 the UNESCO listing. Then came the ferries, the cruise ships and a second fame as a film set, and the old question of how many people can live in a building that everyone wants to visit.

And in 2001, a few kilometres up the coast in Kaštela, a laboratory found the answer to one of the wine world’s oldest riddles in a handful of old vines. That story is told in full in the fold below.

Where the money sits

Split is the ferry port for the islands, and in summer it lives on the people passing through. The palace fills from mid-morning when the ships are in; the menus with photographs are written for people who will not come back.

That is the line you can taste. The value is in the fish hall before nine, the green market before the producers pack up, a konoba in Varoš with a chalk board, a canteen at Matejuška where the fishermen eat their marenda, and a glass of Pošip poured by someone who can tell you whose vineyard it came from.

And the clock is the season. From Easter to October the ferries run full and the palace is crowded at midday; from November the catamarans go on winter timetables, a few kitchens close until spring, the olive presses run and the city gets its alleys back. Whoever comes in May gets St Domnius and a cool sea; whoever comes in late autumn gets the new oil, the first fritule and a palace you can hear yourself walk in.

2001 — Zinfandel's lost parent found in Kaštela
How a Californian grape was traced to a few old vines in a mixed plot between Split and Trogir, and what it is called now.
THE STORY

California's Zinfandel had no known home. In 2001 a DNA test matched it to a handful of old vines called Crljenak Kaštelanski in Ivica Radunić's mixed vineyard at Kaštel Novi, fifteen minutes from Split. Ten years later a dried leaf in a Split herbarium gave the grape its older Dalmatian name: Tribidrag.

The hunt was led by Carole Meredith, a grape geneticist at UC Davis, with Ivan Pejić and Edi Maletić of the Zagreb Faculty of Agriculture. Plavac Mali was the first suspect, but it was not Zinfandel. Meredith sought help from American producers, among them Miljenko (Mike) Grgić, the Napa winemaker born in Croatia. The Zagreb team then searched old Dalmatian vineyards for forgotten varieties, collecting leaves for DNA.

In Kaštel Novi they found a mixed plot about 35 years old belonging to Ivica Radunić. Samples of a vine he called Crljenak Kaštelanski matched Zinfandel. The dates differ: Grgich Hills says Maletić found the vine on 7 September 2000; Radunić recalls hearing from Meredith in September 2001; the SOMM Journal gives the result as 18 December 2001. The number of vines is argued too, from nine to eleven in the plot, with more later found near Omiš.

A 2011 paper reported that a herbarium leaf about 90 years old, labelled Tribidrag and kept in Split's Natural History Museum, had the same DNA, and suggested the grape was grown in Croatia from the early 15th century; one source cites a 1488 barrel shipped from Venice to Apulia. The Zagreb team's 2004 paper had already shown Plavac Mali to be its offspring with Dobričić. Pejić still says there is no proof the variety was born in Croatia. Plantings are now about 250 ha across Dalmatia.

Taste what the laboratory found where the city pours it: Zinfandel, on Marulićeva, is named for this story and keeps Croatian reds by the glass — ask for a Tribidrag from Kaštela.

The palace and its front

The palace that became a town, the stone quarter behind it, and the seafront and harbour in front of it.

The palacethe lived-in palaceFour Roman gates, one colonnaded court, and washing lines strung between 1,700-year-old walls

Diocletian built this as his retirement house; when Salona fell in the 7th century its people moved inside the walls and never left. Today you enter by one of four gates: Golden to the north, Silver to the east by the Pazar, Bronze from the sea, Iron to the west. All four lanes lead to the Peristyle, where red granite columns frame the cathedral steps. One alley off, the palace turns domestic: a scooter against a Roman pilaster, laundry over a medieval window. How many still live here is argued: 3,000 is the usual figure, Fodor's says just over 1,000 and falling as flats become rentals.

Why go. Walk it twice: once at first light, empty, and once after the cruise passengers have gone back aboard.

Odd things in the palace

An Egyptian sphinx, a bronze bishop's toe, and a fish hall with a legend.

The sphinx on the PeristylecuriosityA black granite sphinx about 3,500 years old, brought from Egypt by the emperor and still on guard

Most visitors photograph the columns and walk past it. Low against the cathedral side of the Peristyle lies a black granite sphinx, about two and a half metres long, carved in Egypt in the 15th century BC and usually linked to Thutmose III. Diocletian had put down a revolt in Egypt around 297 and shipped home a dozen or more sphinxes to line his new palace. Only a few survive: this one, a headless one in front of the Temple of Jupiter, and a third in the City Museum. Who smashed the rest is not recorded.

Why go. It is older than Rome itself: put a hand on the oldest object in the city.

Grgur Ninski's toecuriosityRub the bishop's big toe, not his thumb: the bronze has been polished gold by millions of hands

Outside the Golden Gate a bronze bishop some eight metres tall raises his finger at the sky. Ivan Meštrović made Grgur Ninski in 1929 for the medieval bishop of Nin who fought for the Slavonic liturgy against Latin, and first set him in the middle of the Peristyle. During the Second World War he was taken down, and only in the 1950s was he raised again outside the palace's north gate. The custom is to rub his big toe for luck, and the toe now shines bright gold against the dark green bronze. Several guidebooks still say thumb: it is the toe.

Why go. Make a wish on the toe on your way in through the Golden Gate.

No flies in the fish hallcuriosityA hall full of fresh fish in summer, and, so Split insists, not a single fly

Stand by the Ribarnica, Peškarija in dialect, on a July morning and notice what is missing: the flies. The story every Splićanin will tell you is that the sulphur springs next door keep them off. The springs are real. The Art Nouveau spa building of 1903 at the end of Marmontova stands over waters used as a cure since the 18th century, and on some days you can smell them. Local lore even claims the springs drew Diocletian here. Whether sulphur actually keeps insects out of a fish market is another matter: we found the claim repeated, never tested.

Why go. Go early, look and sniff for yourself: the fish is the reason, the riddle is a bonus.

Stone and sea

The claim every guide repeats about Brač stone, and the ball game of the shallows.

The stone of the White HousecuriosityBrač stone built Diocletian's palace. Whether it ever reached Washington is another story

Every guide on this coast tells you the White House is built of Brač stone. It was not. Its walls were raised in 1792-1799 from Aquia Creek sandstone, quarried in Virginia and painted white. The Brač version shrinks on inspection to a claim that island stone went into a 1902 renovation, or into a fireplace, backed by a shipping receipt nobody cites; the islanders say the quarry records were destroyed in the war. The palace you are standing in is different: Split's City Museum says it was built almost entirely of Brač limestone, cut near Škrip and shipped from Splitska.

Why go. Trust the palace wall under your hand, not the Washington story.

Touch it today
Lay a palm on the palace's south wall on the Riva: that pale stone came across the channel from Brač 1,700 years ago
Where the sources argue
Aquia Creek sandstone for the White House is documented (USGS). Brač stone for a 1902 renovation or a fireplace rests on promotional pages; the Reichstag claim we could not trace at all
PicigincuriosityKnee-deep water, a small ball, and one rule: it must never touch the sea

At Bačvice grown men dive full length into knee-deep water to save a ball. That is picigin: players stand in a loose ring and slap a small ball to one another with the flat of the hand, and the only rule that matters is that it must not land in the sea. There is no score and no winner, only the save. It was first played here in 1908, the story goes, by students home from university who found water polo hopeless in such shallow water. In 2013 Croatia's Ministry of Culture listed it as intangible heritage, and Bačvice hosts a picigin world championship.

Why go. Watch one late-afternoon game: the dives into ankle-deep water are the point.

Touch it today
Stand at the water's edge at Bačvice on a warm afternoon; in late October games are rarer, but locals still play on mild days
Where the sources argue
1908 and Bačvice in most accounts; a family history in a folklore archive puts the start in the 1920s

Dalmatia is red-wine country with great island whites. Learn three names; then drink them in a bar that knows the grower.

Ask for it by name

The great red, the great white, and the grape that turned out to be Zinfandel.

Plavac Malithe redLittle blue: the dark, warm red of every Dalmatian table, grown on slopes that face the sea

Plavac Mali means little blue, for the small dark berries. It is the red you will be poured from Split to the islands, and at its best it comes from the south face of the Pelješac peninsula, where Dingač and Postup drop steeply toward the water. DNA work showed it to be the child of Tribidrag, the grape the world knows as Zinfandel, and Dobričić from the island of Šolta, a few miles off Split. Its story is told in the history fold. In the glass it is ripe, dried-cherry fruit, grippy tannin and alcohol that often passes 14%.

Why go. Drink it with peka lamb or brodet, when its weight and tannin have something to hold on to.

In the glass
Deep ruby, dried cherry and fig, firm tannin, 14% and up
On the label
Plavac Mali; the protected slopes appear as Dingač or Postup
Ask for
A Dingač or Postup; the old guide named Saints Hills and Korta Katarina (seen May 2026). Zinfandel pours it by the glass
Pošipthe whiteKorčula's white, protected in 1967 before any other Croatian white wine

Pošip grows almost only on Korčula, in the linked fields of Čara and Smokvica at the island's centre. Island tradition says Marin Tomašić of Smokvica found the vine in the nineteenth century; the sources disagree on the year. In 1967 it became the first Croatian white wine given legal protection of origin. DNA shows it to be a cross of two other Korčula grapes, Bratkovina and Zlatarica blatska. It makes a broad white with soft acidity, citrus peel, apricot, almond and a salty last note, fresh from steel or rounder from the barrel.

Why go. The white with weight enough for grilled fish, crni rižot or a plate of Pag cheese.

In the glass
Straw gold, citrus peel, apricot, almond, a saline finish; soft acidity
On the label
Pošip, often with Čara or Smokvica

Toreta, Bire, Krajančić or the Pošip Čara cooperative (producers named in the old guide, seen May 2026)
TribidragZinfandel at homeThe grape California calls Zinfandel, made again by a dozen families in Kaštela, just west of Split

The grape that became Zinfandel in California and Primitivo in Puglia is back in bottle where it was found, in the Kaštela villages strung along the bay between Split and Trogir. Twelve local growers now carry the Kaštelanski crljenak origin label, among them Anton Kovač of Putalj, whose vines climb the slopes of Kozjak above Kaštel Sućurac and who shows visitors the barrel room, the Matela family next door, and Kaštel Sikuli in Kaštel Novi. Expect a lighter, juicier red than a California Zin: red cherry, pepper, a dry finish. The story of how it was found is told in the history fold.

Why go. Drink Zinfandel's ancestor fifteen minutes from where it was found, poured by the people who replanted it.

In the glass
Mid-ruby, red cherry and plum, pepper, fresher and less jammy than a California Zinfandel
On the label
Crljenak kaštelanski or Kaštelanski crljenak, Tribidrag; sometimes Zinfandel for export

The other glasses

The white of Vis, the sweet wine of dried grapes, and the rakija.

Vugavathe island whiteVis's own white, called Bugava on the island, poured in a cellar cut into an old army tunnel

Vugava is a white grape almost nobody grows outside Vis, the farthest of the inhabited islands off Split, where they call it Bugava. It makes a full, golden, softly aromatic white, apricot and honey with a line of salt, that can take a few years in bottle. Antonio Lipanović is the name you will find most easily: the largest grower, he ages his wine in a tunnel the Yugoslav army cut into the island, and pours Vugava, Plavac Mali and Prošek there. On Vis, Roki's, Senjanović and Ivčević also make it; on Brač, Stina is the rare exception.

Why go. Bring a bottle home: outside Vis you will rarely see it on a list.

In the glass
Golden, apricot, honey, Mediterranean herbs, saline; full and soft
On the label
Vugava, or Bugava, often with Vis or Viška
Ask for
Lipanović, Roki's, Senjanović or Ivčević from Vis; Stina from Brač
Prošekthe sweet oneA sweet wine of sun-dried grapes, not a sparkling, whatever the name sounds like

Prošek is made by laying grapes out on racks until they shrivel, then pressing the concentrated juice into a sweet, amber or garnet wine of around 14%: Plavac Mali for the red, Maraština or Bogdanuša for the white. Dalmatian cooks pour it into the pašticada pot, and families open it for guests with dry figs or almonds. It has nothing to do with Prosecco, the Italian sparkling wine, but the names collide. The EU refused to protect Prošek in 2013; in 2021 the Commission took Croatia's new application as admissible and published it, and Italy objected. We found no final ruling.

Why go. A small glass at the end of dinner, with rožata or a few dried figs.

In the glass
Amber or garnet, raisin, dried fig, honey; sweet, around 14%
On the label
Prošek, often with the grape; Croatian producers have also used Dalmatian sweet wine terms while the name is contested
Ask for
A Prošek from a named grower; ask for 'sweet dessert wine', not 'Prosecco'. Lipanović on Vis makes one
Rakijathe small glassPlum, pear, walnut or a fistful of island herbs, at 40% and at any hour

Rakija is the clear fruit spirit of the whole region, and every house seems to have a bottle. Šljivovica is plum; kruškovac pear; orahovac is dark and bitter-sweet from green walnuts; medica is softened with honey. The Dalmatian one is travarica: grape spirit steeped with sage, rosemary, fennel and whatever grows on the hill, sometimes with the sprigs left in the bottle. A farmer's bottle from the market often beats the supermarket one. Zadar's Maraska, a postwar company heir to the city's old maraschino trade, is the familiar industrial label.

Why go. Accept the glass when it is offered; it is how a Dalmatian host says welcome.

In the glass
Clear or golden, around 40%; travarica smells of sage and rosemary
On the label
Travarica, šljivovica, kruškovac, orahovac, medica
Ask for
Domaća travarica, the house one

Live, the real thing

The a cappella of Dalmatia.

Klapavoices in the vestibuleA small group of voices in a tight half-circle under the open dome, no instruments, the stone doing the rest

Klapa is Dalmatia's unaccompanied multipart song: a small group led by a first tenor, standing close in a semicircle, singing of the sea, love and home, learned by ear. UNESCO inscribed it on its Representative List of intangible heritage in 2012. In Split the place to hear it is the Vestibule, the round, roofless hall above the Peristyle, where the dome's open eye and bare stone carry the voices up and hold them. In June and September 2026 the Tourist Board listed Monday promenade concerts by KUDŽ Filip Dević, Piva klapa isp'o volta, moving between the Vestibule, Pjaca and the Golden Gate. The Omiš klapa festival is on the calendar.

Why go. Stand inside the Vestibule while a klapa sings: the room is the instrument.

When
Summer evenings; in 2026 the promenade concerts ran Mondays at 20:00 in June and September (Visit Split). In late October check the events calendar: the series is seasonal
Where
The Vestibule above the Peristyle, plus Pjaca and the Golden Gate on promenade nights
Cost
Open-air promenade concerts; no ticket price published
Tip
Stand at the edge of the Vestibule, not in the doorway, and let the last chord die before you clap

Queue it before service

Three Split voices for the galley playlist.

Oliver Dragojevićthe voice of the coastThe island boy whose warm, cracked voice still plays in every konoba from Split to Korčula

Oliver Dragojević was born on 7 December 1947 and grew up in Vela Luka on Korčula, the fishing town his family came from, before they moved to Split when his father took a job at the port. For four decades his voice, low and slightly rough, carried the Dalmatian pop song: the sea, the gulls, the one who left. He died in Split on 29 July 2018, after a year with lung cancer. A week later Trogir painted a 25-metre mural of him with seagulls and a line from Moj galebe. In March 2026 Vela Luka set an installation shaped like his early spectacles by the sea.

Why go. Play him on deck at dusk and the whole crew will know the chorus by the second night.

Born
7 December 1947, in Split according to most sources; others say in Vela Luka, where he grew up
Trace today
The Trogir mural (August 2018); the memorial installation by the sea in Vela Luka, Korčula (March 2026)
Read
Moj galebe, then Cesarica
Gibonnithe songwriterA Split metal kid who became the coast's songwriter, and wrote Oliver's best-loved ballad

Zlatan Stipišić, Gibonni, was born in Split on 13 August 1968, the son of Ljubo Stipišić, a composer and poet. He started in the 1980s singing heavy metal with the Split band Osmi Putnik, moved to Divlje Jagode, where a bandmate gave him the nickname, then went solo in the early 1990s. His songs mix Dalmatian melody with rock and a dry, plain-spoken lyric. He wrote Cesarica for Oliver Dragojević, and it became one of the best-known Croatian songs of its decade. In 2003 he became a UNICEF Goodwill Ambassador.

Why go. Queue the songwriter after the singer: the same coast, heard from the other side of the pen.

Daleka obalasplit guitar rockFaraway Coast: a Split guitar band whose sea songs became a handball anthem

Daleka obala, Faraway Coast, formed in Split in 1985 around the singer and songwriter Marijan Ban with Jadran Vušković, Boris Hrepić and Zoran Ukić. Their 1990 debut, Daleka obala, gave them Četrnaest palmi and Tonka, bright guitar songs about summer on the coast and the girls on the waterfront. Morska vila, from Morski pas in 1994, outlived the band's first life and became the anthem of Croatia's handball players. They split in 2002; Ban went on solo, and in 2020 the band came back on stage with a new line-up.

Why go. The sound of a Split summer in the early 1990s: windows open, guitar up.

The islands

The stone island across the channel, and the two further out.

Bračthe stone islandFifty minutes by car ferry to Supetar, then across the island to Bol and the white spit of Zlatni Rat

The car ferry pulls out of Split harbour and fifty minutes later ties up in Supetar, a low town of pale stone on Brač's north shore. From there the road crosses the island's high karst, dry-stone walls and olive terraces, then drops down to Bol on the south coast. Just west of Bol runs Zlatni Rat, a spit of white pebbles reaching into the channel, its tip shifting with wind and current. The island is quarried limestone through and through: the stone's travels and the stonemasons' school at Pučišća have their own cards, as does the Stina cellar on Bol's waterfront.

Why go. Zlatni Rat seen from the water or the path above, with Hvar across the channel.

Hvar and Visthe outer islandsGreek field walls on Hvar, and Vis, the island closed to outsiders until 1989

Hvar is the long island south of Brač. Its town has the harbour and the crowds, but the older wonder is inland: the Stari Grad plain, fields laid out by Greek colonists in the 4th century BC and farmed ever since, their stone boundaries still in place, a UNESCO site since 2008. Vis lies further out, the most distant inhabited island off the coast. Yugoslavia kept it as a naval base and closed it to foreigners until 1989; the army only left around 1992. The isolation kept Vis town and Komiža quiet. Eat a viška pogača there and drink a Vugava, both told elsewhere in this guide.

Why go. Vis: an island that missed the tourist decades because the army held it.

Getting there
Hvar: Jadrolinija car ferry to Stari Grad; catamarans to Hvar town run about twice a day in winter (Oct 2026 listings). Vis: Jadrolinija line 602 sails year-round on a reduced winter timetable from late September. Check every sailing in November

Along the coast

The island town to the west, and the river canyon to the south-east.

Trogirtown on an islandA walled medieval town on its own islet, and Master Radovan's carved portal of 1240

Trogir sits on a small island between the mainland and Čiovo, joined to both by bridges. Inside the walls, the lanes are narrower, darker and quieter than Split's. At the centre the Cathedral of St Lawrence keeps its west door, signed by Master Radovan in 1240: Adam and Eve stand on lions, and around the arch run the months of the year, peasants slaughtering pigs and pressing grapes. Venice held the town from 1420 to 1797, and you see it in the Cipiko palace windows and the Kamerlengo fortress on the waterfront. UNESCO listed the whole town in 1997.

Why go. Radovan's portal: stand close and read the farming year carved into the stone.

Getting there

Omiš and the Cetinathe pirates' gorgeA town wedged in the Cetina gorge, where pirate dukes once charged ships for safe passage

Omiš sits where the Cetina breaks out of a limestone gorge into the sea, the cliffs rising straight behind the rooftops. In the 12th and 13th centuries the Kačić family ruled here and their galleys made the channels to the south dangerous for any ship that had not paid. Their lookout was Mirabella, a 13th-century tower above the old town that watched the Brač channel; the higher fortress of Starigrad, or Fortica, is about an hour's climb. Upriver the canyon narrows into green water and sheer walls, rafted in summer. The klapa festival is the calendar's.

Why go. Climb Mirabella: the gorge mouth and the channel the pirates watched, both from one wall.

The street

The cruise-day midday, and the photograph menu.

The cruise-day middaythe warningWhen several ships are in, the palace lanes jam from mid-morning to mid-afternoon: move your day around it

Split is a cruise port and the ships dock a few minutes' walk from the palace. On a day with several calls, the tenders and shuttles empty onto the Riva in waves, guided groups follow raised flags through the Peristyle and the cardo, and the narrow lanes stop moving. The same stones that were empty at seven are shoulder to shoulder by eleven, and stay that way until the passengers are recalled in the afternoon. The pressure eases from late October as the season winds down, but a fine day with two or three big ships can still fill the old town.

Why go. The palace is a different place before nine and after six: plan the walking for then and the lunch for elsewhere.

The laminated menu in the palacethe warningPhoto menus in five languages and a peka on the table in fifteen minutes: neither was made for you

Peka is cooked slowly under an iron bell buried in embers, lamb or octopus with potatoes, and a kitchen that does it properly needs to know you are coming. So when a terrace inside the palace walls offers peka at around 28 euros, ready in a quarter of an hour, from a laminated board of faded photographs translated into five languages, the arithmetic does not work. Whatever arrives was cooked earlier for someone else, or not under a bell at all. The dish itself is The Dish's to explain; the warning here is about the menu that sells it.

Why go. A peka is ordered the day before, not pointed at on a photograph.

Touch it today
On the palace terraces: laminated photo menus in five languages, a waiter at the door, and peka listed as if it could be ordered on the spot (about EUR 28, seen May 2026)

Read the glass and the plate

The jug wine, and the winter dish in August.

The pitcher of house winethe warningAn unlabelled jug on a Peristyle terrace, priced like Plavac Mali: ask what is in it, or ask for a bottle

Some terraces around the Peristyle pour their house wine from an anonymous jug, with no producer, grape or region on the menu, and charge for it as if it were a named Dalmatian red. Nobody at the table can tell you what it is or where it came from, and that is the problem: you are paying a coastal price for wine you cannot trace. Dalmatia makes plenty of honest bottles, and a labelled Plavac Mali or Pošip by the glass is rarely much dearer. If the waiter cannot name the grape or the cellar, take that as the answer.

Why go. A name on the label is the cheapest quality check in Split.

Touch it today
On the palace terraces: house wine by the jug or carafe, no producer or grape named, priced close to a labelled Plavac Mali

Pašticada in Augustthe warningA winter Sunday braise on a July menu by the Riva: in the heat, ask who made it and when

Pašticada is a slow dish: beef marinated for a day, then braised for hours in wine and prunes until the sauce turns dark and glossy. It belongs to cold months and long family Sundays. Spring: the saint

The Hvar night procession of Holy Week, Split's own saint on 7 May, and the film festival at Bačvice in June.

Za Križen, Hvar: the night of Maundy ThursdayHoly WeekSix villages, six barefoot cross-bearers, about 25 km through the night; UNESCO-listed in 2009

On the night from Maundy Thursday to Good Friday, six processions leave Jelsa, Pitve, Vrisnik, Svirče, Vrbanj and Vrboska at the same time and walk through all six villages, about 25 km in eight hours, without meeting. Each is led by a cross-bearer who walks barefoot or in socks; people put their names down for the role up to twenty years ahead. The kantaduri sing the Lamentations of the Virgin, a 15th-century text. The first written record is from 1658. UNESCO inscribed it in 2009. In 2027 Easter is on 28 March.

Why go. Watch from one village square, in silence; it is a devotion, not a show.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates and their honest passports: which are the city’s own, which are the coast’s, and which the islands and the hinterland send in.

ORIGIN

Gregada

White fish — scorpionfish, weever, bream, whatever the boat brought — layered with potatoes and simmered in white wine, olive oil, garlic and parsley, with no tomato at all. It is the older of the coast's two fish stews, and the one the Hvar fishermen claim. Origin. Hvar and the islands

Eat it — Konoba Fetivi, Veli Varoš
ORIGIN

Brodet

Several fish stewed with onion, tomato and wine and never stirred — the pan is shaken so the fish stays whole — served on white polenta. The tomato and the polenta are the Venetian side of the family; its cousin across the water is brodetto. Origin. The whole Dalmatian shore

Eat it — Dvor, Firule
ORIGIN

Peka

Not a recipe but a method: meat or octopus with potatoes, onion and rosemary under an iron bell heaped with embers, cooking in its own steam for two hours or more. The setup takes time, so it is ordered a day ahead: a peka served in fifteen minutes was not cooked for you. Lamb, veal and octopus are the three classics. Origin. Dalmatia, inland and island

ORIGIN

Pašticada

A joint of beef larded with garlic and cloves, marinated in wine vinegar, then braised for hours with dried plums, Prošek and tomato until the sauce turns dark and sweet-sour, and served with gnocchi. A Sunday, wedding and winter dish: in August, ask how it was made. Origin. Dalmatia-wide

Eat it — Apetit, in the palace
ORIGIN

Crni rižot

Risotto coloured black with cuttlefish and its ink, with onion, garlic and white wine, served loose rather than dry. Standard along the coast; each house argues about how much ink. Origin. The whole Adriatic shore

Eat it — Konoba Fetivi or Villa Spiza
ORIGIN

Soparnik

Two paper-thin sheets of dough round chopped chard, spring onion, garlic and parsley in a lot of olive oil, baked on the hearth under embers and brushed with oil and garlic. A medieval fasting dish with no meat and no cheese, registered as an EU protected geographical indication in 2016. Origin. Poljica, inland from Omiš

ORIGIN

Viška pogača

A covered flatbread filled with salted anchovies, slow-cooked onion and a little tomato, baked in the bread ovens of Komiža. A distant cousin of pizza from a separate Adriatic tradition; in Split, ask the bakeries. Origin. Vis, the furthest island

ORIGIN

Pršut and Paški sir

Pršut is the air-dried ham of Dalmatia, salted and hung in the dry bura wind, sometimes lightly smoked. Paški sir is the hard sheep's cheese of Pag, from native sheep grazing on salt-sprayed grass; it matures at least sixty days and became Croatia's first EU-protected cheese in 2019. Together they are the plate that opens a meal. Origin. Drniš and the hinterland; the island of Pag

Eat it — The Pazar, in the morning
