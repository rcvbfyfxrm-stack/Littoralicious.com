Cavalaire is the un-chic end of the Saint-Tropez peninsula, and that is its use: a family resort at the bottom of its own bay, with a working quay and the wild capes next door. THE ESSAY

Cavalaire sits at the bottom of its own bay on the southern shoulder of the Saint-Tropez peninsula, halfway between Le Lavandou and Saint-Tropez. Two capes hold the water, Cap Cavalaire to the west and Cap Lardier to the east, and behind the town the first ridges of the Maures climb past five hundred metres and break the mistral, so the bay stays warm and calm.

It faces the sea and turns its back on the mountain: a long arc of fine sand, a marina of some eleven hundred berths, and a grid of low apartment blocks and campsites that empties in winter and fills many times over in July. About eight thousand people live here all year.

Be honest about what it is not. There is no Byblos here, no Bardot legend, no paparazzi on the quay: that theatre plays twenty minutes north, over the capes. Cavalaire is the affordable, family-and-caravan sibling, a town that lives frankly off its beaches. Its beauty is borrowed and real at once — the protected coast of Cap Lardier and the Gigaro coves begins where the last beach bar ends.

The quintessence. The truest ritual is small and daily: the boats coming back into Port Héraclea mid-morning and selling the night’s catch off the quay. On Wednesday mornings, all year, the Provençal market fills the Place Jean Moulin. And in the evening the same town pours a cold, pale Côtes de Provence on the port while the light goes copper over the Maures.

Where it comes from

The bay was a sheltered anchorage long before it was a town, which is why the port carries a Roman name; its own card says which. Under Rome the coastal estates here made wine and shipped it, and at Pardigon, on the eastern edge of the bay, the villas still lie under the grass.

Then, for close to a thousand years, the shore emptied. Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Saracen raiders and later corsairs made the coast too dangerous to live on, and people kept to the fortified hills — Gassin, Ramatuelle — while the bay was worked by fishing boats and little else. Medieval Cavalaire belonged to the monks of Pignans, then from the fifteenth century to the Castellane.

The modern town is astonishingly young. The railway brought the first villas and bathers, the commune was cut out of Gassin in 1929, and after the war — this bay was a landing beach in August 1944, the card under Culture tells it — tourism finished what the trains began: classified a seaside resort in 1956, a Station de Tourisme in 2013.

Where the money sits

Cavalaire runs on two engines, and both point at the summer. One is the beach in the narrow sense: the long sand, the water-sports bases and the beach restaurants that sell grilled fish and a carafe of rosé at seafront prices for a few weeks a year. The other is the marina, run by a public company, which sells moorings, chandlery, diving and day charters to the same seasonal tide.

The housing tells the rest. Property portals put second homes at roughly two-thirds of the town’s dwellings (seen Jul 2026) — a town largely owned by people who sleep in it a few weeks a year. That is the line a visitor can taste: the beach-club terrace against where the town itself eats. The value is at the quay in the morning, at the Wednesday market, and in the bottle: a good pale Côtes de Provence bought from the estate costs a fraction of the same glass with a sea view.

And the clock is the season. The beach tables and the hotel dining rooms open in spring and close in October; the paths, the market, the quay and the garden of the Rayol run all year. Whoever comes in late May, June or September gets warm water and a town that is still a town; whoever comes in winter gets the capes to themselves.

THE STORY

The fish sold off the quay at Cavalaire comes from a fleet that has never answered only to the state. Along this coast fishermen elect their own judges, the prud'hommes, an institution first recorded in Marseille in 1431, older than any royal ordinance and still at work in the waters of the bay today.

The prud'homie is a fishermen's court. Skippers elect prud'hommes from among themselves, who share out the grounds, set gear and seasons, and settle disputes between boats. In Marseille the practice is recorded from 1431 and confirmed in 1477 by letters patent of King René; a 2025 press article gives 1452, so the founding date is argued. A decree of 19 November 1859, barely changed, still governs the thirty-three on the French Mediterranean, and the Ministry of Culture lists their practices as intangible heritage.

Each prud'homie has its own stretch of sea, fixed by decree. A Port-Cros national park study by Faget and Le Direach gives Saint-Tropez the waters from the Garonnette at Sainte-Maxime to Pramousquier beach near Cap Nègre, which takes in the bay of Cavalaire, and Le Lavandou those from the Pointe Blanche to the same beach. Saint-Tropez's archives run from 1770 to 1956. The study finds Cavalaire's shores, on the edge of both, full of quarrels between fishing communities from the late 18th century.

Cavalaire's port is attested in the 15th century, with its fishermen's houses. One tourism guide says its Maison des Pêcheurs houses a prud'homie created under Louis XIV; no official source for a separate Cavalaire prud'homie was found. Nor is the quay sale a royal privilege: Colbert's Ordonnance de la Marine dates from 1681, not 1652, and no article on direct sale was found. First sale now normally goes through a halle à marée under a 1989 decree, while small sales on the quay remain common.

Two places in this guide are where it still happens: the fishermen’s stand on the Quai Marc Pajot, mid-morning, and the soupe de roche and bouillabaisse of Maurin des Maures, ordered the day before.

The town on the bay

The marina with a Roman name, the green hollow behind the eastern beach, and the town that was a hamlet of Gassin until 1929.

Port Héracleathe portEleven hundred masts in a bay the Romans already used, and the morning catch sold on the quay

The bay faces south, screened from the Mistral by the Maures, and ships have sheltered here since antiquity. Scholars generally identify it with Heraclea Caccabaria, a stop named in the Roman Antonine Itinerary. Nothing ancient stands above the water: what has lasted is the shelter. Today it is Port Héraclea, about 1,100 berths for boats of 7 to 24 metres in a basin 2.5 metres deep, run since July 2018 by a public company, the SPL Port Héraclea, formed when the private and public harbours merged. Mid-morning the fishermen tie up at Quai Marc Pajot; their stand has its own table card.

Why go. Come at ten for the boats and the quay, then come back at dusk when the masts tick in the breeze and the bars open.

Pardigonthe green endMeadow, reeds and two Roman wine estates behind the beach: the one stretch of this bay nobody may build on

Where Cavalaire's sand runs out to the east, the houses stop and a green bowl of old fields and wet meadow opens up under the Maures, crossed by the Carrade stream. After years of court fights the Conseil d'État declared it a 'site remarquable du littoral' (unbuildable), and in January 2011 the Conservatoire du littoral made it inalienable. Excavations in the 1980s found two Roman villas, one on each side of the Carrade and one in each commune. The La Croix-Valmer one, Pardigon II, covers about 3,500 square metres and is described as one of the largest maritime villas on the coast of Roman Gaul, built around AD 20-40.

Why go. Walk from the beach into the meadow in the early morning, when herons work the reeds and nothing behind you is concrete.

The young towntown centreA chapel of 1910 and a commune of 1929: Cavalaire is younger than many of its visitors' grandparents

Cavalaire looks like an old fishing village and is nothing of the kind. Until 1929 it was a hamlet of Gassin, the hill village above; that year it became a commune of its own and took the name Cavalaire-sur-Mer. Its parish church tells the same story in stone. Saint-Laurent on Avenue Pierre Rameil began in 1910 as a modest chapel that answered to Gassin, was enlarged in 1932 and only became a parish in 1960. Tourism did the rest: the town was classified a beach resort in 1956 and a Station de Tourisme in 2013.

Why go. Look at Saint-Laurent knowing it is younger than the railway that built the town, then cross to Place Jean Moulin on a Wednesday.

La Croix-Valmer and Gigaroover cap lardierA village named after an emperor's vision, its vines running down to the beach where the wild coast begins

La Croix-Valmer takes its name from a legend: in 312, on his way to fight Maxentius, the emperor Constantine is said to have seen a cross in the sky here with the words 'In hoc signo vinces'. A stone cross raised on 16 April 1893 marks the supposed spot; several other places claim the same vision. The village was La Croix de Cavalaire and La Croix des Mimosas before it settled on La Croix-Valmer in 1934. It sits on a hill above its vineyards. Below, Gigaro beach ends at the start of the coast path to the capes, and the Plage du Débarquement continues Cavalaire's sand to the west.

Why go. Go down to Gigaro in late afternoon: the eastern corner of the beach is where the protected coast begins.

Older than it looks

A Ligurian fort on the ridge, the railway that made a resort, and a chapel that has vanished.

The hill fort on MontjeancuriosityTwo thousand five hundred years before the marina, a Ligurian people watched the bay from a dry-stone fort

Long before Greek or Roman ships put in, the people of the Maures built a dry-stone fortress on the Montjean ridge above Cavalaire. The heritage register describes it as a Ligurian oppidum occupied from the 6th to the 4th century BC. It was excavated between 1963 and 1979 and entered on the supplementary list of historic monuments in 1996. Then villas went up on the summit and destroyed part of it: only a narrow strip of ground is left intact. Finds from the dig are shown at the town's archaeology room. Walk up anyway for the view: the whole bay and the Gulf of Saint-Tropez.

Why go. Climb it on a clear winter morning: the people who built it chose the spot for the same view of the bay you get.

The train that made the towncuriosityA coastal railway built the resort, ran for sixty years and left behind a station you can walk into

Cavalaire owes its first villas and bathers to a narrow coastal railway. The Chemins de Fer du Sud de la France won the contract for a line from Saint-Raphaël to Hyères and Toulon, began work in 1882, and Cavalaire's station dates from 1890. The Second World War brought sabotage and bombing, the car took the rest of the traffic, and in 1949 the line was taken up and replaced by buses. Along the coast its route survives as roads and cycle tracks. Cavalaire's station still stands on Place de la Gare and is now the town's exhibition hall.

Why go. Next time you pass the bus station, look up: the town's first front door is still standing.

The lost seafarers' chapelcuriosityCavalaire once had a sailors' chapel older than the town by three centuries; nothing of it stands

The town's own history tells of a seafarers' chapel, Notre-Dame de l'Annonciade, built in 1639 when Cavalaire was a handful of fishermen and a landing place belonging to Gassin. It is said to have been shut in 1793, during the Revolution, and left to fall down, and its ruins to have been cleared away during the German occupation of the Second World War. That makes the young town's oldest building one that no longer exists. Do not confuse it with the Annonciade in Saint-Tropez, a different chapel that now holds the painters' museum (its own card, under Culture).

Why go. Ask at the médiathèque what the town knows of it: it is the oldest name in Cavalaire's history with nothing to show for it.

Under and on the water

The wrecks under the bay, the tuna traps of the gulf, and the boat with two pointed ends.

The wreck of the TogocuriosityA 76-metre cargo cut in two by a German mine in 1918 lies on the sand of the bay, bow towards the beach

The sheltered bay is one of this coast's serious wreck-diving grounds, with more than a dozen wrecks. The best known is the Togo, a 76-metre cargo ship launched in 1882 as the Ville de Valence and renamed in 1912 when the Genoese company Ilva bought her. On 12 May 1918 she hit a 150-kilo mine laid by the German submarine UC 35, broke in two and sank at once. She lay forgotten until 1977, when the biologist and diver Richard Calmes found her while laying buoys for a Club Nautique de Cavalaire regatta. She rests flat on a gently sloping sand bottom, west of the Pointe Dubreuil, bow pointing at Cavalaire beach, in red gorgonians.

Why go. Even if you never dive, look out from Cavalaire beach: the Togo's bow points straight at you from the bottom of the bay.

The tuna traps of the gulfcuriosityBrigitte Bardot's house is named after a fishing net: the madrague, a maze that caught the bluefin

For centuries the Provençal coast caught bluefin tuna in the madrague, a fixed labyrinth of nets set from spring across the fish's path, chamber after chamber, until the last one, where the fishermen hauled them out. The method goes back a very long way around the Mediterranean, and it peaked here from the 17th century before fading through the 19th. Old fishing leases place four madragues around the Gulf of Saint-Tropez: Cap de la Moutte, Pampelonne, Les Canebiers and La Nartelle. None is set today. The word stayed behind in place names, most famously La Madrague, Bardot's house on the Canebiers bay.

Why go. On the Canebiers path, read the name La Madrague as a fishing word first, a film star's address second.

Touch it today

The pointucuriosityPointed at both ends and painted bright: the small wooden boat of the Provençal fishermen

The pointu is the small fishing boat of this coast: wooden, decked, four to nine metres long, pointed at both ends so it rides a following sea and lands easily on a beach. Most now carry a motor where the lateen sail used to be. Along the Marseille shore the same family of boat is called a barquette, and a Marseille specialist says the word 'pointu' belongs to the Var and further east; the barquette has a more curved bow and stern. Boatyards in Marseille still teach the old way of building them, and about a hundred barquettes still sail there in the traditional manner.

Why go. Ask a fisherman on the quay whether his boat is a pointu or a barquette, and listen to the answer.

Touch it today
Look for them in the small-boat moorings of the ports on this coast, from Cavalaire to Saint-Tropez
Where the sources argue
Old guides say the boat's lines go back to antiquity; a Marseille museum says Neapolitan shipwrights brought the type in the late 19th century

The three capes

The great coastal path from Gigaro, and the crest that is its payoff.

Le Sentier des Trois Capsthe coast walkFrom Gigaro to Cap Taillat and back through the cork oaks: the longest wild shore left on the peninsula

Start at the eastern end of Gigaro beach, where the sand gives way to rock and the yellow marks of the coastal path begin. The path is the old customs track, cut along the shore so that patrols could watch every cove, and it still goes where they went: up over Cap Lardier, down to the long Briande bay, on to the sand neck of Cap Taillat. From there the loop turns inland and climbs back through cork oak, pine and cistus. What the walk shows is a boundary: the villas stop where the Conservatoire du littoral's land begins, and from there to Taillat there is almost nothing built. The third cape, Camarat with its lighthouse, stays ahead of you on the Ramatuelle side.

Why go. The only way to see this shore whole is on foot; leave the beach day for the coves you pass.

Cap Lardier's crestthe viewpointMid-walk, the cape's high point opens a half-circle of sea from Camarat's lighthouse to the Îles d'Hyères

Cap Lardier was classified as a protected site in 1966, and from 1978 the Conservatoire du littoral bought its pine massif, about 300 hectares, parcel by parcel. The result is the longest protected stretch of shore of the three capes, some 6 km, and from the crest you take it in at once: the Camarat lighthouse to the east, Cap Taillat's low headland below, the Îles du Levant and the other Îles d'Hyères offshore, and the whole bay of Cavalaire behind you. The Bastide Blanche and La Croix vines run almost to the cliff edge. There is no road to the point.

Why go. The one place on the walk where you see why the capes stayed empty: green on one side, built on every other.

Cavalaire's own

A headland loop behind the town, through cork oak and strawberry trees.

Sentier du Fenouilletthe home walkCavalaire's own headland walk: cork oak, strawberry trees and coves, from Bonporteau beach on foot

The Fenouillet headland is the Domaine Foncin. In 1977 Mireille Foncin, daughter of the geographer Pierre Foncin, a founder of the Alliance Française, left the land to the Conservatoire du littoral, one of the first gifts it ever received, and that is why this piece of the Corniche des Maures has no villas. The path leaves Plage de Bonporteau, follows the cliffs towards Pointe du Dattier with views to the Îles d'Hyères and back to Cap Lardier, then returns through a shaded, damp valley. Come in autumn: the strawberry trees flower and fruit at the same time, and the maquis smells of honey.

Why go. No car needed: the maquis of the Maures begins on the edge of town.

The wild end of the peninsula

A sandbar tying a rock to the land, the last beach of La Croix-Valmer, and three coves most people never learn the names of.

Cap Taillatthe sand neckA rocky headland tied to the shore by a lane of sand, with a different bay breaking on each side

Cap Taillat was once an island. The currents built a tombolo, a strip of fine sand no wider than a lane, that now ties it to the mainland, with the Baie de Briande on the La Croix-Valmer side and the Baie de Bonporteau (Ramatuelle's, not Cavalaire's) on the other. Wind from east or west moves the sand constantly and winter storms eat at it, so the dune is fenced with chestnut ganivelles. The Conservatoire du littoral owns some 142 hectares here. On 24 July 2017 a fire ran from Gigaro to L'Escalet and burned the cape to ash; after about 2,000 dead trees were felled the path reopened eight months later on a new line. The maquis came back fast; the umbrella pines will take decades.

Why go. Stand on the sand neck and swim on whichever side the wind is not blowing.

Plage de la Briandethe last beachLa Croix-Valmer's wildest beach: a long curve of sand with pines behind it and no road in

Briande is the last beach of La Croix-Valmer, a long curve of pale sand on the western flank of Cap Taillat, backed by thick maquis and umbrella pines with nothing built behind it. No road reaches it, so the only way in is the coastal path, about 45 minutes on foot from the southern car park at L'Escalet, and it lies inside the protected Lardier and Taillat corridor, which means it will never be built on or serviced. On a weekday out of August you can have a stretch of it to yourself. Naturists use the wild coves along the walk, but Briande is not an official naturist beach: read the signs.

Why go. The long walk in keeps the crowds down; that is the whole point of the place.

Cavalaire's side and the corniche

A free snorkel trail under the town’s own cove, the small coves of the Rayol, and a sea trail with a guide.

Bonporteaucove · snorkel trailCavalaire's wild cove, with a free buoyed snorkel trail laid over the seagrass just offshore

A steep footpath from the Avenue de Bonporteau drops through eucalyptus and palms to a cove of fine sand ringed with rock and tamarisk, with picnic tables in the pine shade. Just offshore, seven coloured buoys and underwater panels mark a snorkel trail over the Posidonia meadows, the nursery of this coast, where saupes, bream and girelles drift and a young grouper sometimes hangs under a ledge. It lies in the marine area of the Port-Cros national park and is free to anyone who can swim with mask and fins. In summer a state-qualified guide leads it; the park's guided mornings have been on Tuesdays. The buoys are in from June to September, so in late October the trail is closed for the year.

Why go. A free underwater trail on the edge of town, unsupervised and quiet while the main beach fills.

West of Cavalaire the Massif des Maures drops almost straight into the sea, and the coast road strings small pine-backed beaches between the Rayol and Le Lavandou: Le Rayol, Le Canadel, Cavalière, Pramousquier. The gardener and the painters

A garden of five continents on one slope, the pointillists in a chapel by the port, and the bay’s archaeology in one room.

Domaine du RayolgardenChile, California, the Cape and Australia planted on one Var hillside running down to the sea

In the years after 1908 the businessman Alfred Courmes bought wild maquis above the Baie du Figuier and had the architect Guillaume Tronchet build villas, a classical pergola (1910) and the Hôtel de la Mer. The aircraft builder Henry Potez took it over in 1940. By the 1980s it faced a 284-flat development; residents formed Les Amis du Rayol-Canadel in 1980 and the Conservatoire du littoral bought the 20-hectare estate in 1989. The gardener Gilles Clément then planted about 7 hectares with the floras of the world's dry-summer climates, so a Cape protea grows near a Californian ceanothus and a Chilean puya on the same slope.

Why go. Take the guided garden walk included in the ticket, then go down to the cove where the garden meets the sea.

Musée de l'Annonciadethe paintersA former chapel on the Saint-Tropez harbour, hung with Signac, Matisse and Bonnard before the yachts came

Saint-Tropez drew painters before it drew yachts. Paul Signac sailed in on his own boat in 1892, stayed, and pulled his friends south: Henri-Edmond Cross, Matisse, Bonnard, Marquet. The museum, founded in 1922 and housed in a former chapel by the port, holds the work that came out of those years, Pointillist dots, Nabi interiors and Fauve colour, much of it painted within sight of the building. It is a small museum and easy to see in an hour, and the paintings of the old harbour make a good pair with the real one outside the door.

Why go. Find the Signac views of the port, then step outside and compare them with the quay as it is now.

15 August 1944

The beach where the other Allied landing came ashore.

Alpha, the landing beach15 august 1944The other D-Day came ashore on this sand at 8am on 15 August 1944; today it is parasols and pédalos

On 15 August 1944 the bay was Alpha, the westernmost sector of Operation Dragoon, the Allied landing in Provence. At H-Hour, 8am, the US 3rd Infantry Division went in: its 7th Infantry Regiment on Alpha Red in the bay of Cavalaire, its 15th on Alpha Yellow near Pampelonne. Resistance was light and mines were the real danger. That assault shore is mostly the beach just east, in La Croix-Valmer, since renamed the Plage du Débarquement. The next evening, from about 7pm on 16 August, General de Lattre's French Army B began landing at Cavalaire, and on the 17th the 1st Free French Division regrouped at La Croix-Valmer.

Why go. Stand at the granite columns at the harbour entrance, then walk east along the sand to the Plage du Débarquement: one bay, two days.

The honest warning

What is sold as Provençal-made, and is not.

What is sold as Provençal craftthe warningThe soap stamped Marseille and the lavender sachet may have come from much further than the stall

The seafront souvenir shops sell two things above all as Provence in a bag: soap stamped 'savon de Marseille' and lavender. Neither name guarantees where it came from. Savon de Marseille has no protected definition in law; the traditional recipe is about 72 percent vegetable oil with no animal fat, perfume or colouring, yet bars carrying the stamp are made in Asia, sometimes from tallow. Lavender is similar. Bulgaria, not France, is now the world's largest producer of lavender oil, and sachets and soaps sold on Provençal stalls may hold lavandin, the coarser hybrid, or synthetic scent.

Why go. Read the back of the packet, not the front: ingredients and origin tell you what the stamp does not.

Ask for it by name

The pale pink of the coast, the classified growth, and the peninsula’s own old grape.

Côtes de Provence roséthe house pinkPale by design and dry to the last drop: the bottle on nearly every table round the bay

The paleness is a choice made in the cellar. Côtes de Provence rosé is pressed straight from the grape, or left on the skins for only a few hours, then fermented cold, so the juice takes the faintest onion-skin tint while the wine stays dry. It is the great appellation of the region, and rosé makes up close to nine bottles in ten of what it produces. Around the bay the vines grow within sight of the sea, and the better bottles finish with a salty edge. It does not keep: the newest vintage is the one to drink, cold, this year.

Why go. Order the youngest vintage on the list and send back anything served warm.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates and their honest, often contested, passports: which are this coast’s own, and which it borrowed from Marseille.

ORIGIN

Soupe de poissons de roche

The everyday ancestor of bouillabaisse and the one you actually order here: rockfish too small to sell, simmered with tomato, fennel, olive oil and saffron, then sieved to a rust-coloured broth. Croutons, grated cheese and rouille float on top. The fish behind it is landed on the quay a few metres away. Origin. The whole Provençal coast

Eat it — Maurin des Maures, Le Rayol-Canadel
ORIGIN

Bouillabaisse

The famous one, and worth being honest about. Several bony fish — rascasse, chapon, congre, saint-pierre, grondin — go into a saffron broth one after another, by firmness; the broth comes first with rouille, the fish after. On this bay it is ordered a day ahead, for at least two, and priced like the luxury it has become. Origin. Marseille — not a Cavalaire dish

Eat it — Maurin des Maures, ordered the day before
ORIGIN

Grand aïoli

Less a sauce than a Friday: garlic pounded stiff with olive oil and egg yolk, set in the middle of poached salt cod, boiled potatoes, carrots, green beans, snails and egg. Order it on a day with nowhere to be. Origin. Provence

Eat it — Le Comptoir des Saveurs, on the Nouveau Port, in season
ORIGIN

Tapenade

Black olives, capers, anchovy, garlic and oil pounded to a dark paste and spread on bread with the first cold glass. The name comes from tapena, the Provençal for the caper; Marseille credits a nineteenth-century chef with the recipe, and the date is argued. Origin. Marseille

Eat it — the Wednesday market, Place Jean Moulin
ORIGIN

Panisses

Chickpea flour cooked to a paste, set, cut and fried into golden batons, crisp outside and soft within, eaten with salt like chips. Working-class food that came to Marseille’s L’Estaque with Ligurian and Piedmontese families. Origin. Marseille, from Liguria

ORIGIN

Daube provençale

The winter dish of the hills: beef in large pieces marinated overnight in red wine, then simmered for hours with pork belly, carrots, tomato, black olives, orange peel, thyme and bay. A Maures daube may carry chestnuts. Origin. Provence, inland

ORIGIN

Pieds et paquets

For the curious: lamb tripe rolled into small parcels round pork belly, garlic and parsley, stewed slowly with the trotters in white wine and tomato. Nineteenth-century workers’ food that wasted nothing. Origin. Marseille and Sisteron

ORIGIN

Tarte tropézienne

The one sweet of the gulf: a sugar-crusted brioche split and filled with a cream of pastry cream and butter cream, sold by the slice or whole in its box. Eat it the day it is made. Origin. Saint-Tropez, over the capes
