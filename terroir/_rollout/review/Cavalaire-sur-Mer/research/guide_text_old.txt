THE SOUL OF THE PLACE What the place is, underneath Cavalaire-sur-Mer sits at the bottom of its own bay on the southern shoulder of the Saint-Tropez peninsula, in the Var, halfway between Le Lavandou and Saint-Tropez. The water is held in the crook of two capes — Cap Cavalaire to the west, Cap Lardier to the east — and behind the town the first wooded ridges of the Massif des Maures climb to over 500 metres, the Pradels crest breaking the Mistral so the bay stays warm and calm. It is a place that faces the sea and turns its back on the mountain: a long arc of fine sand, a marina of eleven-hundred-odd masts, and a grid of low apartment blocks and campsites that empties in winter and quadruples in July. It carries a serious history lightly. On 15-16 August 1944 this bay was Alpha sector of Operation Dragoon, the "other" Allied landing in the south of France; American divisions and, the following evening, General de Lattre's Army B came ashore across these beaches. A Landing Monument on the seafront marks it, and every August the town parades vintage military vehicles — but by ten in the morning the same sand is umbrellas and pédalos again. Be honest about what Cavalaire is not. It has none of Saint-Tropez's yacht-and-paparazzi glamour, no Byblos, no legend of Bardot — that theatre plays out twenty minutes north, over the capes. Cavalaire is the un-chic, affordable, family-and-caravan sibling: a resort town of roughly eight thousand year-round residents that swells enormously in summer, with the honesty of a place that lives frankly off its beaches. Its beauty is borrowed and real all at once — the wild, protected coast of Cap Lardier and the Gigaro coves begins where the last beach bar ends. The quintessence The truest Cavalaire ritual is small and daily: the fishing boats coming back into Port Héraclea around ten in the morning and selling the night's catch straight off the quay, rock fish and the odd loup still smelling of the sea. On Wednesday mornings, year-round, the Provençal market fills Place Jean Moulin from about 8:30 to 1 — goat cheese, olives, tapenade, sun-split tomatoes, oilcloth and espadrilles — the Var in one square. And then the evening version of the same town: a cold, pale Côtes de Provence rosé on the port as the light goes copper over the Maures and the returning windsurfers become silhouettes.

THE STORY How it came to be Cavalaire keeps a working fishing fleet, and when the boats come in the catch is sold straight off the quay from a single stand — 100% local, sorted by what the sea gave that morning: girelle and rascasse and chapon for the soupe de roche, sometimes a spider crab or a langouste. It is not a fish shop pretending to be a port; it is the port. A right that dates to Colbert's 1652 ordinance lets the fishermen sell part of their haul directly, without passing through the criée. Where — On the port of Cavalaire, quai Marc Pajot (Rue du Port); the pêcheurs sell daily from about 10am, subject to weather and catch Cavalaire's story starts as a name on a Roman sailing chart. The Greeks established trading posts along this coast — Olbia and Heraclea Caccabaria — around the 4th century BCE, and under Rome the site became Caccabaria, a maritime staging-post listed in the Antonine Itinerary. At Pardigon, on the eastern edge of the bay, archaeologists have found Gallo-Roman villa ruins where, tradition holds, land was parcelled out to deserving legionaries; the coastal estates here made and shipped wine. Roman order held until roughly 400 CE, and then the coast became one of the most dangerous in the Mediterranean. What followed was almost a thousand years of leaving. Visigoths at the close of the 5th century, Ostrogoths in the 6th and 7th, and repeatedly from the 8th century the Saracens, then corsairs and Turks from the sea, made the shore uninhabitable; the population retreated to fortified high ground for the better part of nine hundred years, and only a "Cavalaira" market stop clung on as a place ships put in. Medieval Cavalaire belonged first to the monks of the collegiate church of Pignans and, from the 15th century, to the powerful House of Castellane. For centuries the bay meant fishing above all — the wider Provençal coast worked the madrague, the ancient labyrinth of fixed tuna nets that peaked from the 17th century and faded through the 19th, with France's last madrague set off Marseille in 1914. The modern town is astonishingly young. Cavalaire was only detached from neighbouring Gassin and made its own commune in 1929; the little Saint-Raphaël-Toulon railway (the "train des Pignes") had reached the shore and the first villas and bathers were arriving. War interrupted the idyll — the August 1944 landings — and then tourism finished the transformation the trains had begun: Cavalaire was classified a beach resort in 1956, and by the 21st century the fishing village had become, frankly and fully, a summer machine, its population of about 8,100 (2023) multiplying many times over between June and September.

[why-now]
WHY NOW When to come Off the little cove of Bonporteau, reached only on foot down a steep path through eucalyptus and palms, someone has laid a marked path along the seabed: seven coloured buoys with submerged panels that guide you over meadows of posidonia — a flowering plant, not an alga, unique to the Mediterranean — where saupes, dorades and girelles drift and the occasional curious young mérou hangs beneath the rock overhangs. It is free to anyone with a mask and fins, which is the whole quiet magic of it. In July and August a Port-Cros park guide leads it on Tuesday mornings. Where — Plage de Bonporteau, Cavalaire (steep footpath from Avenue de Bonporteau, no cars); self-guided and free all summer, guided Tuesday mornings in Jul–Aug via the Port-Cros national park (approx. 1h30, ~8€/11€) Come in the shoulders — late May and June, or September into early October, when the sea is warm, the light is long, and the town is still a town. July and August are the honest trade-off: the beaches are gorgeous and lifeguarded and the port is alive, but the population multiplies, the campsites fill, and the single coast road in and out of the bay clogs solid at beach o'clock. If August is your only window, arrive at the market and the fish quay before nine, take the Sentier du Littoral toward Cap Lardier for space the beach clubs can't sell, and treat the crowds as the price of the water being this warm.

WHERE THE MONEY SITS What funds the table At the end of the wild coast, a thread of fine sand no wider than a lane ties a rocky headland to the mainland — a true tombolo, a formation the currents built and rare on the Mediterranean shore. You stand on it with a different bay breaking on each side: the Baie de Briande on the La Croix-Valmer flank, the Baie de Bon Porteau on the Ramatuelle flank, the water turning a different blue depending on the wind. There are no roads, no cafes, no lifeguard — just some 142 protected hectares, held by the Conservatoire du littoral since 1987 and reachable only on foot or by sea. Where — Boundary of La Croix-Valmer and Ramatuelle; no cars — shortest walk in from Plage de l'Escalet (Ramatuelle), or the longer coastal path from Gigaro A Cavalaire gem · Cove The douaniers' coves below Cap Lardier The sentier des douaniers slips out of the confidential eastern corner of Gigaro beach and threads the wildest stretch of this coast toward Cap Lardier, past a string of coves most people never learn the names of: the tiny, intimate crique de la Douane just after the start, the sandier Jovat with the ruins of a fishing cabin, and Brouis — the most feral of them, where you are more or less guaranteed to be alone. Because it all sits inside the Port-Cros national park's protected land (acquired by the Conservatoire in 1978) and a Natura 2000 zone, it stays startlingly calm even at the height of August. Where — Sentier du littoral from Gigaro (La Croix-Valmer) toward Cap Lardier; roughly 4.4 km one way, marked in yellow, sometimes steep — sturdy shoes Cavalaire runs on two engines, and both point at the summer. The first is the beach economy in the narrow sense: the long sand, the water-sports base, the beach restaurants that mark up a plate of grilled fish and a carafe of rosé to seafront prices for eight weeks a year. The second is the marina — Port Héraclea, run by a public company (SPL Port Héraclea), with roughly 1,100 berths and a 3-star "Station Nautique" label since 2008 — which sells moorings, chandlery, diving and day-charter to the same seasonal tide. The town has chased official tourism status hard (classified beach resort 1956, "Station de Tourisme" in 2013, "Famille Plus" for families) because the label is the product. A Cavalaire gem · Market Wednesday market on Place Jean Moulin Once a week the town's square fills with around a hundred stalls and the whole Provençal register: tapenade and anchoïade, olives, honey and olive oils, cheeses and charcuterie, mounds of seasonal fruit — and the rosé of the Gulf of Saint-Tropez, of course. It is the unhurried, morning-only kind of market where you assemble a beach lunch by tasting your way down the aisle. Go early; by one o'clock it folds away. Where — Place Jean Moulin, Cavalaire; every Wednesday morning, roughly 8:30–13:00, year-round The deeper economics are written in the housing. Property portals (SeLoger, Century 21) report that only around 30 percent of Cavalaire's dwellings are primary residences against roughly two-thirds second homes, with house prices reported near €5,550 per square metre — a town largely owned by people who sleep in it a few weeks a year. That is the fault line a visitor can taste: the beach-club terrace versus where a local actually eats. The value is at the Wednesday market on Place Jean Moulin, at the fishermen's direct sale on the quay around ten each morning, and in the bottle rather than the setting — a €10-15 pale Côtes de Provence rosé from the Var vineyards that surround the Maures, or a structured, Mourvèdre-based Bandol (AOC since 1941) from just along the coast, costs a fraction of what the same glass fetches with a sea view.

The dishes & the drink The typical plates and the local product 9 dishes
  <card dish:Rosé de Provence (Côtes de Provence & Bandol)> The true drink of this coast and the honest local product. Côtes de Provence is the region's giant AOC, and rosé is roughly 80 percent of what Provence makes — the pale, near-salmon style from short skin contact and cold fermentation. Just west, tiny Bandol (about 1,600 ha) makes a darker, structured, age-worthy rosé and red built on Mourvèdre. Drink it cold, young, and cheaper than the sea view suggests. Origin Var / Provence; Côtes de Provence and Bandol (Bandol AOC created 1941), vines here since Roman times
  <card dish:Soupe de poissons de roche & rouille> The everyday, unglamorous ancestor of bouillabaisse and the one you actually order here: rock fish too small to sell, simmered with tomato, saffron, olive oil and fennel, then sieved to a rust-coloured broth. Served with croutons, grated cheese and rouille — a garlic-and-saffron chilli mayonnaise you float on the bread. A fisherman's thrift dish, not a luxury. Origin Provençal coast, fishermen's cuisine; shares the Phocaean 'kakavia' root of Marseille
  <card dish:Bouillabaisse> The famous one, and worth being honest about: it is a Marseille dish, not a Cavalaire native, descended from the Phocaean fishermen's cauldron ('kakavia'). Several bony Mediterranean fish added one at a time to a saffron-and-fennel broth, the soup served first with rouille, the fish on a platter after. A real one is expensive and slow; a cheap 'bouillabaisse' on a tourist terrace is usually just fish soup — the 1980 Marseille Charter exists precisely because of that drift. Origin Marseille (Greek Phocaean roots, ~600 BCE); Charter de la Bouillabaisse, 1980
  <card dish:Aïoli (le grand aïoli)> Less a sauce than a Friday institution: a stiff emulsion of garlic pounded with olive oil, served as the centrepiece of a spread of poached cod, boiled potatoes, carrots, green beans, snails and egg. Ancient Mediterranean in root, mentioned in medieval Provençal texts. Order it on a day you have nowhere to be. Origin Provence / wider Mediterranean; garlic-and-oil emulsion of great antiquity
  <card dish:Tapenade> The apéritif of the coast: black (or green) olives, capers, anchovy, garlic and olive oil pounded to a dark paste, spread on bread with that first glass of rosé. The name comes from Provençal 'tapena', caper. Its invention is traditionally credited to a Marseille chef around 1880 — plausible, and worth hedging. Origin Marseille, credited to around 1880 (attribution traditional, not certain)
  <card dish:Panisses> Chickpea-flour cooked to a paste, set, sliced and fried into golden batons — crisp outside, soft within, eaten with salt like chips. A working-class street food that came to Marseille's Estaque district with Ligurian and Piedmontese immigrants in the 19th century. Cousin of Nice's socca, but cut thick and fried. Origin Marseille (Estaque), 19th century; Ligurian/Piedmontese roots
  <card dish:Daube provençale> The winter dish of the Var interior: beef cut in big chunks, marinated overnight in Provençal red wine, then simmered for hours with pork belly, carrots, tomato, black olives, orange peel and a bouquet of thyme and bay. Born as a preservation and thrift dish, now the region's Sunday and celebration stew — the leftovers traditionally sauce a plate of ravioli. Origin Provence; a medieval/early-modern braise, elevated over time
  <card dish:Pieds et paquets> For the curious: lamb tripe rolled into little parcels around pork belly, garlic and parsley (the 'paquets'), stewed slowly with lamb trotters (the 'pieds') in white wine and tomato. A 19th-century Marseille workers' dish, still an emblem of Provençal winter tables — offal-forward and not for everyone, which is exactly why it's genuine. Origin Marseille, 19th century (Sisteron also claims a version)
  <card dish:Tarte tropézienne> The one truly local sweet, from over the capes: a sugar-crusted brioche split and filled with a blend of pastry cream and buttercream. Created in Saint-Tropez in 1955 by Polish-born baker Alexandre Micka; Brigitte Bardot, filming 'And God Created Woman', is said to have suggested the name, which Micka trademarked in 1973. The closest thing to a hometown dessert on this gulf. Origin Saint-Tropez, 1955, Alexandre Micka; named c.1956, trademarked 1973

What to Drink rose → cru classe in the glass Pale as a peeled peach, poured colder than the evening and cheaper than the view suggests. This is rose country in the literal sense. Cotes de Provence, delimited as a VDQS in 1951 and raised to full AOC by decree on 24 October 1977, is the region's giant appellation, and rose is roughly nine parts in ten of what it makes: the near-salmon style built on short skin contact and cool fermentation, from Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah and Mourvedre, with Tibouren the local heritage grape. Drink it young and cold. Just west along the coast, tiny Bandol (AOC since 1941) makes the serious counterpoint, a darker, structured, age-worthy rose and red built on sun-loving Mourvedre. A Cavalaire gem · Vineyard Cru Classé rosé, drunk facing the Îles d'Or La Croix-Valmer grows one of Provence's rare classified growths: Domaine de la Croix, founded in 1882 and ranked Cru Classé in the 1955 classification, its vines running down schistous hillsides toward the sea at the exceptional Bastide Blanche site by the water. Buy a bottle of the pale 'Irrésistible' rosé at the cellar, then carry it down to Gigaro beach for an evening apéro with the Îles d'Or on the horizon — Port-Cros floating out to sea. The wine was made within sight of where you drink it: a classified growth, poured on the shore it grows above. Where — Cellar at 816 Boulevard de Tabarin, La Croix-Valmer (Mon–Sat 10:00–13:00 & 15:00–19:30; guided cellar visits by appointment, from March); apéro on Gigaro beach The vineyards run almost to the water here, and the classification proves it: the 1955 Cotes de Provence roll of Crus Classes, twenty-three estates in all, includes Domaine de la Croix at La Croix-Valmer, founded in 1882, its schist hillsides tipping toward the sea at the Bastide Blanche site. Buy the pale bottle at the cellar, carry it down to Gigaro beach, and drink a classified growth in sight of the vines that made it, with Port-Cros floating on the horizon. That is the ritual the whole coast is really selling. For names to trust: Domaine de la Croix for the local Cru Classe apero; Chateau Minuty at Gassin, itself one of the original 1955 Crus Classes, for the gulf's best-known pale rose; and, for the neighbour worth a detour, a Bandol from a benchmark house such as Domaine Tempier, where the Mourvedre earns its cellar time. The market bottle of good Cotes de Provence costs a fraction of the same glass with a sea view, so buy on Place Jean Moulin and pour on the sand.

[story-box]
1944 · the dawn an army woke on the sand The one story worth the detour story Before the umbrellas went up, the war came ashore in this exact bay. On 15 August 1944 Cavalaire's bay was Alpha, the westernmost of the three assault sectors of Operation Dragoon, the other and half-forgotten Allied landing in the south of France. At H-Hour, 0800, the 7th Regimental Combat Team of Major General John 'Iron Mike' O'Daniel's 3rd Infantry Division stormed Alpha Red in the bay, preceded by Duplex-Drive amphibious Sherman tanks of the 756th Tank Battalion swimming ashore ahead of the infantry. By midday the division had pushed its beachhead line inland. The reality on the sand was less cinematic than the plan. German resistance on Alpha and Delta was light, the coastal Osttruppen surrendered quickly, and the real danger was mines rather than machine guns. The next evening, around seven on 16 August, General de Lattre's French Army B began coming ashore here at Cavalaire on its way to take Toulon and Marseille, both liberated inside a fortnight. Dragoon was overwhelmingly successful and, unlike Normandy some ten weeks earlier, is barely remembered. Here is the honest turn: this is not a solemn coast. By mid-morning the same sand is pedalos and parasols again, and each August the landing is marked with commemorations and vintage military vehicles before the beach reopens for trade. The one thing to know on the ground is that the assault shore of 15 August is largely the Plage du Debarquement, just east in neighbouring La Croix-Valmer, an uninterrupted continuation of Cavalaire's Grande Plage marked by a memorial and a large car park, while Cavalaire's own seafront carries the granite Monument du Debarquement de Provence at the harbour entrance, on the spot where de Lattre's Army B came ashore the next day. Go here The Monument du Debarquement de Provence on Cavalaire's harbour-entrance esplanade, paired with the Plage du Debarquement just east in neighbouring La Croix-Valmer — the 15 August 1944 assault shore, a walkable memorial site (memorial, steles and a large car park) at the eastern end of the same bay

Curiosities The odd, the true, the only-here — facts to drop at dinner 11 notes HILLTOP Long before Rome, a Ligurian oppidum crowned Montjean, the 460-metre ridge still looming over the bay, built from around the 6th century BC. BURIED At Pardigon, on the bay's edge, lie the remains of what is described as one of the largest maritime Roman villas known in the Mediterranean - today buried out of sight. MADRAGUE The wider Golfe de Saint-Tropez once ran madragues, fixed-net tuna traps of a roughly two-thousand-year lineage set from spring for the bluefin run; old fishing leases place four of them around the gulf - Cap de la Moutte, Pampelonne, Les Canebiers and La Nartelle. POINTUS The pointu - the double-ended Provençal fishing boat whose lines trace back to antiquity - still works this coast; its Marseille cousin, the barquette marseillaise, is inscribed on France's national inventory of intangible cultural heritage. CATCH The sheltered bay is still fished on a small scale for Mediterranean staples like rouget (red mullet) and langouste (spiny lobster). SAINT-TROPEZ Cavalaire never grew past a modest anchorage; the gulf's great harbour became Saint-Tropez, across the peninsula, rebuilt and repopulated by a Genoese-led colony from 1470. RAIL The town once sat on the coastal railway between Hyères and Saint-Raphaël, which reached Cavalaire in 1890; the line is long gone, but the old station survives as an exhibition hall. CHAPEL The seafarers' chapel of Notre-Dame de l'Annonciade dates to 1639, was shut during the Revolution in 1793, and its ruins were finally cleared during the WWII occupation. TWIN TOWN Its unlikely twin town sits across the Atlantic: New Port Richey, Florida. Alpha Beach, 1944 In Operation Dragoon the bay carried the code-name Alpha, the westernmost landing sector; the US 3rd Infantry Division came ashore on 15 August 1944 and General de Lattre's French Army B followed on the 16th - a seafront monument marks it, and the town parades every 15 August. A drowned fleet under the swimmers The sheltered bay is one of the Riviera's serious wreck-diving grounds, with more than a dozen wrecks - among them the cargo Togo, sunk by a mine on 12 May 1918 and now sheathed in red gorgonian at about 58 metres.

Avoid the traps What to skip, and what to do instead 4 traps Using Cavalaire as a car-base to "do" Saint-Tropez in August. The famous village is only 17 km away on the map, but in peak season you will spend the holiday idling on the peninsula's beach roads rather than in it. Instead Take the Bateaux Verts navette from Quai Marc Pajot across the water, or move at dawn and dusk. Keep the car for the quiet western Corniche des Maures instead (Rayol-Canadel, Le Lavandou), where the road actually flows. Chasing the Pampelonne club-beach fantasy from here. Those celebrity sand-lots sit on the far side of the peninsula, an hour of traffic and a three-figure sunbed away. Instead Cavalaire has its own 2 km-plus sand bay, and the wild coves of Cap Lardier and Gigaro are a short hop east: protected, undeveloped, free. That is the local's beach day, not the paid mattress. Arriving in deep winter expecting the summer resort, then reading the shuttered seafront as "nothing here." Come January half the terraces are closed and the port empties. Instead Come off-season for the real terroir: the Wednesday Provencal market on Place Jean Moulin (year-round, ~8:30-13:00), the daily fisherman's criee at the port from around 10:00, the Domaine du Rayol garden open all year, and the mimosa blazing gold along the corniche in February. Ordering "fresh fish" on a seafront terrace without asking where it came from. Plenty of resort tables plate frozen catch at high-season prices, a short walk from real day-boats. Instead Buy at the port criee in the morning, where a handful of Cavalaire fishermen sell the actual day's catch when the weather let them out: daurade, loup, rascasse for soupe. Then ask which kitchen cooks its own. Cavalaire rewards subtraction: leave the car, skip the club-beach, buy off the boat, and the coast the crowds drove past is suddenly yours.

What to drink — the pours What to actually order — the regional glass, and the growers to look for 7 pours
  <card drink:Côtes de Provence Rosé — the pale, bone-dry archetype> The colour is a decision, not a dilution: these rosés are direct-pressed or briefly macerated, so the pink stays onion-skin pale while the wine drinks bone-dry and saline — the vines sit close enough to the sea to taste it. It is not the sweet blush the tint suggests. One thing to carry: buy the youngest vintage on the shelf and drink it cold this summer, not next. Look for Domaine de la Croix (La Croix-Valmer); Domaine de la Bastide Blanche (its organic seaside estate, La Croix-Valmer)
  <card drink:Cru Classé Rosé — Provence's own 1955 ranking> Long before today's appellation rules, a 1955 ministerial decree classified a handful of Provence estates; of the original list, eighteen still carry the words 'Cru Classé' on the label, and Domaine de la Croix — founded 1882, left derelict, then bought and wholly replanted after 2001 — is one of them. On the schist and clay above the bay it makes a rosé with more grip and density than the crowd. The pedigree is real, but it was awarded on seniority, not a tasting — a fact worth knowing before you pay for it. Look for Domaine de la Croix (La Croix-Valmer)
  <card drink:Seaside sand-terroir Rosé with Tibouren> Tibouren is the peninsula's old grape — savoury, faintly orange-tinged, scented with garrigue rather than fruit-candy, and long tied to this stretch of the Saint-Tropez coast. On the sandy, wind-scoured strips between Cap Lardier and Cap Taillat, where Bastide Blanche's vines run nearly to the cliff, it gives a rosé that smells of hot pine and salt. Both houses are certified organic; taste this against a supermarket Provence and you learn what 'saline' actually means. (Bastide Blanche and La Croix are one estate under two labels — don't chase them as rivals.) Look for Domaine de la Bastide Blanche (Domaine de la Croix's organic seaside estate, vines between Cap Taillat and Cap Lardier); Domaine La Tourraque (Ramatuelle, in the Trois Caps site, organic)
  <card drink:Côtes de Provence Blanc — Rolle by the water> Rolle is simply the local name for Vermentino, and by this coast it turns out a white of citrus pith and sea-spray minerality — the natural partner to the fishermen's morning catch on the quay. It is far rarer than the rosé and rarely leaves the region; if you see it, that is the bottle to carry back to the boat. Look for Domaine de la Croix (La Croix-Valmer); Domaine La Tourraque (Ramatuelle)
  <card drink:Côtes de Provence Rouge — Mourvèdre for the cool evening> Provence is not only pink. Mourvèdre and Syrah on the warmer soils give a dark, herb-and-leather red built for a mistral-cool night off the water. Serve it lightly chilled — around 15°C, not room temperature — which is the single correction most people need to make with southern reds. Look for Domaine de la Croix (Cru Classé, La Croix-Valmer); Domaine La Tourraque (Ramatuelle)
  <card drink:Pastis — the shore aperitif ritual> The ritual is fixed: a measure of pastis, then roughly five of cold water poured over ice until the clear spirit clouds — the 'louche' — drunk before lunch in the shade. Henri Bardouin blends more than 65 plants and spices where the industrial anise leans on a handful. Honest caveat: it is distilled inland at Forcalquier, in the Haute-Provence back-country, not on the coast — the seaside owns the ritual, the hills own the bottle.   <card drink:Bière du Golfe — the local craft beer> The gulf's first microbrewery, with a certified-organic range brewed in the Var — a genuinely local craft pour among a sea of imported lager. Note it is made in Sainte-Maxime, across the Gulf of Saint-Tropez, not in Cavalaire itself; ask for it by name at a caviste rather than expecting it on every terrace tap.   <card landmarks:Espace Archéologique Municipal Aristide Fabre> The town's small municipal archaeological room — on the ground floor of the médiathèque — tracing Cavalaire's deep past through collections, models and panels: the Iron Age oppidum of Montjean, and above all the Gallo-Roman villas of Pardigon, which made and shipped wine from this very coast.   <card landmarks:Port de Cavalaire — the ancient Heraclea anchorage> This deep, south-facing bay is generally identified as the old port of Heraclea Caccabaria, named in the Roman Antonine Itinerary — a well-sheltered anchorage screened from the Mistral, which is exactly why Greek, then Roman, then medieval traders used it.   <card landmarks:Monument du Débarquement de Provence> A granite twin-column memorial (by architect G. Nadal, 2nd Grand Prix de Rome) to the Allied landing of 15–16 August 1944, Operation Dragoon. Culture, art &amp; the beautiful Beauty with a backstory — the rooms and buildings worth the detour 6
  <card culture:Monument du Débarquement, Plage de Cavalaire (Alpha Beach)> An open-air landing memorial on the seafront promenade, marking where the Allied invasion of southern France came ashore. Free, always open; walk it, don't queue for it. The terroir truth locals live with: this mild holiday bay was code-named Alpha Beach.   <card culture:Domaine du Rayol — Le Jardin des Méditerranées> A ~20-hectare hillside garden (about 7 ha planted) that sets the world's Mediterranean-climate regions side by side — Chile, California, South Africa, Australia, the Canaries — tumbling down to the Baie du Figuier opposite the Golden Isles. Terroir made literal: the Var coast rebuilt as a living atlas of every dry-summer climate on earth.   <card culture:Le Sentier Marin du Rayol (underwater snorkel trail)> A guided snorkel — wetsuit, mask, fins — over the protected seagrass meadows of the Baie du Figuier, led by a state-certified guide.   <card culture:Cap Lardier & Cap Taillat coastal path> The sentier du littoral rounding two of the peninsula's three wild capes — a coastal out along the bays of Cavalaire and Briande to the sand isthmus of Cap Taillat, with an inland wooded return. Signal Verified: capes protected by the Conservatoire du Littoral, loop ~14 km / ~4 hrs from Gigaro, 180° summit panorama (Camarat lighthouse, Cap Taillat, Îles du Levant) — AllTrails / Visorando / Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur Tourism
  <card culture:Sentier du Fenouillet, Corniche des Maures> Cavalaire's own home walk: a signposted coastal stretch from Plage de Bonporteau toward Pointe du Dattier, looping back through a shaded valley.   <card culture:Gassin> A medieval hill-village ringed by a rampart-walk terrace — the Promenade/Place deï Barri — shaded by centuries-old hackberry trees, with restaurant balconies hanging over the whole gulf. Best at dusk, when the day-trippers thin. Officially one of Les Plus Beaux Villages de France, Gassin sits some 200 m up with a commanding read of the coast — its founders built high precisely to see sea-raiders coming, and that defensive height is now the finest free viewpoint around: in one slow turn you take in the Bay of Saint-Tropez, the dark Massif des Maures and the Golden Isles. The oddity to hunt for: l'Androuno, a lane just 29 cm wide, claimed among the narrowest streets in the world. Signal Verified: classed Plus Beaux Villages de France, ~200 m elevation, Place/Promenade deï Barri panorama over the bay of Cavalaire and the Îles d'Or, l'Androuno ~29 cm wide — Saint-Tropez Tourisme / Wikipedia (L'Androuno)

[hotel-bars]
A drink in the grand hotels The beautiful hotel bars — worth it for one drink 3
  <card hotel-bars:Bar 1904, Château de la Messardière (Airelles)> A dappled-terrace lounge by day, low-lit speakeasy by night, inside a turreted château hotel looking over the Bay of Saint-Tropez and its own park. Come for one impeccable drink, not the room rate. This is, plausibly, one of the very first bars the Côte d'Azur ever had. The château was a 19th-century wedding gift from cognac merchant Gabriel Dupuy d'Angeac to his daughter Louise. In 1904 Madame de La Messardière began serving refreshments to her glamorous Belle Époque guests — opening what the house counts among the Riviera's earliest bars, and the year the bar takes its name from. It became Airelles' palace after a top-to-bottom restoration, reopening in July 2021. The terroir note: Saint-Tropez's aperitif culture is older and grander than the yacht-set cliché suggests. Honesty: palace prices — a sundowner, not a night out. Signal Verified: refreshments first served 1904 (Mme de La Messardière), among the Côte d'Azur's first bars; Airelles grand reopening 1 July 2021 — Airelles official site
  <card hotel-bars:Le Vista, Lily of the Valley> A west-facing bar and restaurant terrace on the hilltop above Gigaro, positioned to hand you a glass exactly as the sun drops behind the Îles d'Hyères. Philippe Starck designed the whole resort — opened end of June 2019 — like a Californian villa crossed with the Hanging Gardens of Babylon, and then aimed its main terrace at the sunset. The terroir logic is simple and worth knowing: this is the peninsula's west-facing shoulder, so unlike Saint-Tropez proper the light sets over the sea and the Golden Isles here. Honesty by negation: it's a design hotel, not weathered old Provence — dress, crowd and bill are all polished. Worth it for the golden hour. Signal Verified: opened late June 2019, architecture and interiors by Philippe Starck; Le Vista bar positioned to take in the sunset — Starck.com / Robb Report / Leading Hotels of the World
  <card hotel-bars:La Brigantine — Lily of the Valley beach club, Gigaro> A feet-in-the-sand beach club and Italian kitchen at the quiet end of Gigaro, good for a late-afternoon glass with the sea a few metres away. A drink at the bar is the low-commitment way in; the sunbeds are beach-club priced. This is the golfe's sundowner ritual done on the peninsula's calm, west-facing side — the anti-Pampelonne. Here's the honest map, which matters more than any single address: Cavalaire's own long beach is a genuine public promenade (pedalos, gelato, families), lovely but not a polished-club scene; the terrace-drink culture sits a few minutes west at Gigaro; and the see-and-be-seen legends (Club 55, Loulou) are a further 30–40 minutes north at Pampelonne in Ramatuelle. Pick your shore by the mood you want. Signal Verified open 2026: Lily of the Valley's Brigantine beach club at the far end of Plage de Gigaro (beachfront Italian, chef Vincent Maillard), 2026 season Feb–Nov — Lily of the Valley official site / Excellence Riviera

[walks]
Walks &amp; the coast on foot The best of the place, slowly 3
  <card walks:Le Sentier du Littoral — Les Trois Caps (Gigaro → Cap Lardier → Cap Taillat)> The signature coastal walk of this coast: from Gigaro beach the yellow-marked sentier hugs the sea to Cap Lardier, drops along the Briande bay to the sand isthmus of Cap Taillat, and loops back inland through cork oak and maquis. The full Gigaro loop is roughly 14 km / about 4 hours; you can also just walk out-and-back to a beach. This is the last long undeveloped stretch of coast between Cavalaire and Saint-Tropez, and the reason is public money, not luck. Cap Lardier was classified under the 1930 landscape law (a site classé) back in 1966, then bought parcel by parcel by the Conservatoire du littoral from 1978 onward — it holds the longest protected coastal fringe of the three caps, some 6 km. It is managed with the Port-Cros National Park and the commune and has been a Natura 2000 zone since 2006. The lesson the walk teaches: villas stop exactly where the Conservatoire's boundary begins. Signal Cap Lardier classified under the 1930 law in 1966; acquired by the Conservatoire du littoral from 1978; Natura 2000 since 2006
  <card walks:Sentier du Fenouillet (Domaine Foncin)> A roughly 4.5 km land-and-sea loop over the Fenouillet headland just behind Cavalaire, threading cork oaks and arbousiers (strawberry trees), dropping to wild coves and opening onto long views of the Îles d'Or. Some scrambly, narrow sea-edge sections — proper shoes. The headland is the Domaine Foncin, given to the nation early: Mireille Foncin — daughter of geographer and Alliance Française co-founder Pierre Foncin — bequeathed the land to the Conservatoire du littoral in 1977, one of the very first gifts it ever received, keeping this run of the Corniche des Maures from being villa'd over. It teaches one real thing — the arbousier flowers and ripens its fruit at the same time in autumn, which is why the maquis smells of honey then. Nearby Cap Nègre carries its own memory: French Commandos d'Afrique came ashore here on the night of 14–15 August 1944 as the US 3rd Infantry Division landed on the Cavalaire beaches during the Provence landings. Signal Land bequeathed to the Conservatoire du littoral by Mireille Foncin in 1977 (one of its first-ever donations)
  <card walks:Pointe / high point of Cap Lardier> The panoramic crest of the cape reached mid-walk, giving a roughly 180° sweep over the Cap Camarat lighthouse, Cap Taillat, the Îles d'Hyères and du Levant, and back across the whole bay of Cavalaire. This is the payoff of the Trois-Caps hike and the clearest possible read of why the Conservatoire du littoral fought so hard for this land: from the top you see one green, empty cape holding out against a coast built up on every side. It is the longest protected coastal fringe of the three caps — about 6 km — laid out in front of you in a single glance. Honest note: on high fire-risk 'red' days in summer the Maures massif and these cape trails can be closed to walkers, so check the local prefectural access map before you set out. Signal 180° panorama over Cap Camarat, Cap Taillat and the Îles d'Hyères; the caps' longest protected fringe (~6 km)

[calas]
Beaches &amp; calas The coves worth the walk 4
  <card calas:Cap Taillat> A former island tethered to the mainland by a thread-thin sand tombolo, with a turquoise swim beach on each side — the wild Baie de Briande to the west, and the l'Escalet-facing beach on the Ramatuelle side to the east. About 142 hectares of protected headland, no road, only the coast path. A Cavalaire gem · Ritual The Saint-Pierre fishermen's fête and the burning boat At the end of June the fishing community keeps the feast of their patron, Saint-Pierre: a procession through the streets, then a second procession out to sea from the Esplanade Sainte-Estelle, gathering fishermen, the old wooden pointus, divers and the SNSM lifeboats. A great sardinade is grilled on the beach at dusk, there is dancing, and the night ends — the oldest, strangest, most beautiful part — with an old boat set alight on the sand and left to burn against the dark water, a gesture that once meant solidarity with the poorest fisherman. Where — Cavalaire beach and port; late June (the Saint-Jean/Saint-Pierre weekend), run by the town's traditions association — exact dates vary yearly The isthmus is a living thing: east and west winds and the currents constantly redistribute its sand, and winter storms chew at it, so it is fenced with ganivelles to hold the dune together. The Conservatoire du littoral bought Cap Taillat in 1987 to head off a Club Med development, and it lies in the Port-Cros National Park adhesion zone. On 24 July 2017 a fire ran from Gigaro to l'Escalet, burning more than 500 hectares and reducing Cap Taillat to ash; the trail reopened only eight months later, after some 2,000 charred trees were felled and the path was rerouted to stop walkers trampling the recovering ground. The maquis greened back fast — but the umbrella pines will need decades to return. Signal Conservatoire du littoral property since 1987 (bought to block a Club Med); ~142.5 ha; devastated by the 24 July 2017 fire, trail reopened 2018
  <card calas:Plage de la Briande> The last and wildest beach of La Croix-Valmer, on the Briande side of the Cap Taillat tombolo — a long curve of sand backed by dense maquis and parasol pines, with clear turquoise water and almost nothing built behind it. Its famous 'alone in the world' feeling is a direct product of protection: no road reaches it, so the only way in is the sentier du littoral from the southernmost l'Escalet car park, about 45 minutes on foot. Because it sits inside the Conservatoire-protected Cap Lardier–Cap Taillat corridor, it will never be built on or serviced. Honest note: naturists frequent the wild coves you pass along the walk, but Briande itself is not an official naturist beach — read the signage. Signal Accessible only on foot via the sentier du littoral; inside the protected Lardier–Taillat corridor
  <card calas:Plage & Sentier Sous-Marin de Bonporteau> Cavalaire's own wild cove — fine sand ringed by rock, eucalyptus, tamarisk and wild palms, with picnic tables in the pine shade. Just offshore runs a free underwater snorkel trail marked by buoys and submerged information panels, in the town's Marine Protected Area. The cove lies inside the marine adhesion zone of the Port-Cros National Park, and the sentier sous-marin is open to any independent swimmer with a mask and snorkel — no ticket, no guide — following seven buoys over Posidonia seagrass, the meadow that is the nursery of the whole coast and the reason the water is so clear. Swimming here is unsupervised, which is exactly why it stays quiet while the town beaches fill (guided 'Esprit parc national' visits run Tuesday mornings in season). Signal Free marked underwater trail (7 buoys) in Cavalaire's Port-Cros National Park marine protected area (open in season)
  <card calas:Plage du Figuier (the wild coves of the Rayol coast)> A small, wild, white-sand cove tucked under the Corniche — one of a string of coves between the Rayol and Canadel beaches, loved by snorkelers for clear water over rock and Posidonia. Access is deliberately rough: steep, sometimes unmarked paths, described locally as 'sportif'. These coves stay wild precisely because the slope is too steep and the trails are left rugged — the friction is the protection. Figuier sits just along from the Domaine du Rayol's Baie du Figuier, where the estate runs its guided sentier marin (the 'Jardin Marin', its marine landscape). It teaches one thing about this shore: the same Posidonia meadow that keeps the water so clear is what throws up the 'banquettes' of dead leaves on the sand each winter — a sign of a healthy sea, not a dirty one. Signal Wild snorkelling cove adjoining the Domaine du Rayol's Baie du Figuier marine trail; steep unmarked access

[secret-gems]
Secret gems Where the locals actually go 2
  <card secret-gems:Domaine du Rayol — Le Jardin des Méditerranées> A ~20-hectare coastal estate whose 7 hectares of gardens, designed by landscape gardener Gilles Clément, recreate the landscapes of the world's Mediterranean-climate regions and their dry-climate kin — Chile, California, South Africa, south-west Australia, the Canaries and more — tumbling down the maquis slope to the sea, past a 1910 pergola and the Hôtel de la Mer. The story is the terroir. In 1908 Alfred Courmes, a Bormes man who made his fortune in the colonies, bought about 40 hectares of wild maquis and had architect Guillaume Tronchet build the villas, the ancient-style pergola (1910) and the Hôtel de la Mer (1912–19); aviation magnate Henry Potez took it over in 1940. When it was about to be carved up for a 284-flat seaside development at the turn of the 1980s, residents formed 'Les Amis du Rayol-Canadel' (1980) and fought — and the Conservatoire du littoral bought the 20-hectare estate in 1989. Clément's radical idea was to stop fighting the drought and instead garden with plants from every Mediterranean climate on Earth. In summer the estate also runs the sentier marin, a guided mask-and-fins swim in the Baie du Figuier billed as the garden's marine landscape. Signal Founded 1908 by Alfred Courmes; saved by 'Les Amis du Rayol-Canadel' (est. 1980) and acquired by the Conservatoire du littoral in 1989; garden by Gilles Clément
  <card secret-gems:Pardigon> A green amphitheatre of former farmland and wetland set back behind the beach, backed by the Maures foothills and threaded by the Carrade stream — grazing, reeds and, buried under the grass, two excavated Roman villas. It is the rarest thing on this coast: an undeveloped coastal plain in the middle of a summer-resort strip. It survived only after years of court fights — the Conseil d'État declared it a 'site remarquable du littoral', permanently non-buildable, which let the Conservatoire du littoral acquire it and make it inalienable in January 2011. Digs in the 1980s revealed two Roman wine estates straddling the Carrade, one on each commune. It's now being re-wilded: the dune cord rebuilt, the behind-dune wet meadows restored. Signal Made inalienable under the Conservatoire du littoral in January 2011 (declared a 'site remarquable du littoral'); Roman villas excavated in the 1980s

[nightlife]
After dark Where the night actually happens — and only where it honestly does 6
  <card nightlife:La Rhumerie> A rum-and-cocktail bar on the marina quay — a bamboo-and-timber terrace over the moored boats, rum-based cocktails, apéro sharing boards, a small dance floor, and band or DJ nights that run late. Cavalaire is a marina town, and after dark its energy collects in the narrow gap between the moored boats and the quay — La Rhumerie is where that energy lands. It is the closest thing the town has to a club, and the honest tell is that it is a bar: apéro-boards, rum cocktails, a dance floor the size of a dinghy. What sets it apart here is the calendar — it works a long February-to-November season when most of the town shutters from autumn to Easter, so it is the port's near-constant. For an actual nightclub you leave: 30–40 minutes to Les Caves du Roy at the Byblos in St-Tropez, or the Ramatuelle/Pampelonne beach clubs (Nikki Beach and neighbours). Cavalaire itself does not pretend to that. Signal Nouveau Port, lot 151; open ~10 Feb–10 Nov, daily ~9:00–03:00; confirmed on Golfe de Saint-Tropez Tourisme and Petit Futé, Yelp listing updated Feb 2026
  <card nightlife:Playa Mezcala> A feet-in-the-sand beach club near the Saint-Raphaël end of the bay — breakfast and lunch at the water's edge, a cocktail bar running through the day, tacos and pinsas, and, in high summer, dinner under the stars. Cavalaire exists as a resort because of one thing: a long, south-facing arc of sand, so the drink-with-a-view here happens on the beach, not on a rooftop — the town has none. Playa Mezcala grafts a Mexican-cantina conceit onto Var sand (the name, the mezcal, the tacos), exactly the borrowed-elsewhere styling you find all along this coast, even as the kitchen actually plays Mediterranean. Teach the register honestly: outside July and August the bar shuts around 19:00, so for most of the season this is apéro-and-sunset, not a late night; even in high summer it winds down at midnight over dinner, not at 2am. Come for the golden hour with your feet in the sand. Signal 822 avenue de Saint-Raphaël; beach club open daily ~8:30–19:00 (sunbeds and bar), dinner service to midnight only in July–August; official site playamezcala.com + Cavalaire tourism listing, active 2026
  <card nightlife:Bar de La Ménado> The outdoor cocktail bar of the beachfront 4-star Hôtel La Ménado — a quiet terrace a stone's throw from the water, serving classic and house-signature cocktails alongside evening sharing platters. This is the sunset-drink move, and its virtue is precisely what it is not: no DJ, no live band, no dance floor — just a terrace, a low sun on the water, and a barman's cocktail. In a town whose evening is modest by design, La Ménado is the calm end of the register, the anti-Rhumerie. The honest caveat is the calendar again: the bar opens only from 15 June to 15 September, so it is a high-summer address and nothing more — outside those three months this stretch of beachfront goes dark early. Signal 148 allée de la Plage; cocktail bar open 15 Jun–15 Sep per the hotel site (lamenado.com), 4-star hotel, beach 20 m away
  <card nightlife:Les Escales Musicales> The town's real summer live-music engine — a free, roving concert series (jazz, soul, funk, pop) that turns the quays, Place des Bains and the squares into open-air stages through the season. This is how a modest town does live music: not a fixed venue but a municipal series that moves, night to night, from the port to the squares — the actual answer to 'where's the live spot?' is 'wherever the Escale is this week.' It is the honest heart of Cavalaire's evening: free, outdoor, family-easy, and unmistakably seasonal, running only July into early September (it closes 1 Sept 2026 with PashGang on the Esplanade Sainte-Estelle). The bigger names — Hélène Ségara on 23 July, Julie Zenatti on 12 August — play the Sainte-Estelle esplanade the same summer, and those are free too; but the terroir-true thing is the roving programme on the quays. Signal Free itinerant concerts, Thu 2 Jul 2026 (The Color Gipsy, Place des Bains, 18:00) through 1 Sep (PashGang, Esplanade Sainte-Estelle); listed by the Ville de Cavalaire and Golfe de Saint-Tropez Tourisme
  <card nightlife:Festival Face à la Mer> A small free jazz, blues and world-music festival staged over a few September evenings on the seafront esplanade — literally programmed, as the name says, facing the Mediterranean, with the silhouette of the Îles d'Hyères on the horizon. The name is the whole idea: the stage looks straight out at the sea, so the horizon is the backdrop. What makes it worth timing a visit around is when it falls — early September, its fourth edition in 2026, once the August crush has drained away and Cavalaire is at its best and quietest. It is the shoulder-season send-off, and it teaches the real rhythm of this coast: the good nights here come after the crowds leave, not during them. Signal 4th edition, early September 2026, Esplanade Sainte-Estelle; free jazz/blues/world evenings; the 3rd edition ran 4–7 Sep 2025 (Ville de Cavalaire + Festivalenfrance listings)
  <card nightlife:Marina Viva Plage> A private-beach restaurant and cocktail bar on the sand, with signature drinks timed to sunset — and one of the addresses that throws a party on the two nights the town genuinely does. It earns its place for a specific, honest reason: on the two dates Cavalaire truly comes alive after dark — the Fête de la Musique on 21 June and Bastille Day on 14 July — the action is on the sand at the beach clubs, and Marina Viva is one that hosts it, staying open into the evening for the fireworks over the bay. That is the lesson about this town's nightlife in a sentence: it lives on the beach and on the calendar, not in a fixed club. Its bar otherwise winds down around 19:00, so any other night it is a sunset cocktail with your feet near the water — modest, seasonal, and none the worse for it. Signal Allée des Anthemis; private-beach restaurant + cocktail bar (creations by Thomas), season opens 24 Apr 2026; hosts a Fête de la Musique tapas-and-rosé evening (21 Jun) and a 14-Juillet fireworks evening per the venue site (marinavivaplage.fr)
