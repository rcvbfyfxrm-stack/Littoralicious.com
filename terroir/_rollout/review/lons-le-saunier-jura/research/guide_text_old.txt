THE NEIGHBORHOODS Where it lives Lons is small — everything is fifteen minutes on foot. The real Jura terroir, though, sits in a 50-km strip running north-east from Lons through Arbois to Port-Lesney and Dole, and the vines, the cheese caves, the Pasteur lab and the two-starred dining are all on it. Use Lons as the base; rent a car; drive the slope. Place de la Liberté & rue du Commerce — The historic heart — statue of Rouget de Lisle by Bartholdi (1882, the sculptor of the Statue of Liberty), 146 m of medieval golden-stone arcades on rue du Commerce (Franche-Comté's only arcaded street), Rouget de Lisle's birth house at no. 24, the Pâtisserie Pelen (1899). Place de la Comédie / Halles — The market square — Thursday and Saturday morning markets, the 1840 neoclassical theatre, restaurants La Comédie and Le Café de la Comédie. Where you lunch on market days. Quartier de la Chevalerie & Source Salée — Plane-tree park, 1900 bandstand, the Source Salée pool with brine still at ~300 g/L — the town's thermal past. The Thermes Lédonia spa next door. Place Perraud & rue Émile Monot — Quieter inner-centre streets — Ô Tablier , La Table de Perraud , the better Lons-proper tables. Montciel & Chille — Residential to the northeast — La Petite Auberge on route de Besançon (Narbey, soufflé glacé au Macvin) and Le Parenthèse spa hotel in Chille. Arbois (Jura wine capital, 15 km north) — Pasteur's town — the Maison de Louis Pasteur at 83 rue de Courcelles, Le Bistronôme (Bib Gourmand, Brousseau + Vuillemin, both Jeunet/Naessens alumni), Le Bistrot des Claquets (natural-wine cave à manger), La Finette (the canonical tavern since 1961). Arbois AOC: 15 May 1936. Pupillin & Château-Chalon — Two villages, two pilgrimages. Pupillin (the world capital of Ploussard, the Overnoy/Houillon shrine, and from March 2026 La Table du Grapiot ★ under chef Vivien Sonzogni). Château-Chalon (Vin Jaune only, ~50 ha, the Macle and Berthet-Bondet houses). Under 20 km of slope between them. Poligny (Comté capital, 25 km north) — The Maison du Comté (in its new building on the former municipal stadium site — not the old Avenue de la Résistance), the Caveau des Jacobins de Poligny (Fruitière Vinicole d'Arbois network), and the Comté affinage caves of the surrounding plateau. Out of town — Port-Lesney (30 km north, Maison Jeunet par Steven Naessens ★★ at the Château de Germigney), Sampans / Dole (40 km north, Château du Mont Joly ★ and La Chaumière ★), the Bresse (50 min west for Volaille Miéral), the Salines Royales d'Arc-et-Senans (UNESCO 1982 + 2009 Salins extension, 25 km east). The terroir is never centralised; you have to drive.

[history]
History & Terroir Where it comes from Neolithic salt → Rome Lons sits on a geological accident a hundred million years older than the Côte d'Or — the salt deposits beneath the Jura are Triassic, laid down 215–225 million years ago when shallow evaporating seas left thick beds of rock salt under what is now the Revermont. Pressurised brine pushes upward through faults; the three Lons springs — Lédonia, Chavenay and Naparix — emerge near-saturated at around 300 g of salt per litre . That salt has been worked here since the Neolithic (~4000–3000 BCE, ceramic and hearth evidence), continuously through the Gallo-Roman period when Jura salt was exported to Rome for meat-curing. The Lons salt-works ran until 1966; the Source Salée pool in the Parc des Bains is still accessible. East of town, on the Revermont plateau, the Saline Royale d'Arc-et-Senans (Claude-Nicolas Ledoux, construction 1775, operations 1779–1895, UNESCO 1982) received brine from the older Salins-les-Bains saltworks (UNESCO extension 2009) via the famous saumoduc — a 21.25-km wooden pipeline replaced with cast iron from 1782, losing roughly 30 % of its flow to leaks and smugglers. From the same salt-fed pasture come the Montbéliarde cows (95 % of the Comté herd; Simmental française the other 5 %) whose milk — 400 litres per wheel — becomes Comté AOP , France's number-one AOP cheese by volume, 61,000 tonnes in 2024. Some of it ages at Marcel Petite's Fort Saint-Antoine above Pontarlier, a 19th-century military fort (built 1879–1882) at 1,100 m altitude — Petite began renting it in 1966 to age cheese in cold, slow, mountain conditions (6–9 °C, 94–98 % humidity); roughly 100,000 wheels rest there at any time, 10–20 months on spruce shelves. Thirty kilometres east, the steep slope of Château-Chalon (about 50 ha across four communes: Château-Chalon, Ménétru-le-Vignoble, Domblans, Névy-sur-Seille) produces only Vin Jaune — Savagnin aged six years and three months under a yeast voile in barrels left unfilled. The voile is thinner than Sherry flor, lets a little oxygen through, and produces sotolon (3-hydroxy-4,5-dimethyl-2(5H)-furanone, the molecule first isolated from fenugreek in 1975) at concentrations of 120–268 µg/L — the same molecule in curry, maple syrup, walnut. The wine is bottled in the 62 cl clavelin : the volume that remains after the angel's share. In poor years, the growers' syndicate vote to forgo the appellation entirely — 1974, 1980, 1984 and 2001 were all declassified. Lons also gave the world La Vache qui Rit , founded in this town in 1921 by Léon Bel — Benjamin Rabier drew the laughing cow logo in 1923 — and Rouget de Lisle, born 24 rue du Commerce in 1760, who wrote the Marseillaise in Strasbourg in 1792 (Bartholdi's statue of him has stood on Place de la Liberté since 1882). Louis Pasteur grew up in Arbois from 1827; his celebrated 1857 Mémoire sur la fermentation appelée lactique was read at the Académie des Sciences on 30 November 1857 — not a doctoral thesis (his doctorate, 1847, was on crystallography), but the foundational paper that established fermentation is the work of living microorganisms. The Arbois laboratory at 83 rue de Courcelles is preserved as a Maison des Illustres , one of the best-preserved 19th-c. scientific workplaces in France. La Percée du Vin Jaune , founded by the vigneron Bernard Badoz in 1997, rotates through Jura wine villages on the first weekend of February to open the new vintage; the 2026 edition was hosted in Lons-le-Saunier on 31 January and 1 February 2026 and drew over 32,000 visitors — Europe's largest rural wine gathering.

What to Eat At table poularde au Vin Jaune Three truths beyond debate. Comté — not one cheese but a family: 12 months in cellar, milky and fruited; 18 months, fatty and nutty; 24 months, saltier and grainy; 36 months, dry and caramel, the tyrosine crystals crunch under the knife. Tasting three ages side by side is the basic education, and every serious fromagerie offers it. Saucisse de Morteau IGP (in a ring sealed with a wooden plug — the cheville , smoked 48 h to 7 days in a tuyé over conifer and juniper sawdust) and Saucisse de Montbéliard IGP (finer, more cumin-perfumed, the everyday version), with cancoillotte melted with a stroke of garlic and white wine — Haut-Jura peasant cooking is made of this. The poached Bresse poularde with Vin Jaune and morels is the cathedral dish, the plate you order to understand why the region exists: Vin Jaune's sotolon (120–268 µg/L of 3-hydroxy-4,5-dimethyl-2(5H)-furanone, the same molecule responsible for the aroma of fenugreek and maple syrup) binds to the free glutamate of dried morels and the fat of the blue-footed bird — pure chemistry. Drink it at Maison Jeunet par Steven Naessens (Port-Lesney, ★★) or at Château du Mont Joly (Sampans, ★ — Romuald Fassenet MOF 2004, 20 consecutive years of stars). At table in Lons proper: order it at La Petite Auberge (Véronique & Nicolas Narbey) on the route de Besançon. On the cheese course: Morbier AOP (the vegetable-ash line down the middle, vestige of the morning-and-evening curd join), Mont d'Or AOP (October to early May only, wrapped in Norway-spruce bark, baked in its box), and Bleu de Gex AOP — the first cow's-milk blue cheese to receive a French AOC, in 1977, with documented production back to the 13th century by the monks of the Abbey of Condat.

[drink]
What to Drink By the glass 6 yrs → 62cl clavelin The Jura carries five principal varieties (and two permitted accessories), not Bordeaux's count. Savagnin — the great white, parent of Sauvignon Blanc, Chenin Blanc and Grüner Veltliner (UC Davis / INRA DNA work, 1999), genetically identical to Traminer blanc; Gewürztraminer is its musqué mutation. Vinified two ways: ouillé (topped-up, fresh, mineral) or sous voile (oxidative, walnut-curry-honey). Chardonnay (brought from Burgundy in the Middle Ages), Pinot Noir , plus the two indigenous reds: Trousseau (firm, iron-tannin, the serious red) and Poulsard / Ploussard (translucent ruby, low tannin, almost a rosé in pale years — serve at 12–14 °C). Add Vin Jaune — Savagnin aged six years three months under voile in unfilled barrel, bottled in the 62 cl clavelin — and Vin de Paille (grapes raisined three months on straw, sweet, ~14.5° alcohol). Drink Macvin du Jura (the medieval mistelle, AOC since 14 November 1991, marc + must) as an apéritif, Crémant du Jura at any hour, Marc du Jura after. To taste the seven varieties side by side without driving, the Caveau des Jacobins in central Lons is the wine bar where the caviste actually explains. For Vin Jaune at the source — Domaine Macle (Château-Chalon, family since 1850, by appointment), Stéphane Tissot (Montigny-lès-Arsures, biodynamic Demeter since 2004, 50 ha across 30+ cuvées), Jean-François Ganevat (Rotalier, 14th-generation since 1650, sulfur-free since 2006, allocation-only), and Pierre Overnoy / Emmanuel Houillon (Pupillin, 6 ha, the no-sulfur Ploussard reference) are the four houses you learn first. For natural-wine pilgrims, Pupillin and Arbois are the world's destination.

[walk]
A WALK The route Leave Place de la Liberté at dawn, under Rouget de Lisle pointing his violin. Walk down rue du Commerce — Franche-Comté's only medieval arcaded street, 146 metres of golden stone — past the house where he was born (no. 24, plaque). Branch left into rue Saint-Désiré: at the 11th-century Romanesque church, ask the keeper to see the crypt — the Merovingian sarcophagus of Saint Désiré, 6th century. Out again, cross the Chevalerie park (old plane trees, 1900 bandstand), drop back via the Bains, walk past the Source Salée pool (still ~300 g/L salt), finish at the Place du 11 Novembre market . Forty minutes. The town reads through smells: wet stone, salt, leaven, Morteau smoke from the butcher's tuyé .

[avoid]
WHAT TO SKIP What to leave alone Never look for Jeunet at the old 9 rue de l'Hôtel de Ville address in Arbois — Jean-Paul Jeunet retired, and Steven Naessens took the name and the two stars to the Château de Germigney at Port-Lesney in November 2021. Don't drink "Vin Jaune" on a Place de la Liberté café terrace — if the bottle isn't a 62 cl clavelin , it isn't Vin Jaune; real Vin Jaune is tasted at the winemaker's, in Château-Chalon (15 km east). And ignore the myth that Pasteur's doctoral thesis was on Vin Jaune — his 1857 work was a Mémoire on lactic fermentation at the Académie des Sciences, the doctorate (1847) was on crystallography. The actual science is more beautiful than the myth.

Stories — tables with a story (incl. Hi-Fi) Some rooms are worth it for the lineage. The nearest credible options are far — Notre Dame Music Bar in Paris (4.5 h), Berlin's listening rooms (the longer drive). Le Bistrot des Marronniers on rue de Vallière is the Lyonnais-Jura bouchon hybrid since 1987 — wood-fired grill in the room, tablier de sapeur on the menu, pike-perch with Poulsard wine. La Table de Perraud does the pochouse jurassienne (the freshwater bouillabaisse, pike + perch + eel in white wine and cream). Local bistro (konobe / tascas / bistrots de marché) The Jura's bistrot-de-marché is to the region what the konoba is to Dalmatia and the bouchon to Burgundy: a short menu, a stone room, house wines, the braise that was started this morning. Every table named above is the real thing. To extend beyond Lons: the village auberges along the Revermont, the Comté-fueled bistrots of Poligny, and the natural-wine canteens of Arbois and Pupillin are the natural next step — and for the most fervent natural-wine drinkers, the unmarked tables that pour Overnoy by the glass and exist only by word of mouth.

The Dish Canon dishes 7 dishes Four hundred litres of milk for one wheel of Comté. The math is uncompromising. Two breeds only — 95 % Montbéliarde, 5 % Simmental française — each cow given at least 1.3 hectares of pasture. The milk goes to the fruitière (the village dairy), becomes the curd, becomes a 40-kg wheel. France made 61,000 tonnes (~1.6 million wheels) in 2024 — its number-one AOP cheese. The boldest of them age at Marcel Petite's Fort Saint-Antoine, a 19th-century military fort (1879–1882) at 1,100 m altitude above Pontarlier; Petite began renting it from 1966. Roughly 100,000 wheels sleep there at 6–9 °C and 94–98 % humidity, on spruce shelves, for 10 to 20 months.

Drink Wines, spirits, coffee 7 entries Sotolon — 3-hydroxy-4,5-dimethyl-2(5H)-furanone — is the molecule of Vin Jaune. It was first isolated from fenugreek seeds in 1975, named in 1980. By the end of the six-year-three-month voile élevage, Vin Jaune carries it at 120–268 µg/L (the analytical range across studies). The same molecule, at different concentrations, gives curry, fenugreek, lovage, maple syrup, aged rum and caramel their signature notes. When you recognise a Vin Jaune blind, it's sotolon you identify. Savagnin is the parent of Sauvignon Blanc, Chenin Blanc and Grüner Veltliner. UC Davis and INRA Montpellier DNA fingerprinting in 1999 (Bowers, Boursiquot, This, Meredith) confirmed all three parentages — Savagnin = Traminer blanc, the variety Pierre Galet identified with the South Tyrol village of Tramin (documented from ~AD 1000). Savagnin is also the grandparent of Cabernet Sauvignon via the Sauvignon Blanc × Cabernet Franc cross. Gewürztraminer is a musqué mutation of Savagnin rosé. The Jura's signature grape, planted on the blue marls of Château-Chalon, is one of the most genealogically central varieties in European viticulture.

[culture]
Culture Fixed stops 8 places La Vache qui Rit was invented here, in 1921. Léon Bel founded the Bel House in Lons-le-Saunier in 1921; the painter and illustrator Benjamin Rabier drew the laughing-cow logo in 1923. From a town of 17,000 people, one of the most internationally recognised food brands in the world. The Maison de la Vache qui Rit museum stands in Lons today as the city's quiet contribution to the global pantry. Pasteur's 1857 paper was on lactic fermentation — not Vin Jaune. Louis Pasteur (born Dole, 27 December 1822; family moved to Arbois 1827) defended his doctoral thesis in 1847 on the crystallography of tartaric acid — molecular chirality, not fermentation. The foundational fermentation paper is the Mémoire sur la fermentation appelée lactique , read at the Académie des Sciences on 30 November 1857 — proving lactic fermentation is the work of living microorganisms, not an abiotic chemical process. His later Arbois work on wine spoilage produced the pasteurisation technique that bears his name. The laboratory at 83 rue de Courcelles is preserved intact, a Maison des Illustres — one of the best-conserved 19th-c. scientific workplaces in France. Arbois was one of France's six first AOCs. The Journal Officiel of 15 May 1936 gazetted the first six appellations d'origine contrôlée under the 1935 law: Arbois, Cassis, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Monbazillac, Tavel, Vosne-Romanée. Arbois appeared first alphabetically — hence the local claim that the Jura wine appellation is the oldest in France. The reality is finer: it was one of six gazetted on the same day, and it happened to be first on the page. The honour is real either way.

Avoid Named traps — unambiguous 4 traps
  <card avoid:Looking for Jeunet at 9 rue de l'Hôtel de Ville in Arbois> The historic address has been closed since Jean-Paul Jeunet's retirement Jean-Paul Jeunet retired; Steven Naessens took the name and the two stars to the Château de Germigney in Port-Lesney in November 2021. The old Arbois address at 9 rue de l'Hôtel de Ville no longer operates as a starred restaurant. Many guides (including the previous version of this one) still send readers there. The current address is Maison Jeunet par Steven Naessens, 31 rue Edgar Faure, 39600 Port-Lesney .
  <card avoid:"Vin Jaune" tasting on a Place de la Liberté terrace> Real Vin Jaune is tasted at the producer's, not under the arcades If the bottle on the terrace is 75 cl, it isn't Vin Jaune — Vin Jaune is mandatorily 62 cl clavelin , the format is legally protected. Several centre-town "dégustation" cellars in Lons and Arbois pour topped-up oxidative Savagnin under the name and charge tourist prices. Real Vin Jaune is tasted at the winemaker's: Macle in Château-Chalon, Tissot in Montigny, or at the Caveau des Jacobins in Lons. The 62 cl clavelin is the proof of identity.
  <card avoid:Industrial "Comté" at a tourist cellar> Three ages side by side, or it's not the real lesson Cellars selling pre-cut Comté in plastic at €25/kg are selling young, mass-affined wheels. Real Comté differentiates radically by age and affineur — Marcel Petite's Fort Saint-Antoine wheels at 24+ months are a different cheese from a 12-month industrial wheel. Buy from a real fromager-affineur — Fromagerie Vagne on rue du Commerce, or at the Thursday market — and taste three ages side by side before deciding.
  <card avoid:"Pasteur defended his thesis on Vin Jaune"> Honest correction — the 1857 paper was on lactic fermentation A widely repeated story says Pasteur defended his doctoral thesis on the fermentation of Pupillin's Vin Jaune. The reality: his doctorate (1847) was on crystallography (tartaric-acid optical asymmetry). The 1857 work was a Mémoire sur la fermentation appelée lactique , read at the Académie des Sciences on 30 November 1857 — establishing that lactic fermentation is the work of living microorganisms. The Arbois work on wine spoilage that gave us pasteurisation came later. The myth blurs the timeline; the actual achievement is no less. Microbiology genuinely began at the foot of Jura barrels — just not as a doctoral thesis.
