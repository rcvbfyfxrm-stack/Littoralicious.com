THE SOUL OF THE PLACE What the place is, underneath The Var is the Cote d'Azur without the sunglasses — or rather, the half that never needed them. Wedged between glittering Nice and Monaco to the east and the Marseille conurbation to the west, it keeps the biggest forests in the region: the cork oak and chestnut of the Maures, the red porphyry of the Esterel. More than half the department is wooded, second in France only to the Landes. This is a coast that still works for a living. Its capital is not a resort but a warship town. Toulon is the largest naval base in the Mediterranean, home port of the carrier Charles de Gaulle and of most of the French fleet's tonnage. Offshore lie the Iles d'Hyeres — Porquerolles, Port-Cros, Le Levant — the "golden islands," much of them national park. Hyeres itself was the Riviera's first winter resort, all palms and Belle Epoque villas, decades before Cannes learned the trick. And yes, Saint-Tropez is here too, with its superyachts and its Club 55. But drive twenty minutes and you are in a vigneron's cellar or a fishing port where the cade is still baked in a copper pan. The Var wears both without apology. It also remembers August 1944, when the "second D-Day," Operation Dragoon, put half a million Allied troops ashore on these very beaches and freed Provence in under a fortnight. The quintessence The true ritual is the rose apero: late afternoon, the light going amber, a chilled bottle of Provence pink (or a serious Bandol) on a cafe table by the port at Sanary-sur-Mer or Le Brusc, a saucer of tapenade and olives, pastis for the older men, the pentaque balls clicking on the gravel. The morning version is the Provencal market — Sanary's is among the best on the coast — heaped with tomatoes, tapenade, cade and chickpea galettes. And to walk it off, the sentier du littoral, the old customs path first cut in 1791 against smugglers, threading the rocks cove by cove from Gigaro toward Cap Taillat.

[history]
THE STORY How it came to be The vine came by sea. Greek sailors from Phocaea, founders of Massalia (Marseille), brought viticulture to this coast in the 6th century BC, and the Romans later terraced the slopes above what is now Bandol and shipped the wine from its port. At Frejus they raised Forum Julii, founded around 49 BC by Julius Caesar as a counterweight to Greek Massalia; it was here that Octavian brought home the galleys captured from Mark Antony at Actium in 31 BC, and Augustus made it one of the three great naval bases of the western Mediterranean, then capital of Gallia Narbonensis in 22 BC. Toulon's destiny was sealed by its deep, sheltered roadstead. Henri IV founded the arsenal (a shipyard from 1599); Richelieu, then Colbert and Vauban, enlarged it into France's Mediterranean naval capital. Inland, the Maures lived for centuries on cork and chestnuts — the cork oak of the massif corked the bottles of the south — and on wine. Bandol was granted its own appellation in 1941, largely through the work of Lucien Peyraud of Domaine Tempier (a farm in his wife Lulu's family since 1834), who rebuilt the region around its old, tannic Mourvedre grape. Two later chapters define the modern Var. Hyeres and the Villa Noailles drew the interwar avant-garde — Man Ray, Bunuel, Giacometti — while nearby Sanary-sur-Mer became, after Hitler's rise in 1933, the self-styled "capital of German literature in exile": Thomas Mann, Brecht, Feuchtwanger, Werfel and Alma Mahler; Aldous Huxley had already written Brave New World there. Then, on 15 August 1944, Operation Dragoon landed between Cavalaire and Saint-Raphael and liberated Toulon and Marseille within two weeks — the invasion Provence, not Normandy, remembers as its own.

[why-now]
WHY NOW When to come It is high summer — the season the Var was built for and the one it can barely hold. The rose is being poured across a thousand terraces and the markets are at full tilt, while the great autumn set-piece, Les Voiles de Saint-Tropez (300 classic and modern yachts racing the bay), is still months off in late September. Come for the light and the sea, but know that the Saint-Tropez peninsula chokes in August; the reward goes to whoever slips to a working port like Sanary or Le Brusc, inland to the cellars, or onto a ferry to a car-free island.

[money-sits]
WHERE THE MONEY SITS What funds the table Three economies share this coast. The oldest and steadiest is the Navy: roughly 20,000 military and civilian staff work the Toulon arsenal, which holds more than 60% of the French fleet's tonnage — unglamorous, permanent money that has nothing to do with the season. The loudest is summer tourism and the superyacht trade: the Port de Saint-Tropez takes boats up to about 70 metres, a week's charter runs from roughly 30,000 euros to a million, and France drew over 100 million visitors in 2025, with the Mediterranean coast absorbing much of the August crush. The quiet fortune is wine. Provence makes roughly 40 to 45% of France's AOP rose and around 5% of the world's rose; Cotes de Provence alone is about 20,000 hectares, some 90% of it pink, and Sacha Lichine's Whispering Angel turned pale rose into a global luxury category now worth over a billion euros a year (Provence rose export value rose more than 1,000% between 2008 and 2017). That is where the markup lives — a fashionable pink can cost more than a red of real substance. The genuine value sits just to the west in Bandol: age-worthy Mourvedre reds, at least 18 months in old oak foudres, from Tempier or Pibarnon, still a fraction of a comparable Burgundy or Bordeaux. And Toulon, for all its lack of glamour, is where you eat and drink honestly for a third of a Saint-Tropez bill.

  <card dish:Grand aioli> Less a sauce than an occasion: a stiff, pungent emulsion of raw garlic, egg yolk and olive oil, whipped by hand and served with a mountain of poached salt cod, boiled vegetables, eggs and sea snails, dipped communally.   <card dish:Pieds et paquets> Sheep's trotters and little parcels of stuffed tripe stewed for most of a day in white wine and tomato — poverty cooking that wasted nothing, credited to Louis Ginouves around 1880. The Var eats it, but Marseille and Sisteron own it. Origin Marseille / Sisteron
  <card dish:Tarte tropezienne> A sugar-crusted brioche split and filled with two creams (pastry cream and buttercream), created in 1955 by Polish patissier Alexandre Micka in Saint-Tropez.   <card dish:Tapenade> The definitive rose-apero smear: black olives, capers (Provencal 'tapena,' hence the name) and anchovies pounded with olive oil. Invented in 1880 by chef Meynier at the restaurant La Maison Doree in Marseille — his original even carried tuna. Origin Marseille
  <card dish:The tuna (thon)> Mediterranean bluefin was once caught in madragues — vast net-mazes — off the Var coast from the 17th century; Brigitte Bardot's Saint-Tropez house, La Madrague, is named for one. [st-tropez]
Saint-Tropez — the scene & the legend How a fishing village became the glossiest summer address — and where the money parties now 10 places Saint-Tropez spent most of its life with its back to the world: a fortified fishing and naval village on the southern shore of its own gulf, hard to reach by land, named for an early Christian martyr, Torpes of Pisa, whose decapitated body legend says drifted ashore here in a boat. What broke the isolation was, first, a book and then a boat of a different kind. Guy de Maupassant's 1888 sailing memoir "Sur l'eau" lodged the place in the imagination of the Pointillist painter Paul Signac, who first sailed into the bay in 1892, fell for the light off the water, and settled — buying a house he called La Hune (a few years later, in 1897) and building a vast studio. He drew the others down: Henri Matisse spent the summer of 1904 working beside Signac and Henri-Edmond Cross and painted "Luxe, calme et volupté" here, its title lifted from Baudelaire. Signac bought the canvas; it is widely called the starting point of Fauvism, which makes this small port something like the birthplace of modern color. Charles Camoin and the avant-garde followed, and in the 1920s Colette bought a villa above the Baie des Canoubiers she named La Treille Muscate and wrote through the next decade there. The second rupture was cinema. In 1956 Roger Vadim shot "Et Dieu créa la femme" on these streets and beaches with his young wife Brigitte Bardot, filming in real locations rather than a studio. Overnight Bardot became a sex symbol and Saint-Tropez became shorthand for sun, sex and freedom; she bought the villa La Madrague in 1958 and the jet set came after her. The paparazzi came after them. By the time Louis de Funès was playing a hapless local gendarme in the 1960s, the myth was fully built. What was a canteen for a film crew is now one of the most expensive stretches of coast in France. In summer the gulf fills with superyachts moored stern-to along the old quay, the beach clubs of Pampelonne run daybed minimums and magnum service, and a village of a few thousand year-round residents swells with a crowd that comes to be seen. The light Signac chased is still free; almost nothing else in Saint-Tropez in August is. Saint-Tropez, in facts The painters and Fauvism Saint-Tropez is arguably where modern color was born. Paul Signac first sailed in during 1892 after reading Maupassant's sailing book 'Sur l'eau,' fell for the light and drew a colony of painters south (he bought his house La Hune a few years later, in 1897). In the summer of 1904 Matisse worked beside Signac and Henri-Edmond Cross and painted 'Luxe, calme et volupté' (title from Baudelaire) — the canvas Signac bought and that is widely called the starting point of Fauvism. The 1956 Bardot film Roger Vadim's 'Et Dieu créa la femme' (1956), shot on location in the village with his young wife Brigitte Bardot, turned a quiet port into the capital of jet-set glamour. Bardot became an international sex symbol and bought the villa La Madrague in 1958; the stars and the paparazzi followed her south. Les Voiles de Saint-Tropez regatta The great late-September/early-October classic-yacht regatta traces to 1981, when a friendly challenge — associated with Patrice de Colmont of Club 55 — pitted two boats, Ikra and Pride, in a race around the Nioulargo shoal to a finish at Pampelonne. Ikra won and took a silver platter and a free lunch at Club 55. Run from 1982 as 'La Nioulargue' until a fatal 1995 collision halted it, it was revived in 1999 as Les Voiles de Saint-Tropez. Place des Lices and pétanque The town's social heart is a sandy square shaded by rows of plane trees. It hosts the Tuesday and Saturday market, then turns back into a pétanque court where boules are still thrown in the afternoon — a scene painted by Signac, Matisse and Camoin from the terrace of the old Café des Arts. The gendarmes and Louis de Funès Louis de Funès played bumbling gendarme Ludovic Cruchot in 'Le Gendarme de Saint-Tropez' (1964), the top-grossing French film of that year (about 7.8 million admissions) and the first of six films in the saga. The town's real gendarmerie, in service from 1879 to 2003, reopened in 2016 as the Musée de la Gendarmerie et du Cinéma. The Tarte Tropézienne The town's signature cake — a sugar-crusted brioche split and filled with two creams — was created in 1955 by Polish-born pâtissier Alexandre Micka, who catered the 'Et Dieu créa la femme' shoot. Brigitte Bardot, eating them on set, is credited with suggesting the name 'tarte tropézienne'; Micka registered it as a trademark in 1973. A working fish market survives Beneath the glamour the port keeps a genuine covered fish market in the old town, tucked behind the Quai Jean Jaurès at the Place aux Herbes, where fishermen still sell the day's catch — sea bream, bass, red mullet, scorpion fish, octopus — laid out on marble slabs, and where the chefs of the expensive restaurants do their morning rounds. It trades mornings (roughly 8am–1pm); the tourist office lists Thursday to Saturday, with near-daily selling through high summer. La Ponche, the old fishing quarter Below the citadel, the original fishermen's quarter, La Ponche — its name from the Provençal 'la pouncho,' 'the point' — keeps cobbled lanes and colored facades far from the yacht crowd. Its Hôtel La Ponche grew from a 1930s fisherman's bar (where Bardot, Vadim and Romy Schneider once stayed) into a five-star hotel, restored in a 2021 renovation.
  <card st-tropez:Le Club 55> The founding legend of Pampelonne and the most famous beach club in the world. Bernard and Geneviève de Colmont built wooden cabins on the Ramatuelle sand in 1954; in 1955, seeing the family eating on the beach, a young Brigitte Bardot asked Geneviève to feed the crew of 'Et Dieu créa la femme.' [gastronomy]
The wines The appellations that define the coast 4 wines
  <card gastronomy:Bandol> The Var's glory and the reason to take the region seriously. Mourvedre-led (minimum 50%), the reds are aged at least 18 months in large old oak foudres — tannic and closed young, built to run for decades. AOC since 1941. Domaine Tempier (the Peyraud family; farm since 1834) and Chateau de Pibarnon (vines on hand-built restanque terraces in a natural amphitheatre of blue marl) are the references. The rose is serious too — the one Provence pink genuinely worth cellaring a year.
  <card gastronomy:Cotes de Provence> The workhorse and the volume: the largest Provencal appellation, roughly 20,000 hectares, about 90% of it rose (Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah, Rolle). Quality runs from industrial to genuinely fine. Its cooler inland sibling, Coteaux Varois en Provence around Brignoles, is often the better value.
  <card gastronomy:Cassis> An honest edge case — just over the Var line in Bouches-du-Rhone, east of Marseille, entirely inside the Calanques National Park. One of France's first AOCs (1936) and, rare for Provence, a white-wine appellation (Marsanne, Clairette): saline and mineral, made for the sea urchins and grilled fish under the cliffs. Included because the terroir belongs to this coast even if the postcode doesn't.
  <card gastronomy:Provence rose (the machine)> The caveat, told straight: Provence is the world's rose engine (about 40-45% of France's AOP rose, ~5% of global rose), and Sacha Lichine's Whispering Angel made pale pink a luxury category worth over a billion euros a year. Much of it is fashion, priced above reds of more substance. Drink it young, cold and local — but don't mistake the colour for the region's real depth, which is in Bandol.

[around]
Around — nature & villages When the town is done with you — the wild coast, the gorge, the village worth the drive 8 trips
  <card around:Gorges du Verdon & Lac de Sainte-Croix> Europe's grandest limestone canyon, dropping to nearly 700 m in places, opening onto a turquoise reservoir where you rent a kayak or pedalo and paddle into the gorge mouth. The colour isn't filtered — the Verdon runs milky jade from fluorine, microalgae and fine mineral particles held in suspension. Go weekday and early. The lake basin is man-made, filled 1973–74; it drowned old Les Salles-sur-Verdon, whose last houses were dynamited in March 1974.
  <card around:Moustiers-Sainte-Marie> A Plus Beau Village wedged into a cliff cleft, famous for tin-glazed faïence since the 17th century, with a gilded star strung on a chain between two rock faces. The star is the village's oldest legend — supposedly hung by a returning crusader as a votive offering. Pair it with the Verdon; the two are one outing. Faïence workshops still fire here.
  <card around:Porquerolles & Port-Cros (Îles d'Or)> Two of the Golden Islands: Porquerolles for white-sand coves, vineyards and the Fondation Carmignac art foundation; Port-Cros for wild trails and no cars. Port-Cros became France's (and Europe's) first marine national park in 1963 — snorkel its underwater trail. Porquerolles was a 1912 wedding gift from industrialist François Fournier to his wife; the state bought most of it in 1971, and its Conservatoire vineyards preserve heritage vines.
  <card around:Domaine du Rayol — Le Jardin des Méditerranées> A twenty-hectare seaside domain whose gardens landscape philosopher Gilles Clément replanted from 1989 with the floras of every Mediterranean-climate zone on earth — California, Chile, South Africa, South Australia — tumbling to a turquoise cove. Clément's 'garden in movement' made concrete: no clipped parterres, just curated wildness, managed ecologically. Owned by the Conservatoire du Littoral. The same coast road strings past Bormes-les-Mimosas and, further east, the hill villages of Gassin and Ramatuelle.
  <card around:Massif des Maures — Collobrières & Chartreuse de la Verne> A dark massif of cork oak and chestnut forest around Collobrières, the chestnut capital, with a Carthusian monastery, La Verne, hidden about 12 km deeper on a rough track. The Maures is geologically alien to Provence — ancient crystalline schist, not limestone, which is why chestnuts and cork oaks thrive. La Verne was founded by the Carthusians in 1170. Come for the Fête de la Châtaigne on three October Sundays (in 2026: the 11th, 18th and 25th); the marrons glacés are the real thing.
  <card around:Cotignac> A Plus Beau Village strung under an ~80-metre tuff cliff riddled with troglodyte dwellings and pigeonniers, ringed by vineyards and plane-shaded squares. The soft tufa rock let villagers carve homes and refuges straight into the cliff — some still accessible. A working wine village, not a museum; it only earned the Plus Beaux Villages label in October 2022. Time it with the market.
  <card around:Tourtour & Aups (Haut Var)> Tourtour, a Plus Beau Village and 'village in the sky' at 635 m with long views over the Haut-Var; nearby Aups, the black-truffle capital. Aups holds one of Provence's great truffle markets every Thursday morning from November to February — Tuber melanosporum, cash only, checked under the nose. Off-season it's a quiet stone village; the truffle is the reason to time it right.
  <card around:Bandol wine country — La Cadière-d'Azur & Domaine Tempier> Terraced Mourvèdre vineyards climbing between the hilltop villages of La Cadière-d'Azur and Le Castellet, home to Bandol's cult estate, Domaine Tempier. Bandol reds are built on Mourvèdre, a late-ripening grape that only fully works in this sun-trap amphitheatre facing the sea. Tempier — run by the Peyraud family since 1940, whose Lucien is called the 'spiritual father' of Bandol — is the name that made the appellation serious and age-worthy; tastings by appointment, so call ahead.

[eat]
What to Eat aioli → chestnut the canon Raw garlic pounded to a paste at noon by the sea; sweet chestnut roasting in the hills by October — the Var eats from the coast to the forest in a single day's drive. The coast's two pillars are garlic and slow fire. The grand aioli is less a sauce than a Friday occasion: a stiff, pungent emulsion of raw garlic, egg yolk and olive oil whipped by hand, served with a mountain of poached salt cod, boiled vegetables, eggs and little sea snails to dip communally. Its cold-weather twin is the daube provencale — beef braised for hours in red wine with orange peel and herbs, traditionally in a bulbous clay daubiere that rains the condensed steam back down onto the meat. Both are peasant cooking the coast simply never abandoned, and Toulon adds two of its own: the cade, a chickpea-flour galette baked thin over a wood fire, and the sugared, fried chichi fregi. The great beach sandwich, the pan bagnat, is worth ordering and worth one honest footnote: it is Nicois, not Var — pan banhat, 'bathed bread' in the old Nissart tongue, a whole salade nicoise of tuna, egg, anchovy, tomato and olive oil crushed into a round loaf until the bread drinks the oil. The catch that lets it belong on this coast at all is a matter of borders: the very line splitting the shore into the Var and the Alpes-Maritimes was only drawn in 1860, when Nice was annexed to France and the Var shed its eastern arrondissement — two neighbouring kitchens whose frontier is barely a century and a half old. Turn inland and the register goes sweet. Collobrieres, deep in the Maures, is the chestnut capital of Provence — some 900 hectares of groves around the village, part of a wider chestnut belt across the massif (La Garde-Freinet, Gonfaron, Les Mayons, Pignans) — feeding its marrons glaces (the Confiserie Azureenne has candied them there for over half a century) and a chestnut festival across three weekends each October. East of Toulon, Sollies-Pont calls itself the capital of the fig: the purple-black Bourjassotte Noire, granted protected origin as the Figue de Sollies AOP in 2006, with the Sollies basin alone growing well over half of France's fresh figs, sold dead-ripe from the vergers from late summer into October.

[drink-place]
What to Drink rose → Mourvedre in the glass The colour of a Var afternoon is pale pink; its real depth is almost black. Provence is the world's rose engine — roughly 40 to 45% of France's AOP rose — and the Var is its heartland. Cotes de Provence, the largest of the region's appellations at some 20,000 hectares and about 90% pink (Grenache, Cinsault, Syrah, Rolle), an AOC since 1977, is the wine of the apero: poured cold at sundown from a thousand terraces. Sacha Lichine's Whispering Angel, made at Chateau d'Esclans in the Var hills, turned pale pink into a global luxury category — drink it young, cold and local, but know that much of it is fashion priced above reds of more substance. The cooler inland Coteaux Varois around Brignoles is often the better value. The region's true glory is Bandol, granted its own AOC on 11 November 1941. The reds are led by Mourvedre — a legal minimum of 50%, usually far more — and aged at least eighteen months in large old oak foudres: tannic and closed young, built to run for decades. This is one of France's great age-worthy reds still selling for a fraction of a comparable Burgundy or Bordeaux, and the one Provence pink genuinely worth cellaring a year is a serious Bandol rose. The references sit in a tight arc of hills behind the bay: Domaine Tempier (the Peyraud family, farm since 1834), Chateau de Pibarnon (Mourvedre on limestone and marl above 300 metres, staring straight down at the bay of La Ciotat), and the organic Domaine de Terrebrune at Ollioules, whose reds are prized for their salinity. The ritual is patience: buy the flagship red and forget it for a decade, or pour the Bandol rose beside a whole grilled fish and drink the coast at its most serious.

[story-box]
1941 · the couple who saved a black grape The one story worth the detour story Before Lucien and Lulu Peyraud, Mourvedre was being torn out of Bandol for grapes that yielded more; after them, it became one of the great red wines of France. The bastide at Domaine Tempier had stood in the Tempier family since 1834, but the wine was ordinary and the future looked pinker. Across Bandol, growers were pulling up their old Mourvedre — low-yielding, slow, stubborn to ripen — and replanting with varieties that gave more juice for less trouble. When Lucie 'Lulu' Tempier married Lucien Peyraud in 1936 and her father handed the young couple the estate, Lucien made exactly the opposite bet. He was convinced Mourvedre was the historic grape of this coast and its salvation: slow to ripen but resistant to oxidation, capable of wines that could age for decades. He championed a protected appellation, and on 11 November 1941 Bandol was granted its AOC — with Mourvedre written into the rules. Lucien Peyraud is remembered as the father of the modern Bandol appellation; the appellation's insistence on Mourvedre, and its long rest in old oak foudres, is essentially his doing. The second chapter is a kitchen. The American food writer Richard Olney lived nearby and, in the 1970s, introduced the Peyrauds to a young California importer, Kermit Lynch — who has shipped Tempier to the United States since 1976 — and to Alice Waters of Chez Panisse. Lulu's unfussy Provencal cooking, laid out under the plane trees, became a touchstone of the American farm-to-table movement; Olney devoted a whole book to it, Lulu's Provencal Table, in 1994. Lulu Peyraud died in 2020, aged 102, having watched a grape she and her husband refused to give up become a benchmark of France. Go here Domaine Tempier — cellar-door tastings at the family estate at Le Plan-du-Castellet (commune of Le Castellet), in the Bandol AOC; visits by appointment, book ahead.

Curiosities The odd, the true, the only-here — facts to drop at dinner 12 notes Fig The only fig in France with its own appellation is the near-black Bourjassotte Noire of Sollies, northeast of Toulon (AOC 2006, AOP 2011), which accounts for roughly three-quarters of the country's commercial fig harvest. Cork The Massif des Maures is cork-oak country: the trees shrug off wildfire behind bark thick enough to spare the trunk, then resprout green from blackened limbs. Fritter In Toulon, Alexis Guglielmi claimed to have invented the chichi fregi, the orange-blossom chickpea-and-wheat fritter, and sold it on Cours Lafayette from 1907, though the treat appears in Provencal writing decades earlier; his family's stall finally closed in 2022. Water The Verdon runs an improbable turquoise, a colour born of microalgae and fluorine on the riverbed, and it named the river itself, from the Latin for green. Salt On the Giens double tombolo, a twin sand bridge so rare only a handful exist worldwide, the Pesquiers salt pans, worked from 1848 until 1996 and now a reserve, shelter pink flamingos, up to a couple of thousand by late summer. Mimosa Bormes only added -les-Mimosas to its name in 1968, honouring a flower that is not local at all: mimosa came from Australia and escaped Riviera gardens in the 1800s. Corso The oldest flower parade on the Cote d'Azur rolls through Bormes at the end of February, a corso that has run since 1920. Vine Porquerolles' vineyards, planted by Fournier after his 1912 purchase, were among the very first anywhere to be classed Cotes de Provence. Drydock Toulon's arsenal keeps drydocks built to swallow a supercarrier: the two largest run 422 metres by 40. Birds More than 320 bird species have been logged in the Hyeres salt marshes, the pink flamingo the headline act among them. The Golden Islands are named for a glint The Iles d'Or, Porquerolles, Port-Cros and Le Levant, earned their golden name in the Renaissance because the mica-schist in their rock flashes gold when the light catches it, not for any treasure. Most of the French Navy sleeps in Toulon The Arsenal de Toulon, built up into Europe's great Mediterranean naval yard under Louis XIV and fortified by Vauban, is the largest naval base in the Mediterranean and Western Europe, home to the carrier Charles de Gaulle and more than 60 percent of the fleet's tonnage.

What to drink — the pours What to actually order — the regional glass, and the growers to look for 7 pours
  <card drink:Bandol Rouge (Mourvèdre)> The one Var wine built to age like a serious red, because the appellation forces it to: at least 50% Mourvèdre, and eighteen months' ageing - much of it in old-oak foudre - before release. Late-ripening Mourvèdre only finishes fully in this sea-facing amphitheatre of limestone terraces (Pibarnon climbs to 300m), which is why no other French appellation is built around it. Young, it drinks of leather, garrigue and black olive more than fruit; decant it. Look for Domaine Tempier (Le Plan-du-Castellet), Château de Pibarnon (La Cadière-d'Azur), Château Pradeaux (Saint-Cyr-sur-Mer), Domaine du Gros'Noré (Le Castellet)
  <card drink:Bandol Rosé> Not the pale poolside kind. Tempier's rosé runs roughly 55% Mourvèdre - darker and structured, built for bouillabaisse rather than sunbathing. It was Domaine Tempier, carried to America by importer Kermit Lynch from the late 1970s, that taught drinkers a Provençal rosé could be a serious wine. Treat it like a light red: it takes fish soup, aïoli, even lamb. Look for Domaine Tempier, Château de Pibarnon, Domaine de la Bégude
  <card drink:Côtes de Provence Rosé> The everyday Var pour, and an industry: rosé is around 80% of the appellation's output, mostly Grenache-Cinsault-Syrah pressed pale and bone-dry. Drink the most recent vintage, cold and young - it is not built to keep, and last year's bottle is already tiring. Look for Château Minuty (Gassin), Domaines Ott – Clos Mireille (La Londe-les-Maures), Château Léoube (Bormes-les-Mimosas), Château de Berne (Lorgues)
  <card drink:Bellet (the Riviera neighbour)> Be honest - Bellet is not in the Var. It sits inside the city limits of Nice (Alpes-Maritimes), one of France's smallest AOCs at about 55 hectares, and it earns a place here because nothing else on the coast tastes like it. Whites are Rolle (Vermentino); reds and rosés lean on two near-endemic grapes, Braquet and Folle Noire (Fuella). Production is tiny and rarely leaves the region - a bottle to hunt, not to expect. Look for Château de Bellet, Clos Saint-Vincent, Château de Crémat
  <card drink:Pastis> The 6pm ritual, not a wine. Star anise, liquorice and garrigue herbs, cut roughly five parts cold water to one until it louches milky - water first, then ice, so the anethole doesn't clump. It is not made in the Var: Ricard is Marseille's child, created there in 1932 amid the wave of pastis that filled the void left by the 1915 absinthe ban. Reach for Henri Bardouin, built on more than sixty plants and spices, if you want to taste the herbs past the liquorice. Look for Ricard and Pastis 51 (Marseille), Henri Bardouin – Distilleries et Domaines de Provence (Forcalquier)
  <card drink:Fleur de Figue (fig liqueur)> The Var's other obsession is the fig - the violet Bourjassotte Noire of Solliès-Pont, France's only AOP fig. This liqueur, macerated and bottled in the Var at Taradeau, is September in a glass: a digestif, or splashed into cold rosé or sparkling wine as an apéritif. Real fruit, none of the synthetic fig-candy note. Look for Liquoristerie de Provence (Taradeau)
  <card drink:Liqueur de châtaigne / Crème de marrons (Collobrières)> Collobrières, deep in the Massif des Maures, is the chestnut capital of the coast, its wooded slopes long planted with châtaigneraies. The Confiserie Azuréenne - making marrons glacés since 1945 - turns the chestnut into everything, alcohol included, alongside its celebrated crème de marrons. Autumn drinking; the village chestnut festival fills the last three Sundays of October. Look for Confiserie Azuréenne (Collobrières)

[landmarks]
Landmarks & great buildings The must-sees, told with soul — not a postcard listing 6
  <card landmarks:Villa Noailles (Hyères)> Robert Mallet-Stevens's modernist villa in the hills above Hyères, begun in 1923 for arts patrons Charles and Marie-Laure de Noailles - the house that drew Man Ray, Buñuel and Giacometti into their circle. Now an art centre for design, architecture, fashion and photography, and each October home to the Hyères International Festival of fashion and photography. Signal Open Wednesday-Sunday, afternoons (roughly 2-7pm in summer, closing earlier off-season). Admission was introduced on 1 July 2025: a guided-tour visit runs about €10 (reduced €8). Confirm days, hours and prices at villanoailles.com.
  <card landmarks:Rade de Toulon (from Mont Faron)> One of Europe's great natural roadsteads and the home port of the French Mediterranean fleet, seen whole from the summit of Mont Faron (584m): the harbour, the naval base, the Massif de la Sainte-Baume beyond on a clear day. A memorial to the August 1944 Allied landings in Provence sits at the top. Signal Mont Faron téléphérique from its lower station on Boulevard Amiral Vence, a 6-minute ride, running most of the year but weather-dependent and often shut in high wind - check hours and price first. Alternatively, take a boat tour of the rade from the Quai Cronstadt on the old port.
  <card landmarks:Fort de Brégançon> An island fortress on a rocky islet off Bormes-les-Mimosas, fortified over the centuries, that has been the French President's holiday residence since 1968. Opened to guided visits in 2014. Signal Guided tour only, about three hours on foot, booked ahead through the Bormes-les-Mimosas tourist office (no tickets on site); adult €12 (reduced €10). Limited dates in season, groups capped at 20, steep and not suited to reduced mobility - and it closes for presidential use, so confirm it is actually running.
  <card landmarks:Chartreuse de la Verne> A Carthusian monastery founded around 1170, isolated deep in the Massif des Maures and built largely from the local greenish serpentine stone. Listed as a historic monument since 1921; today home to the contemplative Sisters of Bethlehem. Signal Open most of the year, self-guided with a booklet: 11am-6pm June-August, 11am-5pm the rest of the season; closed in January and on religious holidays; adult €7. From about 21 June to 20 September the forest road (RD214) up to it can shut for fire risk - check with the Collobrières tourist office before driving. Silence is requested; dress modestly.
  <card landmarks:Citadelle de Saint-Tropez> The hexagonal hilltop keep above the port, built 1602-08, now the Musée d'histoire maritime telling St-Tropez's sea story from the Bailli de Suffren to the sardine fleet. The finest panorama over the gulf and the Maures. Signal Open daily 10am-6.30pm (Apr-Sep), 10am-5.30pm (Oct-Mar); adult a few euros. Climb up through the old town; the ramparts and the view reward the walk as much as the museum.
  <card landmarks:Église Notre-Dame-de-l'Assomption (Saint-Tropez)> The Italian-baroque parish church whose ochre-and-sienna bell tower is the town's emblem on every postcard. Inside sits the gilded bust of Saint Torpes, carried through the streets each May during the Bravade. Signal Free, open in daytime outside services, in the old town just below the citadelle (coordinates approximate). Come mid-May (16-18 May) for the Bravade if you want the church alive rather than a quiet photo stop.

[culture]
Culture, art &amp; the beautiful Beauty with a backstory — the rooms and buildings worth the detour 4
  <card culture:Villa Noailles> Robert Mallet-Stevens' 1920s modernist villa above Hyères, now an art centre, with a triangular Cubist garden and two summer festivals born inside it. Charles and Marie-Laure de Noailles, the most reckless art patrons of their day, handed the young Robert Mallet-Stevens a hillside and asked for 'an interesting little house to live in.' In successive campaigns from 1923 to 1933 he answered with a white, cubic manifesto of the Modern Movement: indoor pool, gymnasium, squash court, and a triangular Cubist garden built in 1926-27 by Gabriel Guévrékian, once crowned by Jacques Lipchitz's bronze 'La Joie de Vivre,' which rotated on its base every four minutes. The couple bankrolled the avant-garde with the same open hand: Buñuel's scandalous L'Age d'Or and Man Ray's 'Les Mystères du Château du Dé' (shot here in 1929) were their money. Hyères took the house over in 1973; today it hosts the two events it spawned, the Hyères International Festival of Fashion & Photography (founded 1985 by Jean-Pierre Blanc, then 21, and now the oldest young-designer fashion competition in the world) and Design Parade. The one real thing to carry away: this is where modernism learned it was allowed to be joyful, not just severe. Signal Built in campaigns 1923-1933 by Robert Mallet-Stevens; municipal art centre since 1973; Design Parade held 25-28 June 2026
  <card culture:Fondation Carmignac (Villa Carmignac)> A contemporary-art foundation buried under a restored Provençal farmhouse on a car-free national-park island, walked barefoot. Financier and collector Édouard Carmignac sank a 2,000 m² gallery beneath an old mas and a ceiling of water on the protected island of Porquerolles, opening it in 2018. You leave your shoes at the door and cross the galleries barefoot before spilling into a fifteen-hectare garden studded with sculpture. Because Porquerolles is a national park, you arrive only by boat and there are no cars, so the art is framed by umbrella pines, salt air and heat-shimmer instead of white walls. Verified: the 2026 show 'Sea, Pop & Sun' (curated by Dieter Buchhart and Anna Karina Hofbauer, setting Warhol, Lichtenstein and Martial Raysse against living artists) runs 25 April to 1 November. The real thing to know: it borrows its title from a summer mood and is engineered to feel like a daydream rather than a museum. Signal Opened 2018 by Édouard Carmignac; 2026 exhibition 25 Apr-1 Nov
  <card culture:Domaine Tempier> The estate that made Bandol serious wine, and whose rosé is one of the Mediterranean's genuinely beautiful drinks. This is the terroir story of the Var itself. The Tempier family had held the domaine since 1834; in 1936 Lucie Tempier married Lucien Peyraud and her father gave the couple the estate, which they took over around 1940. Together they fought to win Bandol its own appellation (granted 11 November 1941) and, on 27 October 1943, bottled the estate's first wine, a rosé, instead of selling in bulk. Bandol is mourvèdre country, sun-baked, saline, structured, and Tempier proved it could age for decades. Lucie 'Lulu' Peyraud (1917-2020) was also a self-taught cook whose long feasts became legend; the American writer Richard Olney gave her a whole book, 'Lulu's Provençal Table' (1994), and her disciples Alice Waters of Chez Panisse and importer Kermit Lynch carried her name across the world. The real thing: the pale, mineral Bandol rosé you drink up and down this coast exists in its modern form largely because of one family in the hills above Bandol. Signal Estate in the Tempier family since 1834; Peyrauds drove Bandol AOC (1941); first bottled wine (rosé) 1943; Lulu Peyraud 1917-2020
  <card culture:La Tarte Tropézienne (Alexandre Micka)> The cream-filled brioche invented and still sold here, the pastry Bardot named. In 1945 a Polish confectioner named Alexandre Micka settled in Saint-Tropez, bringing a family recipe for a sugar-crusted brioche split and filled with two creams. In 1955, catering the shoot of 'And God Created Woman,' he served it to Brigitte Bardot, who suggested he call it 'la tarte de Saint-Tropez'; Micka kept the idea and trademarked the name Tarte Tropézienne in 1973. His original shop still trades under that name. Not a drink, but the definitive edible souvenir of the peninsula and the perfect foil to a glass of cold rosé. The real thing: the town's most famous dessert was christened by an accident of cinema, and the woman who named it never took a centime for it. Signal Micka settled 1945; tarte created 1955; named by Brigitte Bardot; trademarked 1973; original shop still open

[hotel-bars]
A drink in the grand hotels The beautiful hotel bars — worth it for one drink 6
  <card hotel-bars:Les Caves du Roy — Hôtel Byblos> The legendary cellar nightclub-bar inside the Byblos, open only in season. The whole hotel is a love letter to a muse: Lebanese businessman Jean-Prosper Gay-Para built the Byblos as a 'palace of a thousand and one nights' inspired by Brigitte Bardot, a faux-Provençal hamlet in apricot and salmon stucco inaugurated on 27 May 1967 with godmothers Bardot and Mireille Darc and three days of festivities for more than 700 guests. Its cellar-bar, Les Caves du Roy, has been the 'temple of Tropezian nights' since July 1967, with Juliette Gréco, Françoise Sagan and Jacques Chazot at the opening and Mick and Bianca Jagger's wedding celebrated here in 1971. Gay-Para lost the hotel within months, selling to Sylvain Floirat on 19 September 1967 after the Six-Day War pulled him back to Beirut; the Floirat family has kept the flame for over fifty years. The real thing: the legend is deliberately rationed, weekends only in spring and autumn and nightly only across high summer, roughly late June to end of August. Signal Byblos inaugurated 27 May 1967; Caves du Roy open since July 1967; seasonal, nightly late Jun-late Aug (2026)
  <card hotel-bars:Bar de La Ponche> The bar of a discreet 5-star hotel in the old fishermen's quarter where the Tropezian myth actually began. Before Saint-Tropez was Saint-Tropez, La Ponche was a fishermen's inn in the eponymous old quarter, run for generations by the Duckstein family. In 1949 Boris Vian annexed the neighbouring barn to make a bar, and through the 1950s it filled with the New Wave: Bardot's laughter, Vian's trumpet, Vadim shooting scenes for 'And God Created Woman' a few metres down the lane, Sagan, Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir drifting through. Reopened as a year-round five-star in 2021 after an eight-month renovation redesigned by Fabrizio Casiraghi, it kept its low-key soul. The real thing: this shadowed corner above the little port, not the superyacht quay, is where the whole Tropezian legend was actually lit. Signal Bar created 1949 by Boris Vian; reopened year-round 5-star 2021, redesign by Fabrizio Casiraghi
  <card hotel-bars:Terrasse de la Villa Belrose> A panoramic terrace bar high in the Gassin hills for a glass of rosé over the whole bay. The genius of the Althoff Villa Belrose is altitude and distance: it sits five minutes but a whole world away from the Saint-Tropez crush, on a Gassin hillside where the terrace hangs above the roadstead and the pool. You come here not for a scene but for the oldest ritual on this coast, a cold glass of Provence rosé while the light goes long over the gulf and the yachts below shrink to toys. The gastronomy carries a real name, chef Jimmy Coutel's 'Le Belrose' (Coutel has led the kitchen since 2022), but the terrace apéritif is the point. The real thing: this is a Leading Hotel of the World that trades entirely on the view, and it closes for winter, so the drink is a summer-season pleasure. Signal Leading Hotels of the World; reopened 24 April 2026 for the season, closes for winter; chef Jimmy Coutel since 2022
  <card hotel-bars:Bar du Sube> A first-floor balcony bar with the oldest terrace in town, looking straight down the working port. The Sube is the anti-Byblos: a modest, centuries-old three-star hotel right on the quay whose first-floor balcony is said to be the oldest in Saint-Tropez, and whose bar is a front-row seat on the endless choreography of yachts and sailing boats coming and going. Its five small terrace tables are the most fought-over in town. Climb higher and there is an English bar of mahogany and model boats, with a fireplace, subdued light and Art Deco panelling, a sailors' snug that nods to the town's 'Les Voiles' regatta rather than any nightclub. The real thing: for the price of one drink you get the single best free theatre on the port, from a perch that predates every palace on the peninsula. Signal Historic 3-star port hotel; oldest balcony in town over the harbour; mahogany English/Art Deco bar above
  <card hotel-bars:Bar Le Soleil d'Eau — Château de la Messardière> A terrace aperitif at a fairy-tale hilltop château-palace above the town. The Messardière is a 19th-century folly with turrets, domes and arcades, built as a wedding gift from cognac merchant Gabriel Dupuy d'Angeac to his daughter Louise and her husband, cavalry officer Henry Brisson de La Messardière, and perched in a 25-acre park of umbrella pines and cypress above Saint-Tropez. It joined the Airelles collection in 2019 and reopened as a palace in July 2021; the drink to take is on the terrace of the Bar Le Soleil d'Eau, aperitif in hand, with the Gulf of Saint-Tropez laid out below and the pine-resin heat all around. The real thing: this is the largest hotel in Saint-Tropez, yet its pleasure is the oldest and simplest, height, shade, and a slow glass while the bay turns gold. Signal 19th-century château (wedding gift, Dupuy d'Angeac family); Airelles Collection since 2019, reopened as palace July 2021
  <card hotel-bars:Sénéquier> The red-chaired café on the quay, an open-air observatory of the port since the age of the New Wave. Sénéquier began as a patisserie founded in 1887 by Marie and Martin Sénéquier, pastry-makers already famous for a white nougat of Spanish almonds, Sicilian pistachios and Provence honey. In 1930 Lisette Sénéquier turned it into a café and tea room on the harbour, and its unmistakable bright-red tables and chairs became the front row for the Nouvelle Vague and everyone after. Bombed in 1944 and rebuilt by 1951, its thirty-metre terrace of director's chairs and triangular tables is still the most conspicuous seat on the quay. The real thing: order a drink here and you are paying for a sightline, not the glass, it is the most famous café on the Riviera precisely because it faces the whole promenade of the port. Signal Patisserie founded 1887 (Marie & Martin Sénéquier); harbour café from 1930; rebuilt 1951; landmark red terrace

[walks]
Walks &amp; the coast on foot The best of the place, slowly 3
  <card walks:Sentier Blanc-Martel> The Verdon's flagship hike: a roughly 16 km one-way descent into Europe's deepest river canyon, with steel ladders and a long unlit tunnel of around 670 m (bring a torch), from La Maline down to the Couloir Samson. A navette shuttle links the two trailheads so you don't double back. Named for the two men behind the first full traverse of the canyon in August 1905: Paris speleologist Edouard-Alfred Martel, sent by the Ministry of Agriculture to survey drinking water for Provence (the Fontaine-l'Eveque spring), and Isidore Blanc, the naturalist schoolmaster of Rougon who guided him. The Touring Club de France engineered the trail proper, ladders at the Breche Imbert and all, between 1926 and 1928; Blanc, made a TCF delegate, worked it up until his accidental death in 1933, and the path was renamed Blanc-Martel in his honour in 2005. Honesty by negation: the path itself runs on the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence bank, not the Var; the Var owns the south rim (the Corniche Sublime belvederes above Aiguines). The wilder Imbut/Styx trail on the Var bank has been closed by municipal order since July 2022 for rockfall, so this is the canyon walk that is actually open. Signal First full canyon traverse by Martel and Blanc, August 1905; trail engineered by the Touring Club de France 1926-1928; renamed Blanc-Martel in 2005.
  <card walks:Presqu'ile de Giens, double tombolo & Salins des Pesquiers> A peninsula lashed to the coast by two parallel sand bars, one of only a handful of double tombolos on Earth (three to five worldwide), enclosing old salt pans; the sentier du littoral rounds the wild, wind-hammered west and south shores. The salt works, run by the Compagnie des Salins du Midi, closed in 1995; the Conservatoire du littoral now owns the marsh (since 2001), a roost for pink flamingos year-round that peaks around 2,000 birds in late summer. The coast path you walk is the old sentier des douaniers, the early-19th-century customs path along which patrols once watched the shore for contraband, and it is the very right-of-way that a 1976 French law fixed nationwide as a public easement (a 3 m passage across private coastal land), later reinforced by the 1986 loi Littoral. Signal Salt production ceased 1995; marsh Conservatoire-owned since 2001, flamingos year-round; path is the old sentier des douaniers, and the 3 m coastal easement was set by a 1976 law (loi Littoral 1986).
  <card walks:Collobrieres> A stone village on a chestnut-shaded river deep inside the Maures, ringed by roughly 2,000 hectares of chestnut groves; the trailhead for the best Maures walks (up to La Verne, up to Notre-Dame-des-Anges) and the self-declared capital of the chestnut. The sweet chestnut is the Maures' terroir tree, and the Confiserie Azureenne has candied its marrons in the village for over half a century, roughly seven days steeping in vanilla-scented syrup to make a single marron glace, while the village throws its chestnut festival across three Sundays each October. One real thing to carry away: the marron is a selected large-fruited variety of the wild chataigne, not a separate nut. Signal Chestnut festival held three Sundays each October; Confiserie Azureenne candying marrons here 50-plus years.

[calas]
Beaches &amp; calas The coves worth the walk 2
  <card calas:Plage Notre-Dame, Porquerolles> 800 m of pale sand backed by Aleppo pines that run almost to the water, a 30-min walk or bike from the car-free village, and angled so it shelters from both the Mistral and the east wind. The whole island was a wedding gift. On 22 February 1912 Francois-Joseph Fournier, a Belgian boatman's son who made a gold fortune on the Veta Verde lode in Mexico, bought nearly all of the island's 1,254 hectares at auction for his wife Sylvia, then planted the eucalyptus, mimosa and umbrella pines that still shade the paths. European Best Destinations voted Notre-Dame the most beautiful beach in Europe in 2015, and it has paid for the fame: a 6,000-visitor daily summer cap (in place since 2021) and compulsory ferry booking now guard the freshwater table and the island's rich flora. Signal Island bought 22 Feb 1912 by Fournier; named Europe's most beautiful beach 2015; 6,000/day summer quota since 2021.
  <card calas:Cap Taillat> A wild sand isthmus tying a scrub islet to the mainland, reached on 2.5 km of coastal path through maquis, with a Neolithic dolmen (the Dolmen de Briande, excavated 1935, the southernmost in the Var) on the ridge above and swimmable coves on either flank of the tombolo. It is wilderness today only because a Club Med was stopped here: a company bought 27 hectares in 1970 to build 400 bungalows and a marina, Ramatuelle's mayor called in the Conservatoire du littoral, and it bought Cap Taillat in 1987. A fire on 24 July 2017 burned some 550 hectares across Taillat and neighbouring Cap Lardier to ash; the cistus and heather maquis has been regrowing since, which is why the vegetation looks so young. Signal 27 ha bought for a 400-bungalow Club Med in 1970; saved by the Conservatoire du littoral (Cap Taillat acquired 1987); 24 July 2017 fire burned ~550 ha.

[secret-gems]
Secret gems Where the locals actually go 4
  <card secret-gems:Sentier sous-marin de la Palud, Port-Cros> A 40-45 min walk from the harbour to the Plage de la Palud, then a free, marked snorkel loop (mask, fins, tuba) past six submerged interpretation buoys in a few metres of water, among grouper, wrasse and posidonia meadow. Port-Cros became the first marine national park in Europe by decree of 14 December 1963, born of a donation rather than a purchase: the Henrys had run the island since 1921, and Marceline Henry gave a third of it (213 hectares of the western island) to the State on condition it be protected, with Andre Malraux carrying it through. The underwater trail here, laid in 1979, was the first in France. The real lesson: on this island you read the sea on foot, not just the land, and the fish are unafraid because nobody has speared them in over 60 years. Signal National park by decree of 14 Dec 1963 (first marine park in Europe); underwater trail opened 1979 (first in France).
  <card secret-gems:Chartreuse de la Verne> A huge, near-black monastery built of green Maures serpentine, hidden on a forested ridge above the chestnut woods and reached only by a rough road; a working silent cloister with a partial visitor route. Founded around 1170 by the bishops of Toulon (Pierre Isnard) and Frejus (Fredol d'Anduze); the first Romanesque church was consecrated on 3 October 1174, pillaged by the lords of Bormes in 1416, and emptied when the Carthusians left for Italy at the Revolution (its property confiscated by decree of 2 November 1789). The green serpentine framing its portals was quarried nearby in the Maures and hauled up by mule, the local stone made monumental. Silent again since 1983, when the monastic Family of Bethlehem moved in. Honesty: much is enclosed, so you visit the edges of a living silence, not a museum. Signal Founded c.1170, church consecrated 1174, pillaged by the lords of Bormes 1416; monastic Family of Bethlehem in residence since 1983.
  <card secret-gems:Village des Tortues, Carnoules> A 2-hectare sanctuary and veterinary clinic at the foot of the massif where you walk among Hermann's tortoises: rescue, breeding pens, incubation and a release programme. The Maures and the neighbouring Plaine des Maures hold the last wild population of Hermann's tortoise on the French mainland. Naturalists Bernard Devaux and David Stubbs founded the SOPTOM association in 1986 and opened the first village at Gonfaron on 28 May 1988; it moved to its current Carnoules site in June 2017. The one real thing it teaches: France's only native land tortoise survives essentially here, and its two great killers are summer wildfire and the roads that cut its range. Signal SOPTOM founded 1986; village opened 28 May 1988 (Gonfaron); relocated to Carnoules June 2017.
  <card secret-gems:Domaine du Rayol, Le Jardin des Mediterranees> 20 hectares of protected coast (7 of garden, 13 of maquis) facing the Port-Cros islands, with a grand antique-style pergola and cypress-lined stair down to the sea and, in summer, a guided snorkel trail in the bay. The Conservatoire du littoral rescued the abandoned estate in 1989 and handed it to landscape gardener Gilles Clement, who refused a manicured garden and instead planted a mosaic of the world's other Mediterranean climates: South African fynbos, Chilean matorral, Californian chaparral, Australian kwongan and mallee, all thriving under the same Var sun. The monumental pergola was raised around 1910 for the estate's first owner, the Parisian businessman Alfred Courmes; the aviator Henri Potez, who bought the domain in 1940, later drove the long cypress-lined stair down through it. Terroir made literal, and gardened without irrigation or pesticides from the start. Signal Pergola built c.1910 for Alfred Courmes; estate bought by the Conservatoire du littoral 1989; garden conceived by Gilles Clement.
