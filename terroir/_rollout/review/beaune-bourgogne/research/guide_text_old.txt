THE NEIGHBORHOODS Where it lives Beaune is a ring. The ramparts draw an almost unbroken circle barely 700 metres across, and everything of consequence — every cellar, every table, the market, the Hôtel-Dieu — sits inside it or in a faubourg just outside a gate. You walk it in fifteen minutes; you read it in three. Place Carnot & Place de la Halle — The commercial heart, two linked squares: Place Carnot for the cafés, the brocante and Le Carmin; Place de la Halle for the covered market and the Hôtel-Dieu one block on. Mulot & Petitjean's pain d'épices and the Marché aux Vins both open onto this space. Rue Maufoux & the cellar streets — The wine-trade spine running south-west — rue Maufoux, rue Paradis, rue d'Enfer — lined with négociant doorways and the vaults beneath. Loiseau des Vignes, Bissoh and the Bistro de l'Hôtel are all on or off Maufoux. Place Madeleine & Faubourg Madeleine — The eastern square and its faubourg — the after-dark side of town, wine bars and the natural-wine bistros. Caves Madeleine and Le Comptoir des Tontons sit here, away from the monument crowds. Place Monge — A quieter square that is, quietly, the charcutier quarter: Raillard and Franck Bourgeon within a few doors, the jambon persillé and pâté-en-croûte address of Beaune, plus the Bistrot Bourguignon and Pâtisserie Berteau. Faubourg Bretonnière — The mustard axis, running west out of the old Porte Bretonnière — terraced houses, the Bastion Bretonnière, and at number 31 the Edmond Fallot mill, Burgundy's last independent family moutarderie. The vineyard slope — West of the ramparts the premier crus begin within 200 metres of the last street — Clos des Mouches, Les Grèves, Les Teurons climbing toward the Montagne de Beaune. No other wine capital lets you walk from a café into a grand vineyard in five minutes. Out of town — The Route des Grands Crus runs south through Pommard, Volnay and Meursault and north to Savigny, Pernand-Vergelesses and Aloxe-Corton — all within 12 km. Levernois, with its Relais & Châteaux table, is five minutes east. The terroir is never centralised; you have to drive.

[history]
History & Terroir Where it comes from monks → climats Beaune sits on a geological accident. About 30 million years ago the rising Alps caused the crust east of here to drop along the Bressan rift , foundering the Saône plain and tilting the Jurassic limestone of the Côte to the morning sun — a 60 km escarpment of marl and brown limestone, east-facing, perfectly drained, the whole reason wine is here at all. The Côte de Beaune, the southern half, lies on Upper Jurassic stone (Bathonian–Oxfordian, 150–165 million years) and gives the world's great dry whites (Meursault, Puligny-Montrachet, Corton-Charlemagne); the Côte de Nuits to the north stands on older Middle Jurassic rock and holds 24 of Burgundy's 33 red grand crus. Two grapes do all the work — Pinot Noir and Chardonnay — and in 1999 John Bowers and Carole Meredith at UC Davis (the same team that proved Zinfandel was Croatian two years later) showed by DNA, in Science , that Chardonnay, Aligoté, Gamay, Melon de Bourgogne, Auxerrois and a dozen more Northeastern French varieties are all crosses of Pinot Noir × Gouais Blanc : a single peasant grape, Gouais, fathered Burgundy's whole white catalogue. The earliest Burgundian Pinot Noir of which we have a record was planted by Benedictine monks at what is now Château de Pommard in 909 CE , nearly two centuries before Cîteaux. The Cistercians of Cîteaux (Abbey founded 1098, 30 km north) assembled the Clos de Vougeot from 1109–1115 onward and spent four hundred years tasting each parcel and naming it — the climats system was an invention of patient monastic palates, two centuries before the printing press. The Dukes of Burgundy made Beaune the trade capital of the Côte from the 14th century, when Burgundy stretched from Mâcon to the North Sea (Bruges, Ghent and Antwerp were Burgundian); in 1443 Nicolas Rolin, chancellor to Philip the Good, with his wife Guigone de Salins, founded the Hôtel-Dieu as a free hospital for the poor — and from the first gift of vines in 1457 onward it treated patients without charge for 528 years , until 1971, the bill always paid by the vineyard. Beaune also gave France one of the founders of the École Polytechnique : Gaspard Monge (1746–1818), the local merchant's son who invented descriptive geometry, served as Napoleon's Minister of the Marine, and helped found the school in 1794. Burgundian cooking comes straight from this geography: limestone slopes too poor for wheat, rich pasture for Charolais cattle on the plain — hence the slow braises ( œufs en meurette , bœuf bourguignon , coq au vin ) built on the village red that wasn't going to sell as cru. The Hospices estate today: 60 hectares, 85 % premier and grand cru, sold every third Sunday of November en primeur , by candlelight — first held in 1859, the same year Auguste-Denis Lagoute's Crème de Cassis de Dijon was given its official Dijon medal — run with Sotheby's since 2021. 166th edition: Sunday 15 November 2026.

[eat]
What to Eat At table wine → the sauce Three pillars. Œufs en meurette — eggs poached in a reduction of Pinot Noir, with lardons, button mushrooms and a garlic-rubbed croûton: the dish where the appellation IS the sauce, the cathedral plate of Beaune, eaten at breakfast or lunch but built on a great red. Bœuf bourguignon — its long-cooked sibling, beef shoulder or cheek browned then braised four hours in red until the sauce turns to lacquer; built, like every great Burgundian dish, on the village wine that wasn't going to sell as cru. Coq au vin — old rooster (tougher, deeper-flavoured than young hen), braised in red with lardons and small onions, the sauce sometimes finished with the bird's own blood; a peasant economy made noble by hours. On charcuterie: jambon persillé (Easter Burgundian terrine — shoulder ham simmered with tarragon and white wine, hand-torn, set in parsley jelly) — Eddy Raillard 's on rue Monge is called by critic Gilles Pudlowski "a model of the genre". Cheese course: Époisses , washed-rind cow's milk from the village 86 km north-west, brined and bathed in marc de Bourgogne — said to have been Napoleon's, and historically banned from French TGV trains for its smell. Open every cellar visit with gougères — warm Gruyère choux puffs that are not a snack but a ritual, the savoury bite that resets the palate before the first wine. The escargots are Helix pomatia , scarce and seasonal; if they arrive frozen in industrial garlic butter, they aren't escargots de Bourgogne .

[drink]
What to Drink By the glass 42 1ers, 0 grands Two grapes and a UC Davis pedigree. Pinot Noir — the red of the entire Côte d'Or, the same vine giving a different wine every hundred metres of slope; the marker grape of the climat system. Chardonnay — the white that became the world benchmark in Meursault and Puligny-Montrachet, a few kilometres south of town; Beaune's own celebrated white is the Clos des Mouches , an entire 25.2 ha premier cru owned by Joseph Drouhin. Aligoté — the sharp third white, base of the Kir (one part crème de cassis under four to five of cold Aligoté, named for Félix Kir, Resistance priest and mayor of Dijon 1945–1968, who served it at every reception); the 1999 UC Davis DNA paper (Bowers & Meredith, Science ) showed Aligoté — like Chardonnay and Gamay — is a cross of Pinot Noir × Gouais Blanc , the medieval peasant grape Philip the Bold of Burgundy banned from the Côte in 1395 to favour Pinot. Drink without driving at the Marché aux Vins (5 wines, 25 €) and at the natural-wine counter Enstase . Cellars to book: Bouchard inside the 15th-c. Château de Beaune — their flagship is the Vigne de l'Enfant Jésus , a 3.92 ha parcel inside Beaune Les Grèves once owned by the Carmelite convent of Beaune; it was named in gratitude after the 1638 birth of Louis XIV, foretold to the supposedly sterile Anne d'Autriche by the Carmelite nun Marguerite du Saint-Sacrement, and bought by Bouchard at the 1791 sale of biens nationaux , in monopoly ever since. And Patriarche , five kilometres of galleries under a former Visitandine convent — about two million bottles, the largest cellars in Burgundy. Apéritif: Kir, or Kir Royale on Crémant de Bourgogne base (méthode traditionnelle, AOC since 1975); Crème de cassis de Dijon itself was first commercialised in 1841 by Auguste-Denis Lagoute, from the Noir de Bourgogne blackcurrant. Digestif: Marc de Bourgogne (distilled from the pressed grape skins) or Fine de Bourgogne (distilled from wine lees, oak-aged 3+ years) — Marc is an AOC since 1942 (the oldest pomace-brandy AOC in France), Fine joined the club in March 2011.

[walk]
A WALK The route Leave the Place de la Halle early and walk the ring. The Hôtel-Dieu's glazed roof catches first; cut west through the cellar streets — rue Paradis, then rue d'Enfer, Hell Street, so named because the wine vaults beneath keep it cold even in July, the venting slats breathing cool air at your ankles. Reach the ramparts at the Rempart des Dames, a double avenue of plane trees on a 1637 bastion, the prettiest stretch of a 2.5-kilometre circuit that never lost its towers. Drop down through the Faubourg Bretonnière past Edmond Fallot 's mustard mill, then climb out of town: two hundred metres past the last house the vines begin, and you are in the premier crus — Clos des Mouches, then Les Grèves on the upper slope. Forty minutes, and the town reads through smell: cold cellar stone, crushed mustard seed, box hedge, and the green sap of a vineyard in flower.

[avoid]
WHAT TO SKIP What to leave alone Do not drink Burgundy by the glass on a Place Carnot café terrace and think you have met the place — the terraces pour the négoce's most anonymous cuvées at a tourist markup, and the wine that explains the slope is tasted three streets away, in a cellar, with someone who made it. Skip the "Burgundy snail" menus around the Hôtel-Dieu that serve frozen helix from elsewhere in industrial garlic butter; real escargots de Bourgogne are scarce and seasonal, and an honest bouchon will tell you so. And do not come for the day on the third Sunday of November expecting Beaune to itself: that is the Vente des Vins , the town's loudest weekend of the year — magnificent, but the opposite of quiet.

Tables — by tier Best · Recognized · Stories (incl. Hi-Fi) · Authentic · Local-bistro 5 tiers The Best Beaune carries two Michelin stars inside the walls, and they read the town from opposite ends. Le Carmin (Place Carnot, chef Christophe Quéant) is the instinctive one — a market menu with no fixed signature, lunch from 50 €, dinner to 178 €. Clos du Cèdre (chef Jordan Billan, in the Hostellerie Cèdre) is the terroir-anchored one, starred since 2022, with a list past a thousand references. Five minutes east, in the village of Levernois, La Table de Levernois holds a third star under Philippe Augé — the grand-gesture option, Relais & Châteaux, a vegetable garden in the grounds. Recognized — tier guide Below the stars sit the tables the guides keep flagging. La Table du Square took a Bib Gourmand in 2024 — the best craft-for-money in town, a market menu rewritten fortnightly. Loiseau des Vignes , the Bernard Loiseau group's bistro, pours 32 Burgundies by the glass. An honest flag: Le Bénaton, on Faubourg Bretonnière, held a star from 2006 until it was withdrawn in the 2024 guide — chef Keishi Sugimura cooks to win it back, and the room is still worth the table; confirm its current standing before you book. Stories — tables with a story (incl. Hi-Fi) Some rooms are worth it for the building. Bouchard pours inside a fortress built for Louis XI; Marché aux Vins tastes you through the chapels of a deconsecrated Franciscan church; Garum — the table that was the one-star Jardin des Remparts until its chef gave the star back — now runs a looser room of charcuterie, oysters and natural wine at the same address by the Hôtel-Dieu. One honest note for the listening-bar traveller: Beaune has no audiophile listening bar in the jazz-kissa sense, and neither does Dijon or Lyon. The nearest are in Paris (Bambino, Listener). The Beaune equivalent ritual is a cellar at dusk and a glass that someone can talk you through — and for an electronic night, drive 45 min to La Vapeur , Dijon's SMAC, or 1h45 to Le Sucre in Lyon. Authentic — local soul The Burgundian soul tables are the bouchons. Ma Cuisine , down a passage, has been the reference since 1996 — à la carte only, a cellar that goes very deep. Le Bistrot Bourguignon on rue Monge does the canon straight — œufs en meurette, bœuf bourguignon — for a 33 € menu. Caves Madeleine seats you at shared tables and lets you pull wine off the shelf. Le Conty, in a 16th-century room on rue Félix Ziem, holds the same line. Local bistro (konobe / tascas / bistrots de marché) The bouchon is to Burgundy what the konoba is to Dalmatia and the bistrot de marché to the Jura: a short menu, a stone room, house wines by the pot, the braise that was started this morning. Every table named above is the real thing. To go further out, the village tables of the Route des Grands Crus — Pommard, Volnay, Meursault — are the natural next step, and the natural-wine counter at Enstase is where the town's own young drinkers end the night.

The Dish Canon dishes 7 dishes The Burgundian canon was invented to use what wouldn't sell. Œufs en meurette, bœuf bourguignon, coq au vin — the great braises of Burgundy were peasant recipes built on the everyday village red and the tougher cuts (or the old rooster, finished laying), redeemed by hours of cooking. Cru wine never has to do this. The lesson is general: every appellation's table dishes were once a way of using up the appellation's wine — and a Côte d'Or village cook will still ask which bottle you opened last night before deciding what to braise tonight.

Drink Wines, spirits, coffee 7 entries The Burgundian whites all descend from a single peasant grape. In 1999 John Bowers and Carole Meredith published in Science the DNA proof — the same UC Davis team that traced Zinfandel back to Croatia two years later — that Chardonnay, Aligoté, Gamay, Melon de Bourgogne, Auxerrois and a dozen other Northeastern French varieties are all crosses of Pinot Noir × Gouais Blanc . Gouais was a medieval workhorse grape so disdained that Philip the Bold of Burgundy banned Gamay (one of its offspring) from the Côte in 1395 to protect Pinot — not knowing he was banning its half-cousin. The greatest white-wine catalogue in the world descends from a vine the dukes called common. Pinot can turn white inside a single vine. In 1936 Henri Gouges of Nuits-Saint-Georges noticed a vine in his Clos des Porrets-Saint-Georges Premier Cru producing white berries after véraison — a somatic mutation on a Pinot Noir. He propagated the cuttings and after the war planted half a hectare. The mutation was registered as Pinot Gouges , and Domaine Henri Gouges still bottles a Nuits-Saint-Georges 1er Cru Les Perrières from it — Pinot's white sibling, unique to a single Burgundian family.

[culture]
Culture Fixed stops 7 places The Hôtel-Dieu treated patients, free, for 528 years. From 1443 to 1971 the hospital never charged the poor a centime — the bill was paid, the whole time, by vines given to it by the dying. It still owns 60 hectares of Côte d'Or vineyard, and the November auction of its wine still helps fund the modern hospital that replaced it. Cinema's grandfather was born in Beaune. Étienne-Jules Marey (5 March 1830 – 15 May 1904), born to a Beaune wine-merchant family, invented the fusil photographique in 1882 — a rifle-shaped single camera firing 12 frames a second onto a rotating glass-plate cylinder to record a flying bird or a running body as separate images on one plate. In October 1888 he demonstrated the first sensitised paper strip-film at the Académie des Sciences, running at 20 images per second — the first "film" in history by material definition. The Lumière brothers' cinématographe (1894–95) was built on Marey's principles. The Musée Marey, in Beaune's bastioned Porte Marie de Bourgogne, keeps the originals. Beaune produced one of the founders of the École Polytechnique. Gaspard Monge (1746–1818), born in Beaune to a local merchant and schooled by the Oratorians, drew as a teenager such a precise large-scale plan of the town with instruments he had built himself that the Royal Engineering School at Mézières recruited him on sight. He invented descriptive geometry, served as Napoleon's Minister of the Marine, and co-founded the École Polytechnique in 1794 alongside Lazare Carnot. The town's library bears his name; the Marey-Monge family of Château de Pommard descends from his daughter.

Avoid Named traps — unambiguous 4 traps
  <card avoid:Burgundy by the glass on a tourist terrace> Place Carnot café terraces — the négoce's most anonymous cuvée at a markup The big café terraces pour the most generic regional Bourgogne by the glass at a tourist price, and call it the experience. The wine that explains the slope is tasted in a cellar, with someone who made or chose it — at Marché aux Vins , at Enstase , or on a négociant visit. Use the terrace for a coffee and the view, not for your impression of Burgundy.
  <card avoid:The frozen-snail tourist menu> The 'menu escargots' clustered around the Hôtel-Dieu Several menus around the monuments sell frozen helix from elsewhere, drowned in industrial garlic butter, as 'escargots de Bourgogne'. True local Burgundy snails are scarce and seasonal. An honest bouchon — Ma Cuisine , Le Conty — will tell you straight which it is serving. Ask the question.
  <card avoid:Arriving for a quiet day on auction Sunday> The third Sunday of November — the Vente des Vins If you want Beaune calm, do not come on the third Sunday of November. That is the Vente des Vins weekend — the town's loudest, with the auction in the Halles, projections on the monuments, crowds in every street. Magnificent, but the opposite of the quiet this guide is built around. Come for the auction deliberately, or avoid that weekend deliberately.
  <card avoid:Driving the Route des Grands Crus and tasting> Cellar visits, a car, and France's drink-drive limit It is tempting to drive Pommard–Volnay–Meursault and taste at each. France's limit is low and the gendarmerie know the season. Either walk the vineyard route, hire a driver or a cycling tour, or appoint one taster who stays dry. The wine will keep; the licence might not.
