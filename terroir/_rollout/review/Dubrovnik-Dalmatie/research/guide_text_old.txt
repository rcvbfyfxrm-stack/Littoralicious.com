THE NEIGHBORHOODS Where it lives Dubrovnik fits in a 1,940 m ring of walls, then expands by sea: Lokrum off the Old Port; Gruž and Lapad on the western peninsulas; the Elaphiti and Pelješac by tender or by car. Time the visits by tide of cruise ships, not by clock. Old Town (Stari Grad) — The 1,940 m walled republic — Stradun runs Pile to Ploče, 300 m of polished limestone. The five key squares: Pile (Onofrio's fountain), Luža (Sponza Palace + St Blaise + the Bell Tower), Gundulić (morning market), Poljana Boškovića (Jesuit Stairs + Kopun ), and Bunić (the old port view). Walk before 09:00 or after 18:30 — cruise hours are the rest. Ploče — East of the Old Town outside Ploče Gate — Lazareti (the 16th-c. plague quarantine, now Linđo Folklore Ensemble's summer venue), Banje beach, and the old quarantine lanes that climb to Sv. Jakov. Pizzeria Tabasco is up Hvarska; the Argentina and Excelsior hotels look back at the walls from this side. Pile — West of the Old Town just outside Pile Gate — the working land entrance, with the former Maritime School (now Nautika ), the Lovrijenac access path, and Konzum supermarket on Gundulićeva Poljana for sane-priced provisioning. Tender drops at the Old Port (Porporela) land you here in 4 minutes by foot. Gruž — The yacht harbour and the working market town. ACI Marina Dubrovnik and the cruise terminal share the bay; the morning fish-and-produce Gruž Market on Obala Stjepana Radića is the closest fresh-fish source to a moored yacht. Boats land 06:30; fish gone by 10:00. Bring cash. Lapad — Peninsula 10 minutes west of Gruž, residential and quietly local — Lapad Bay, Sunset Beach, the long pedestrianised Kralja Tomislava strip. Konoba Atlantic Kitchen and a handful of family konobas are where city professionals eat when the Old Town is full of cruise traffic. Two beaches; one decent supermarket; no walls. Lokrum — Tiny island 600 m off the Old Port. 12-minute ferry (Easter to November). Benedictine abbey founded 1023; Archduke Maximilian's villa and Botanical Garden; the dead sea "Mrtvo More" salt lake; nudist beach at Skalice. No restaurants — picnic instead. UNESCO buffer zone of the Old Town. The tourist Iron Throne replica sits in the monastery cloister, Game of Thrones tribute. Pelješac peninsula — 70 km of vineyards, oysters and Mali Ston walls. The south slope between Trstenik and Podobuče is Dingač (1961, first Croatian appellation). Mali Ston Bay is the European-flat oyster PDO. Ston walls (begun 1333, 5.5 km — longest in Europe still standing) and the salt pans. Day trip from Dubrovnik: 50 min to Mali Ston by road; by sea ~2 h 15 to the same point. Konavle — The rural valley + coast south of the city to the Montenegrin border. Cavtat (Roman Epidaurus) is the anchor town; the cold river Ljuta feeds watermills (Konavoski Dvori); Sokol Grad fortress in Dunave village commands the karst hinterland from a 25 m vertical rock. The Easter procession in traditional Konavle dress is the cultural event of the spring. Olive oil and pršut country. Elaphiti islands — Three inhabited islands 30 minutes by ferry from Gruž — Koločep (car-free, the smallest), Lopud (best sand beach: Šunj), and Šipan (largest at 15.8 km², two harbours, the most konobas). Greek elaphos = deer; Pliny the Elder named them so in the 1st c. CE. Day-trip standard from Dubrovnik. Korčula (for Pošip & Grk) — Not Dubrovnik proper — but the wine reason to extend a Dalmatian trip. Smokvica and Čara are the Pošip villages; Lumbarda on the eastern tip is the only Grk village in the world. Korčula Town itself is a small Dubrovnik in miniature (Venetian fortified, mediaeval, the alleged birthplace of Marco Polo). Cavtat — Roman Epidaurus , ancient predecessor of Dubrovnik. 17 km south of the city, easy harbour to drop tender into. Stone-paved waterfront, the Račić mausoleum by Ivan Meštrović (1922) on the peninsula. Quieter, family-warm konobas; the airport (DBV) is 5 km away. Easter-procession anchor; good provisioning for asparagus, lemon, fig.

[history]
History & Terroir Where it comes from Ragusa → 3 rebuilds The city was founded in the 7th century by Greco-Roman refugees from Cavtat (Epidaurus) , who fled Slav and Avar raids and built on the rocky islet of Ragusa. By 1358 the Treaty of Zadar released the city from Venetian rule into a four-and-a-half-century de facto independence — the Republic of Ragusa — under nominal Hungarian, then Ottoman suzerainty, with diplomatic offices in every Mediterranean capital and a merchant fleet of ~200 ships at peak. The constitution was designed to defeat personal ambition: a Senate of 45 patricians over 40 years old, elected for one year by the Major Council (~300 nobles aged 18+); daily government by the seven-member Minor Council; a Rector who served exactly one month , eligible again only after two years, and locked inside the Rector's Palace during his term. On 6 April 1667, around 08:45 , an earthquake estimated at magnitude ~7 (intensity X EMS) flattened most of the medieval city in 8–15 seconds. Historian Robin Harris counts ~2,000 dead inside the walls and ~1,000 across the rest of the Republic; older sources cite 5,000 total. The sitting Rector and half the Major Council died with the city. Rebuilding was directed from Rome by Stjepan Gradić (1613–1683), the Vatican librarian and Ragusan diplomat who fundraised across Europe and imposed a uniform Baroque rhythm along the Stradun — the reason every façade rises to the same cornice and the windows align across 300 metres of polished limestone. The Republic ended in 1808 when Napoleon's Marshal Auguste de Marmont abolished the Senate; the city passed to the French Illyrian Provinces and then to Habsburg Austria from 1815. The next defining trauma was the Siege of Dubrovnik, 1 October 1991 – late May 1992 — nine months of Yugoslav People's Army and Montenegrin reservist bombardment. The single day of 6 December 1991 killed 19 civilians and wounded 60; over the siege ~116 civilians and 194 Croatian defenders died, and 11,425 buildings across the region were damaged. UNESCO placed the site on the List of World Heritage in Danger and led the post-war restoration. The Memorial Room of the Defenders inside the Sponza Palace (built 1516–1522) is where the city remembers, quietly, with photographs of the dead. The terroir of Dubrovnik is what you taste after three rebuilds: Roman, Baroque, post-1995.

[eat]
What to Eat At table dirty pasta → a badge The Dubrovnik canon is short, specific, and tied to feast days the city actually still keeps. Šporki makaruli — "dirty macaroni" — is the pasta you eat on or around 3 February (St Blaise Day) , when ~19 Old Town kitchens run a unified menu of the dish: hand-cut macaroni in a deep slow-cooked beef/veal ragù with bay and cloves. The name comes from the patrician practice of pouring leftover sauce over pasta for the servants after the nobles had eaten the meat. Pašticada is the other long-haul dish: beef topside, larded with pancetta and cloves, marinated 24–48 hours in vinegar and Prošek (the Dalmatian dessert wine — not Italian Prosecco; this confusion is the Apulian-trade-mark fight that Croatia partially lost in 2022), then braised four to six hours with prunes and dried figs. Always with njoki (gnocchi), full stop. At Mali Ston the noble flat oyster Ostrea edulis — peak March to May, opening day 19 March (St Joseph) — is farmed by EU PDO since the 2020 regulation, the only farmed Ostrea edulis PDO in Europe and ~90% of Croatian oyster production. Eat them at the boat with lemon and Pošip, not in the Old Town an hour and a half later. The companion shellfish is the Mali Ston mussel (Mytilus galloprovincialis), served na buzaru. Beyond shellfish: crni rižot (cuttlefish-ink risotto, not a Venetian copy — independent Adriatic tradition); brodet (fish stew with at least three species, never stirred, always shaken, served with polenta and a glass of red); and from the Konavle hinterland, janjetina ispod peke — lamb cooked three hours under the iron bell over embers, ordered three hours ahead at Konoba Dubrava in Bosanka. The strange dish is stonska torta : cooked macaroni layered with ground walnuts, grated dark chocolate, lemon zest, cinnamon and sugar, wrapped in shortcrust — tradition links it to the building of the 5.5 km Ston walls (begun 1333), high-density worker rations that became a wedding cake. The winter dish is zelena menestra , documented in Dubrovnik writings since 1480: cabbage, smoked pork, dried sheep (kaštradina), sausage and potato in the meat broth, three regional sub-versions (City, Župska, Primorska). And the city's confection is not the Sicilian rice ball but arancini — candied bitter-orange peel, the oranges arrived from Arabia in the 10th c., peel boiled, dried, candied, rolled in sugar.

[drink]
What to Drink By the glass Plavac → Zinfandel The grape is Plavac Mali ("little blue") and the story is genetic. In 1998 Prof. Carole Meredith at UC Davis , with vine cuttings collected and shipped by Miljenko "Mike" Grgich himself, proved that Plavac Mali is the offspring of Crljenak Kaštelanski × Dobričić — and that Crljenak Kaštelanski is genetically identical to Zinfandel (California) and Primitivo (Apulia). So Croatia is the grandparent of California Zinfandel, and Plavac Mali is its Dalmatian child. The appellations: Dingač (declared 1961 — the first protected wine appellation in former Yugoslavia and modern Croatia, ~45° south-facing slopes between Trstenik and Podobuče on Pelješac, where the grapes are sun-cooked three times — direct, off the sea, off the white stones); Postup (1967, adjacent, slightly less extreme); and Komarna (2012, mainland, organic-certified). The producers a chef should know in 2026: Grgić Vina (Trstenik, founded 1996 — Mike Grgich's homecoming after he made the 1973 Chateau Montelena Chardonnay that won the Paris 1976 tasting; two wines, Plavac Mali and Pošip); Miloš Stagnum (Ponikve, 15 ha organic, wild yeast, three years in old Slavonian oak — Hugh Johnson called the 1993 Stagnum Croatia's first cult wine); Bura-Mrgudić (Potomje, family lineage in Dingač documented since 1410); Madirazza (Potomje, 20+ ha Dingač and Postup); Saints Hills (Oskorušno, consulting oenologist Michel Rolland — Sv. Lucija Dingač and Sv. Roko Komarna are the flagships); and the historic Dingač Vinarija cooperative (founded 1961, the standard-bearer label of the appellation). The whites worth the boat trip: Pošip from Smokvica and Čara on Korčula (first documented vine 1864; PGI 1967, the first protected white in Croatia), and Grk — "the Greek" — grown only in Lumbarda on Korčula's eastern tip, planted by Greek colonists from Issa ~2,400 years ago, vines female-only, must be co-planted with Plavac to fruit. The spirits: Maraschino , clear cherry liqueur from the Marasca cherry of the Zadar hinterland — Dominican friars perfected it in the 16th c., the Venetian merchant Francesco Drioli industrialized it in 1759, and the liqueur was on the first-class drinks list of the Titanic. Travarica (herb rakija, sage and immortelle), Loza (straight grape rakija), Orahovac (green-walnut liqueur picked around St John's Day, 24 June, steeped 40 days), and the Dubrovnik-specific Rozalin — homemade rose-petal liqueur that scents the local Rožata caramel and makes it taste of Dubrovnik rather than anywhere else.

[avoid]
WHAT TO SKIP What to leave alone Three rules and a curse word. One — the cruise-day crush is real: when three or more ships dock at Gruž the Old Town absorbs 8,000–10,000 day-trippers in a four-hour window. Check the Dubrovnik Port Authority schedule before sending the principal ashore, and either tender to Porporela before 09:00 or skip the Old Town entirely on those days. Two — Prijeko Street is the historic tourist-trap row: menus in eight languages, touts on the street, frozen fish. Pick the side lanes (Antuninska, Boškovićeva, Palmotićeva) for the honest konobas. Three — oysters belong at Mali Ston, not in the Old Town. They travel 1 h 30 by road and may be three days old by the time they reach a plate inside the walls. The curse word: truffle. Truffle is an Istrian product, not a Dubrovnik one — if a menu pushes it, you are reading a tourist trap. Add to the list: don't drive into the Old Town (pedestrian zone, you will lose 90 min parking); don't walk the walls between 11:00 and 17:00 in July or August (stone reads 38–42 °C, no shade on the second half); and don't buy bottled water in Stradun cafés (3–5× supermarket markup — Konzum on Gundulićeva Poljana is the sane stop).

Bring a clean basket, ice, and small bills. The Bota Šare farm has a fixed dock stall most mornings; ask at the restaurant if no one is at the quay. PDO 2020/1469 Ostrea edulis at zero kilometres. Address Mali Ston waterfront, Pelješac
  <card gastronomy:Grgić Vina — Trstenik, Pelješac> Mike Grgich's homecoming winery. Two wines only, every year: Plavac Mali (multi-vintage Dingač-style) and Pošip (Korčula). Tasting room at Trstenik on the south shore of Pelješac. Mike Grgich died 2023; daughter Violet and nephew Ivo Jeramaz run the estate. Tasting EUR 25–45 pp by appointment.
  <card gastronomy:Miloš Stagnum — Ponikve, Pelješac> Frano Miloš at Ponikve, on south-facing 150–250 m slopes. 15 ha organic, wild-yeast fermentation, three years in old Slavonian oak. Hugh Johnson called the 1993 Stagnum "Croatia's first cult wine." Frano personally pours; appointment only. The Stagnum top cuvée is the wine to bring back to the boat.
  <card gastronomy:Mali Ston Bay oyster farms (collective)> The oyster collective of the Mali Ston Bay — small family farms (Bota Šare, Pelješki Sušac, Drače) holding the PDO since the 6 October 2020 EU registration. Peak season March to May; opening day St Joseph 19 March. About 90% of Croatian oyster production. Buy at quay or at the restaurants — the cost-of-living index for the bay is the per-oyster price (EUR 2.50–4 dockside).
  <card gastronomy:Saints Hills — Oskorušno, Pelješac + Komarna> The Tolj family estate with the Rolland consulting badge. Flagships: Sv. Lucija Dingač and Sv. Roko Komarna. Also Istrian whites (Mala Nevina). Open tasting room with food pairing; one of the easier Pelješac visits to book.
  <card gastronomy:Maraska d.d. — Zadar> Maraska d.d. (founded 1946) consolidated the historic Zadar maraschino producers after the war. The Marasca cherry was brought from Asia Minor in Roman times; Dominican monks perfected the liqueur in the 16th c.; Francesco Drioli industrialized in 1759. The liqueur was on the first-class drinks list of the Titanic in 1912. Distillery tours by appointment in Zadar; the bottles are everywhere in Dalmatia.
  <card gastronomy:Franciscan Monastery Pharmacy (1317)> Founded 1317; the third-oldest pharmacy in the world counting non-continuous operations, and the oldest still-operating one in Europe . Sells rose creams, sage soaps, almond oils and pine balms made from the friars' historical recipes. The shop is at the front of the Monastery; the museum-pharmacy hall behind (EUR 6 entry) preserves the 14th-c. apothecary. Address Stradun 2, Old Town
  <card gastronomy:Dežo Art (Konavle textiles)> Hand-embroidered Konavle textiles — the silk-trimmed costume worn in the Easter procession is the most distinct in Dalmatia. Aprons, scarves, the traditional oglavlje head-piece. Real Konavle work, not mass print. Ask for Lela; she will explain the stitch families. Address Dropčev prolaz, Old Town
  <card gastronomy:Uje — Croatian olive oils> The Uje brand selects from Croatian estates — Istrian and Dalmatian. The Konavle oils (smaller estates than the famous Istrian ones, but very respectable) and the Korčula oils are the ones to bring back. Salt-flake selections from the Ston pans; rakija pours by the small bottle. Tasting flights at the back. Address Placa 5, Old Town
