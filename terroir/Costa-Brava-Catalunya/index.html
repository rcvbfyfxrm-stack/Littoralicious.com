The soul of the place — the essay
Two coasts and a plain, a plate for every port, a door for twenty-five centuries, and what the summer pays for
THE ESSAY

The Costa Brava is the Girona coast of Catalonia, where the last folds of the Pyrenees reach the Mediterranean. It is not one place but a sequence: the pine-and-cork coves of Palafrugell, the working fishing port of Palamós, the salt town of L’Escala beside a Greek city, the long bay of Roses, and the bare schist of the cape — with the flat, fertile Empordà behind all of it, and Girona at its inland edge.

Read the coast by what it lands. Palamós is a prawn town: the deep-water red gamba comes in on the trawlers every weekday afternoon and is sold on the quay before it reaches a kitchen. L’Escala is an anchovy town, still curing in salt the way its grandmothers did. Roses is a bay of fish and, at its far end, of one restaurant that changed everything. Each port has its own plate, and the honest way to eat the coast is to eat each plate where it lands.

Behind the sand is the larder. The rivers Muga, Fluvià and Ter drain the Empordà into marsh and paddy, and rice has grown on the lower Ter for more than five centuries. The hills carry olives for the Argudell oil and old bush vines of Garnatxa and Carinyena, kept low and healthy by a north wind that the curiosities explain. Fresh curd drained in a cloth, sweet sausage, salted fish: the plain feeds the coast as much as the sea does.

The quintessence. A plate of red prawns seared with salt on the Palamós quay, the heads sucked for their coral; a garoina opened in February and spooned onto bread; a glass of Empordà red poured in a stone village under arcades. Nothing on it is clever, and nothing on it could come from anywhere else.

Where it comes from

The coast has been a door for twenty-five centuries. Greeks traded at Emporion from the sixth century before Christ, and Rome landed on the same beach in 218 BC to begin the conquest of Hispania; a medieval County of Empúries ruled from Castelló, and the Benedictines of Sant Pere de Rodes kept the vineyards going through the Middle Ages. Later the coast sent its sons to Cuba and took back their fortunes, and in the hungry years after the Civil War its coves ran contraband. The cards below have the dates and the disputes.

Then, in the space of a generation, two kitchens made this the most talked-about table in the world: one in a cove beyond Roses, one in a suburb of Girona. Their story is told just below; their children cook all along the coast.

Where the money sits

The coast runs on summer and on second homes. The big resorts of the south were built for package holidays; the coves of Begur and Palafrugell for Barcelona weekends; and the money that matters to a chef sits in two places — at the table in Girona that sells out months ahead, and on the auction floor at Palamós, where the gamba climbs towards its Christmas price.

So the value is where the work is. The prawn bought by weight at a port house, the anchovy bought at the salter’s counter, the village guisat under the arcades of Peratallada: these cost a fraction of a promenade platter and taste of the place. A galley that provisions here spends on the fish market, the salted anchovy and the wine of the bush vines, and nothing on the front line in August.

And the clock is the season. The coves fill from June to September; the summer festivals end with August; and the real gastronomic season is the cold half of the year — the urchin from winter into March, the calçot from late autumn, the prawn at its dearest before Christmas. Come in spring, autumn or winter, and the coast is yours.

elBulli and the Rocas — the coast that changed cooking
A beach house beyond Roses and a family dining room in Girona, and what they left on every table of the coast
THE STORY

For a quarter of a century the most influential restaurant in the world was a beach house at Cala Montjoi, on the far side of the bay of Roses; before it closed, the most decorated had risen in a Girona suburb. No other stretch of coast can say both.

elBulli was Ferran Adrià’s laboratory: it opened only part of the year, closed its kitchen in the off-season to invent, and was named the world’s best restaurant five times before it served its last dinner on 30 July 2011. In June 2023 the same building reopened as elBulli1846, a museum of the 1,846 dishes the team created — its card is under Culture. The cooks who learned there spread down the coast: three of its head chefs opened Compartir in Cadaqués in 2012, and Adrià’s own favourite counter in Roses is in the port lane.

Inland, three brothers built El Celler de Can Roca in Taialà, on the edge of Girona: three MICHELIN stars since 2009, twice first in the world’s best-restaurant list, a cellar run by the middle brother and a pastry kitchen by the youngest, whose gelateria in the old town lets anyone taste the house for a few euros.

What both left behind is a habit, not a style: start from the product of this coast — the prawn, the anchovy, the rice, the Garnatxa — and think hard about it. You will taste that habit in the port houses as much as in the starred rooms.

The name, the wind, the rock

Who named the coast and when, why the vines grow knee-high, and how old the cape really is.

Named on 12 September 1908curiosityThe coast got its name in a newspaper column signed 'Pol', and not everyone accepts the credit

On 12 September 1908 the journalist and politician Ferran Agulló published a piece called 'Per la Costa Brava' in the Barcelona daily La Veu de Catalunya, signed with his pen name, Pol. It is the first known printed use of the name for this coast, and it meant wild or rugged, not brave. The name spread the next year, when a cruise sold as a trip along the 'costa brava catalana' put it into wider use. How much shore Agulló meant is unclear: some read it as the cliffs from Blanes to Begur, others as everything from the Tordera river to Cap de Creus and beyond.

Why go. Brava here means rugged: say it that way and you will sound like you read the original column.

Where the sources argue
Josep Pla questioned the credit and placed the name's origin at the Hotel Paradís in Fornells, Begur; the Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana dates the coinage to 1905; and the phrase was already used for a stretch of Mallorca's north coast
The wind that plants the vines lowcuriosityThe tramuntana has topped 200 km/h at Portbou, and it decides how every Empordà vine is grown

The tramuntana is a cold north wind that pours off the Pyrenees and down across the Empordà, sometimes for days at a stretch. Gusts above 100 km/h are routine in a strong episode; the highest recorded at Portbou reached 218 km/h, on 29 January 2001. Growers learned to work with it. A vine left tall would be torn, so they keep it low, as a short bush tied to a stake. The wind pays back the trouble: it dries the leaves after rain and dew, so mildew and powdery mould rarely take hold, and the grapes stay small and concentrated. From now until spring is its season.

Why go. Drive the vineyard roads on a tramuntana day and watch the low bushes take a gale that bends the cypresses flat.

The oldest rock in CataloniacuriosityThe cape's dark rock began as sea mud and ash more than half a billion years ago

The dark, layered rock of Cap de Creus started out as mud, sand and volcanic ash on an ancient sea floor, at the turn of the Precambrian and the Cambrian. Volcanic layers inside it have been dated to about 560-540 million years ago, and a gneiss nearby to about 553 million. Around 300 million years ago the collision that raised a mountain chain across Europe buried, heated and squeezed it into schist, and partly melted it at Tudela; bands where the rock was sheared into thin stripes run across the cape. The Gran Enciclopèdia Catalana calls it the most complete series of old rocks in the Catalan lands.

Why go. Run a hand over a shear band at Tudela: the stripes are a mountain range's worth of pressure, frozen.

Who came ashore

The Greeks and the vine, the emigrants who came home rich, and the men who ran the coves after the war.

Did the vine land at Empúries?curiosityLocal lore says the Greeks brought the vine ashore at Empúries; the archaeology points further south

Stand on the beach at Empúries and someone will tell you that wine came to Spain here, carried by the Greek traders who founded Emporion in the 6th century BC. It is a good story and a stubborn one. The digs of recent decades tell another. Phoenician merchants were trading along the Andalusian coast by the 8th century BC, and wine and the vine came with them; by the first half of the 6th century BC, local people at the Alt de Benimaquia near Dénia were already making their own. The Greeks surely traded wine at Empúries. They were probably not first.

Why go. Drink an Empordà red to the Greeks anyway: they made this gulf a wine port, even if they did not plant the first vine in Iberia.

Touch it today
The amphorae and trade finds at MAC-Empúries; the site has its own card under Culture
Where the sources argue
The 'vine came in through Emporion' line is tradition. A 2025 book by Dani López and Lluís Tolosa, reported in Ara, says the Phoenicians in the south came first; the archaeologist Sebastián Celestino, via the ICAC, agrees but calls it a question under constant review
The Indianos of BegurcuriosityWhen Begur's cork trade failed, its sons sailed for Cuba and came home rich enough to build palaces

In the 19th century Begur lived off cork, and when the cork trade fell into crisis its young men left for the Americas, Cuba above all; one tally puts the emigrants at more than five hundred. Some came back with fortunes and built them into the hill town: tall houses with columned porches, painted walls and gardens behind, some modelled on the mansions of colonial Havana where their owners had lived. Why go. Walk the streets below the castle and look up: the porches and painted façades are Havana, built with Begur money.

The camí de ronda, the path that threads along the cliffs from cove to cove, was a patrol route before it was a leisure walk. Ask for it by name

The three Garnatxas, the Carinyena, and the sweet wine of the Empordà.

The three Garnatxasthe house grapeBlack, white and a dusky pink-grey: one grape family in three skins, and the Empordà bottles all of them

On the Empordà plain the old farmers called Garnatxa lledoner, and they grew it in every colour. Garnatxa negra makes most of the reds. Garnatxa blanca gives soft, floral whites. Between them sits lledoner roig, a berry that ripens to a dusty pink-grey and is pressed like a white; the DO Empordà lists it among its white grapes. Of roughly 61 hectares of it in Catalonia, about 60 grow here, so a bottle of it is close to a local exclusive. The vines are low bushes, many of them 50 to 120 years old, which give small crops of concentrated fruit.

Why go. Order a lledoner roig white once this week: you will rarely meet it bottled on its own anywhere else.

Castanyada and Tots Sants: 31 Oct-1 NovchestnutsRoasted chestnuts, sweet potatoes and pine-nut panellets on All Saints' eve; 1 November is a holiday

On the night before Tots Sants, Catalonia eats the castanyada: roasted chestnuts, roasted sweet potatoes and panellets, little balls of almond paste rolled in pine nuts, with a glass of sweet wine. Street chestnut stalls appear in the last week of October. One story ties the custom to bell-ringers fed through All Saints' night; another sees panellets as old offerings for the dead. The Dish — where the list comes from

Seven plates with their honest passports: what each port lands, what the plain grows, and what the whole of Catalonia shares.

ORIGIN

Gamba de Palamós

Aristeus antennatus, the red prawn trawled from the deep canyon off Palamós, four hundred metres down and more, landed the same afternoon and graded by size before it is sold. It is cooked as little as possible — a minute on the plancha with coarse salt — and the head, full of coral, is the part the locals fight over. A quality mark protects the name. Origin. Palamós, between Cap de Begur and the Ridaura

Eat it — La Gamba on the port, or bought at the auction
ORIGIN

Garoines

The violet urchin, Paracentrotus lividus, picked by divers off the rocks of the Baix Empordà. Opened across the mouth, rinsed, and eaten raw: five orange tongues of roe, spooned onto toasted bread, sometimes with a drop of lemon, sometimes scrambled into eggs. The roe swells through the cold months and is at its fullest in late winter. Origin. The Palafrugell coves, Calella, Llafranc and Tamariu

ORIGIN

Anxoves de l'Escala

Anchovies layered head to tail in barrels of coarse salt, pressed under a salt cap and left for six to eighteen months, then cleaned and filleted by hand and laid in olive oil. Eaten on bread rubbed with tomato. The work was done for generations by women, the anxoveres, and the town has salted fish since at least the eighteenth century. Origin. L’Escala

Eat it — Anxoves Solés, at the source
ORIGIN

Suquet de peix

The boat stew: monkfish, scorpionfish and hake with potatoes over a sofregit, bound at the end with a picada pounded from garlic, parsley, nuts and fried bread, with saffron or paprika. Made from the fish that would not sell. Tossa and L’Escala also make a version with anchovy. Origin. The whole coast; fishermen’s cooking

Eat it — Cal Tet, L’Estartit
ORIGIN

Arròs de Pals

Rice from the paddies of the lower Ter, grown round Pals since the fifteenth century: Bahia and Bomba of old, Carnaroli and Nembo now. The long, mild Empordà autumn gives a grain that keeps its shape in the seafood arrossos of the coast. Origin. Pals and the Baix Ter plain

ORIGIN

Recuit de drap

Fresh goat’s or sheep’s curd set for seventeen to twenty-four hours and drained in the cloth — the drap — that gives it its name. It is eaten as a dessert, with honey or sugar, and is closer to a set cream than to cheese. Origin. The Empordà farms

Eat it — Mercat del Lleó, the dairy stalls
ORIGIN

Botifarra

The Catalan pork sausage in all its forms: white and black, raw or cooked, the cooked botifarra de perol, and the Empordà’s oddity, botifarra dolça, sweetened with sugar and lemon and eaten as dessert. The plain one is grilled and served with white beans, amb seques. Origin. All Catalonia; the sweet one from the Empordà

Eat it — Mercat del Lleó, the charcuterie stalls
