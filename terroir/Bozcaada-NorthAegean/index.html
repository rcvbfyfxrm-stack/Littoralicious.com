Bozcaada

Tenedos · Çanakkale · North Aegean · Türkiye
"A small island of wind and vines in the mouth of the Dardanelles: a castle on the harbour, two quarters under one bell tower, and squid on the coals while the meltem bends the low vines."
The name Bozcaada: the grey island, in Turkish. To the Greeks, and to Virgil, it was Tenedos.
The Turkish name came with the Ottomans; the Greek one is three thousand years older and still on the island’s wine labels and in its church. The other word you need is the meltem, the steady summer north wind of the Aegean, which the islanders call poyraz when it blows hard from the north-east and which decides where you swim, how the vines are trained and when the ferry runs.

Bozcaada is a small wine island off the Troad coast, a short ferry from Geyikli: one harbour town under a castle, vines over much of the rest, and a table that eats off two heritages at once, the fishing island’s grilled squid and raw fish and the Rum kitchen’s olive-oil meze and mastic sweets. Make or break: drink the island’s own grapes at the cellar door, and eat squid off the coals under a vine. The warning: it is a summer island. From late autumn most of it closes and many residents leave for the mainland; every card says whether it is open in winter.

The soul of the place — the essay
An island in the wind, its own grapes, two communities, and a table that eats off both
THE ESSAY

Bozcaada is a single crop on a rock in the wind. The meltem blows so steadily that the vegetation grows low and hard, and the vines are pruned into knee-high bushes and left to the dry summer. Read the island as you would read a vineyard: the wind, the soil, the people who worked it and the people who drink it.

The wind is the whole argument. It dries the dew off the grapes before mould can take hold, so the vines need little spraying and no irrigation; it makes the south-coast beaches calm when it blows and turns the north cape into a kite beach. The vines are the island’s own varieties, not the international ones: two whites and two reds whose names you will see on every list, each with its card under Boire & sortir. Guides like to call it a pre-phylloxera island; we found no source for that, and the curiosity card says why.

Then the people. For centuries the island’s wine was made by its Greek Orthodox families, and the treaty that ended the war in 1923 left Tenedos to Turkey with its Greek inhabitants exempted from the exchange of populations. The community still shrank to a remnant, and Turkish families took up the presses. That story, and the cellars that came out of it, is the history fold; the numbers are a curiosity.

The quintessence. The table is the meyhane’s: meze in waves, rakı with water, squid laid over the coals rather than battered, raw fish with torn basil, and a plate of the island’s tomato jam against white cheese. Add the home cooking of the back streets, hand-pinched mantı under garlic yoghurt, and the mastic-almond cookie of the Rum kitchen at the pastane, and you have eaten the island’s two halves in one evening.

Where it comes from

The town is one small grid, walkable in a quarter of an hour: the harbour and the castle square, the Rum quarter rebuilt in straight streets after a fire, and the older Turkish mahalle beyond the dividing street. Everything else is out among the vines and on the beaches, reached by bicycle, scooter, dolmuş or taxi.

There is no airport and no bridge. The car ferry crosses from Geyikli on the mainland; Troy, Assos and Çanakkale are all on the mainland side.

Where the money sits

Wine is the island’s engine, but tourism is its till. Much of the island is under vine, worked without irrigation; yet commercial wine fights a labour shortage, a heavy tax on alcohol and the distance to the drinking cities, so many islanders make wine in small batches for themselves. The money is in the summer visitor, and the harbour front is built for volume.

That is the line you can taste. The value is at the cellar door, at the weekly market for the tomato jam and the GI Çavuş grape, and at the tables where the squid comes off the coals and the meze come out in waves.

And the clock is the wind and the grape. Late May and June are warm and uncrowded, the cellar doors open; August is full, the beds booked and the beaches crowded; late summer brings the harvest and its festival (in August in 2026, though older guides say September: the calendar has the dates), the moment the Çavuş is in your hand rather than in a bottle. From late autumn to spring the island empties and much of the food scene shutters: whoever comes in November gets the castle and the vines to themselves, and must check every door.

1925 — A Greek cellar becomes the first Turkish winery
How Tenedos wine passed from the island's Greek growers to Turkish families, through the state monopoly years, to the 2002 revival.
THE STORY

Tenedos stamped grapes on its money more than two thousand years ago, and for most of the centuries since the island's wine was made by its Greek families. In 1925 a Turkish family bought a Greek's workshop and began making it themselves. What followed was monopoly, decline and, from 2002, a revival on the island's own grapes.

Silver coins struck on Tenedos in about 375 to 350 BC already pair a double-headed axe with a bunch of grapes, and the island's mint was still using the two together around 100 to 70 BC. Island tradition says Tenes, who gave Tenedos its name, found a wild vine near today's Poyraz Limanı and bred Kuntra from it: a legend, not a record. Local accounts also claim the island exported some 800,000 barrels of wine a year in the mid-1800s; we found no independent source for the figure.

By the island's own accounts, Turkish farmers grew grapes and sold them to the Greek families who made the wine until 1925. Lausanne in 1923 kept the island with Türkiye and its Greeks from the exchange (see 'Left out of the exchange'), but the community left over the decades and took its skills with it. In 1925 the Yunatçılar family, later Çamlıbağ, bought a workshop from a Greek as the island's first Turkish Muslim winemaker; Ataol reportedly dates from 1927, and the Talay brothers started their brand in 1948.

The state built its winery at Tekirdağ in 1925, and for much of the century Tekel, the alcohol and tobacco monopoly, dominated the trade; its wine side was sold in 2004. Island accounts date machine production to 1956, call 1960 to 1980 the peak and record a decline after it, with vineyards being torn up. In 2002 the architect Reşit Soley founded Corvus on the island, first harvest 2004 by one account. Six wineries now work Çavuş, Vasilaki, Karalahna and Kuntra.

Taste what the story is about: a glass of Çavuş at Talay’s shop in town, the red at Corvus, and the meze it was made for at Asmalı Meyhane.

Officially this is Cumhuriyet Mahallesi, but everyone still calls it the Rum quarter. It is where the island's churches and chapels stand, and its lanes run straight because they were laid out again after the fire of 1874. The tallest house put up in the years just after the fire, built in 1876 by Dimosten Tulmidis, now holds the island museum. Why go. Walk it after the day-trippers have gone, when the straight lanes run empty down to the bell tower.

The record, corrected

The island in the Aeneid, the louse that came late, and the treaty that left the island its Greeks.

Tenedos and the wooden horsecuriosityVirgil hid the Greek fleet behind this island while Troy pulled the horse inside its walls

'Est in conspectu Tenedos', Virgil writes in Book 2 of the Aeneid: there is an island in sight of Troy, famous and rich while Priam's kingdom stood, now only a bay and an unsafe anchorage for ships. Aeneas tells it at Dido's table. The Greeks sail to Tenedos and hide on its empty shore, the Trojans think they have gone, and the horse is pulled into the city. The island's own coins tell another story. From about 420 BC Tenedos struck a double-headed axe on its silver, and beside it, again and again, a bunch of grapes.

Why go. From the town's west side, look across at the Troad coast: the poem's view, read in reverse.

Touch it today
Look across the strait from the harbour to the Troad shore where Troy stood; the island museum keeps coins among its collections
Where the sources argue
This is literature, not archaeology: no find places the fleet here. The axe has been read as the emblem of a local god, and Cook (1925) tied the grape-and-axe pair to a Dionysiac cult; the stories of Tenes, the island's founder hero, are later, and Cook argued his name was taken from Tenedos, not the other way round
The louse that came latecuriosity'A pre-phylloxera island', say the guides; the sources found do not back them up

The line runs through many Bozcaada guides, ours included: the root louse that wiped out Europe's vineyards in the 19th century only reached the island in the 1940s, so its vines are a pre-phylloxera survival. Searched in English and Turkish in October 2026, the claim has no source. What the record does show is the louse reaching the Ottoman Empire early: in Thrace from 1881, then on the Asian shore of the Bosphorus, at Kızıltoprak, and around Bursa and Aydın from 1885. The state did not formally admit it until 1886. When it reached Tenedos, and whether the vines stand on their own roots today, no source we found records.

Why go. Ask a grower in the vines whether his stocks are grafted: the honest answer is worth more than the slogan.

Touch it today
At a cellar door ask whether the vines are grafted or on their own roots, and when the old rows were last replanted
Where the sources argue
The 1940s arrival date and the 'pre-phylloxera island' label appear in guidebooks only. Ottoman sources put the louse in Thrace from 1881 and in Anatolia from 1885; no source found dates its arrival on Bozcaada or says whether today's vines are grafted
Left out of the exchangecuriosityIn 1923 the Greek–Turkish population exchange left out two islands, and Tenedos was one of them

The Treaty of Lausanne returned Imbros and Tenedos to Turkey, three years after the Treaty of Sèvres had given them to Greece, and wrote a promise into Article 14. The islands would have a special local administration with guarantees for their non-Muslim people, and the Greek–Turkish exchange of populations would not apply to them. So while more than a million people elsewhere were moved, Tenedos's Orthodox families stayed, with their vines and their churches. The promise did not hold. The community dwindled over the century that followed, and how far it fell depends on whose figures you take.

Why go. Behind the Rum quarter's bougainvillea is a treaty clause: read it before you walk those lanes.

Domates reçelithe jarTomato jam, the island's sweet preserve, cooked in late summer and sold by the jar all year

Domates reçeli is tomato jam: tomatoes cooked down slowly with sugar until they turn glossy and dark red, sweet, with a faint savoury edge that tells you it was once a vegetable. Turkish food writers trace the recipe to the island's Rum families, and today it is the jar every visitor carries home, next to fig jam, from homes, shops and market stalls. It comes out at breakfast and with cheese. Despite the name on so many labels, it has no geographical indication: the island's registered product is the Çavuş grape, not the jam.

Why go. Buy a homemade jar rather than a factory one and taste it with the island's white cheese.

Çavuş grapesthe grapeA big, pale-gold table grape, protected since 2 June 2020 as Bozcaada Çavuş Üzümü

Bozcaada Çavuşu is first a grape for eating: big berries, thin pale-gold skin and a sweet, juicy bite. In September 2018 the island's agricultural development cooperative applied to TÜRKPATENT, and on 2 June 2020 the name was registered as a designation of origin, No. 497, for grapes grown in the Bozcaada district. A university study afterwards found the gain was smaller than hoped, because the island lives on summer visitors and the variety is delicate. By late October the harvest is over and the vines are bare.

Why go. In late summer, buy a whole box at the market and eat it on the boat the same day.

When the north wind blows, the south coast’s sand; when the south wind turns, the clear coves of the east and the rocks of the west
6 beaches

Swimming here is organised by wind, not by postcode: on a north wind you swim the south coast (Ayazma, Sulubahçe, Habbele); when the wind turns south you cross to the other shores. Out of season the beach cafés are shut and you bring everything.

The south coast, on a north wind

Sand and shallow water facing away from the north wind: the busy one, the quiet one, and the bay of the campers.

Ayazmathe big beachThe south coast's long sand bay under the chapel, the place to swim when the poyraz blows from the north

Ayazma is the beach the whole island means in August: a broad curve of fine pale sand on the south coast, shallow turquoise water, umbrellas in rows and a strip of fish-and-meze places behind it. The bay takes its name from the Aya Paraskevi chapel and spring on the slope above it. On a north wind the sea here lies flat while the north coast churns. Come back in late October and the picture changes: loungers stacked away, the kitchens mostly shuttered, a handful of locals walking dogs along the waterline and the sand to yourself.

Why go. The calmest big swim on the island on a poyraz day; in high summer claim your patch of sand early in the morning.

Sulubahçebring everythingAyazma's quieter neighbour on the south coast: the same clear water, no loungers, no café

Walk on from Ayazma's umbrellas and the south coast goes quiet at Sulubahçe. The sand is good and the water as clear, but nobody rents you a lounger or sells you a beer, so the people here are campers, families with a cool box and the occasional snorkeller working the rock shelves at the eastern end. Like the rest of this shore it is a north-wind beach: when the poyraz blows it lies calm, and when the lodos swings in from the south the swell comes straight onto it.

Why go. The south-coast swim without the lounger rows: come for the rock shelves with a mask.

Habbelethe family bayA rounded, shallow bay that stays calm on a poyraz, about 7 km from town

Habbele is a small, enclosed bay with golden sand, shallow water that warms faster than the open beaches, and a shape that keeps the poyraz swell outside. That makes it the bay families choose in summer: a paid lounger section at one end, a free stretch where you put down your own towel at the other. Look before you step in: sea urchins sit on the rocky patches of the bottom, so water shoes save the afternoon. Out of season the loungers go and it is simply a quiet cove.

Why go. The gentlest water on the island for children and weak swimmers; wear water shoes for the urchins.

East and west — clear water and the wind

The snorkelling cove, the kite beach on the north cape, and the rocks of the east.

Akvaryumthe snorkel coveNamed for water clear enough to count the fish: the cove to cross to when the lodos turns

Akvaryum means aquarium, and the name is a description: over pale rock and sand the water is clear enough that you watch fish moving below you without a mask. There is nothing else here: no sunbeds, no showers, no changing cabins. The track down is rough, so most people leave the car at the roadside and walk the last stretch, about fifteen minutes. This is the swim for a south (lodos) wind, when the south-coast beaches take the swell and this side lies flatter.

Why go. The island's best snorkelling: come on a lodos day with a mask and fins.

Çayırthe kite beachThe island's wind beach, where Europe's Formula Kite champions raced in June 2015

Çayır is the beach for people who want the wind. On a flat point on the island's north side it takes the poyraz head-on, and offshore reefs flatten the water inside them, which is why kite racers rate it. From 23 to 28 June 2015 it hosted the IKA Formula Kite European Championships during the Go Bozcaada festival, with riders from about a dozen countries; Britain's Oliver Bridge and Russia's Elena Kalinina kept their titles. On a windy day the sky over the beach fills with kites and the swimmers move to the south coast.

Why go. Watch, or take a lesson: this is not a calm swim, it is where the boards go.

Tuzburnu, the salt cape, is a swim for people who prefer rock to sand. There is no café, no lounger and no shade, only clear water over stone, colder than the shallow south-coast bays, and a car parked at the end of the road. Tuzburnu lies on the island's eastern side, among the coves that give shelter when the lodos blows from the south.

Why go. A lodos-day alternative to Akvaryum, for a quiet swim off the rocks.

Up, out and through the vines

The high point on foot, the cape and its light at sunset, and the vineyard roads.

Göztepethe high pointThe island's high point on foot: vines, pines, the windmills and the islets in one turn of the head

Göztepe, the lookout hill, is the top of the island, and the climb is on a path, gentle, through scrub that smells of thyme when the sun is on it. Count about half an hour from the centre. From the top the whole of Bozcaada lies below: the green and rust of the vineyards, the dark pines, the white sails of the rebuilt windmills, the turbine ridge to the west, the islets offshore and the mainland across the strait. The meltem that keeps the vines dry blows hardest up here. By late October the vines below are picked and turning, and a sweater earns its place.

Why go. Go at the start or the end of the day, when the light lies low across the vineyards.

Polente at sunsetthe western capeAn hour out along the bare western cape to the 1861 lighthouse, as the turbines turn behind you

The walk out to Polente is longer than it looks from town: an hour each way, west past the wind farm, over open ground with no tree to stand under. The light at the end has stood on the cape since 1861, a tall white tower of about 30 m. The ridge behind it carries the wind farm that now makes the island's power. The reward comes at the end of the day, when people drive or walk out, sit on the rocks with a beer, and watch the sun drop into the sea beyond the lighthouse.

Why go. Stay until the light comes on: the old tower lit against the turbine ridge is the island's evening.

The vineyard countrythrough the vinesLow gobelet vines raked by the meltem, from Göztepe's flanks down to the south coast

Between a third and a half of Bozcaada is under vine, and the inland walk is through it. The vines are gobelet bushes, trained low and knuckled close to the dusty ground. They are dry-farmed, and the steady meltem dries the morning damp off the leaves before mildew can take hold. The tracks run open between the rows from below Göztepe to the south coast, Late August into September is the harvest, with the Bağ Bozumu festival at its end. By late October the rows are picked, the leaves go rust and yellow, and the smell of the island's must still hangs around the cellars.

Why go. Walk between the rows themselves: the low, wind-shaped vines explain the island's wine better than any glass.

Culture — the castle, the church, the mills and the spring
The harbour fortress, the last Greek church, the island museum; the rebuilt windmills and the chapel under the plane trees

Three stops in town, two out on the island. Check the winter hours: the island’s season is short.

In town

The fortress on the harbour, the church with the bell tower, and a private museum of the island’s layers.

The castleharbour fortressMehmet II's 1455 fortress over the harbour, where a 2023 dig reached Late Bronze Age layers

The walls you see are Ottoman. Mehmet II took the island and rebuilt its fortress in 1455, two years after taking Constantinople. Köprülü Mehmet Paşa and later Mahmud II repaired it. Earlier Venetian and Genoese works were pulled down after the 14th-century War of Chioggia. Inside are an outer bailey, an inner citadel and a dry moat on the landward side; from the ramparts you look down on the harbour and across to the Troad. In 2023 the castle was excavated for the first time, and the team under Prof. Turan Takaoğlu found Byzantine, Roman and Late Bronze Age settlement traces below the Ottoman floor.

Why go. Walk the ramparts for the one view that holds the harbour, the two quarters and the strait together.

The church of St Marythe bell towerThe Rum quarter's church of 1869 rang its bells again after a two-year restoration

The Kimisis Theotokou, the church of the Dormition of the Virgin, has served the island's Orthodox since 1869. Inside, five Corinthian columns divide a three-aisled nave; outside, the four-storey bell tower stands over the Rum quarter's roofs, and you can see it from most of town. After a two-year restoration the church reopened for worship, and the bells rang for a liturgy led by the Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew. The mayor and the island's mufti came, and so did Orthodox families from several countries. Its feast is the Dormition, 15 August.

Why go. Go in quietly on an ordinary morning, when it is still a parish church before it is a sight.

The island museumlocal historyOne collector's maps, coins and wine bottles in the tallest house built after the 1874 fire

The Bozcaada Yerel Tarih Araştırma Merkezi, the Local History Research Centre, fills a house that Dimosten Tulmidis built in 1876. It was the tallest and grandest of the houses put up just after the fire of 1874. The collection is Hakan Gürüney's, an islander who has spent years gathering Bozcaada. There are fossils and archaeological finds, coins, old maps and engravings of Tenedos, island wine bottles, and the household goods of Greek and Turkish families side by side. The building was given over to the collection by a 7 June 2006 protocol with the district governorship. On a windy day it is the quickest way to read the island's layers.

Why go. Look for the old island wine labels: the vineyard history in glass and paper, before you walk the rows.

Out on the island

The rebuilt stone windmills, and the chapel and its spring under the plane trees.

The windmillsstone millsRebuilt from islanders' old photographs, the stone mills that once ground the island's wheat

The island once ground its own wheat in round stone mills along its ridges, and by the late 2010s they were ruins. The district governor İbrahim Gültekin said they had collapsed 50 to 60 years before, from neglect. The district governorship rebuilt them in 2019 from the islanders' memory. Islanders brought out old family photographs to show where each mill had stood, then the sites were cleared and levelled. Two mills, 4 m across and 6.5 m tall, went back up on the island's south side, and a third was under way at the north end. They do not grind. They give the skyline back its old silhouette against the turbines.

Why go. Go up for the silhouette at the end of the day, with the sea and the strait behind the mills.

Ayazma chapel and springholy springA 1734 chapel under plane trees that opens one day a year, when bread, wine and oil are blessed

Ayazma comes from the Greek hagiasma, holy water. The chapel of Aya Paraskevi was built in 1734, attributed to one Manolaki Manolidis, and stands among old plane trees beside a two-spouted fountain that runs all year. For most of the year the door is shut. On 26 July, Saint Paraskevi's day, it opens for the Ayazma Panayırı: islanders and families back from Greece fill the shade, the Metropolitan of Gökçeada and Bozcaada blesses bread, wine and olive oil, and pilgrims light candles and make wishes at the spring. In July 2025 the Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew led the service. In October you have the plane trees to yourself.

Why go. Sit by the running fountain under the planes, the coolest shade on the south coast.

The island makes wine, jam and sweets, and little else by hand. Honestly, nothing here clears the whole bar in October 2026: no workshop we found names a maker on a class anyone can join. The card at the top comes closest; the others show the craft without teaching it.

Do this one

The closest the island comes to a class.

The harvest at Bağ Bozumupick and crushNothing on the island clears the bar fully; the closest is picking grapes in the rows at harvest

We searched for a hands-on class proper to Bozcaada with a named maker you can sign up with: ceramics, weaving, jam, cooking, boatbuilding. None turned up. The nearest thing is the harvest itself. During the grape-harvest festival, held from 14 to 23 August in 2026, visitors go out to the vineyards to cut bunches by hand, the Çavuş and Karalahna pruned low against the wind, and the grapes come back to town with music. It is real work in a real crop, open to anyone who turns up, but it is an event, not an apprenticeship: no named master teaches you, and it happens once a year.

Why go. It is the one week you hold the island's grapes in your hand rather than in a glass.

The honest warning

What is sold as island-made, and is not.

What is sold as island-madethe warning'Island wine' in a plain bottle is not always what it claims: police seized 1,300 litres of fake on Bozcaada

The island's reputation sells, and not everything that trades on it is what the label says. In one operation the gendarmerie raided a vineyard house on Bozcaada and seized 1,300 litres of counterfeit wine, with one person detained. Home-batch wine is part of island life, made in small lots for the family, but a plain unlabelled bottle sold over a counter carries no guarantee of grape, maker or safety. The old guide's advice holds for food too: the year-round tourist stalls are not the same thing as the Wednesday producers' market, and the Çavuş grapes and jams are best bought from the people who grow and cook them.

Why go. Buy wine with a producer's name on it, from the producer.

Boire & sortir — at the glass
The island’s four grapes by name, the rakı that goes with the meze, the late bar, and the cellars
7 to know

Learn four grape names and every list on the island makes sense. Then taste at a cellar door: most are in town.

Ask for it by name

The two whites and the two reds the island grows and almost nobody else does.

Çavuşthe grapeThe island's sweetest eating grape, pressed into a dry white

Çavuş is the grape Bozcaada eats: islanders call it the most flavourful of the island's table grapes, big, thin-skinned and fragile. The wineries also press it, alone or with Vasilaki, for dry whites and pale rosés. A grape bred for the fruit bowl is not an obvious candidate for a serious wine, which is exactly why the bottles are worth a glass: expect something light and soft rather than sharp, made to drink young with the meze. Corvus and Talay both make one.

Why go. Ask for a Çavuş white with the first cold meze, before the reds come out.

In the glass
A light, soft dry white, sometimes a pale rosé; drink it young and cold
On the label
Çavuş, alone or blended with Vasilaki; Bozcaada on the label
Ask for
"Bir şişe Bozcaada Çavuş": a Corvus or Talay bottling
Vasilakithe grapeA thin-skinned white that ripens early on the island and is drunk young

Vasilaki keeps a Greek name from the island's Rum winemakers. It is a white with thin-skinned berries that ripens early on Bozcaada and goes into both dry and sweet wines; the ministry's grape guide is plain that it is not one for the cellar. Writers describe the dry version as acidic and fuller than you expect, one comparing it to a Chardonnay, nearly. The old guide said it grows nowhere else; the sources we found call it Bozcaada's own but do not go that far. Drink it the year after the harvest, with grilled fish.

Why go. If a cellar has a sweet Vasilaki, taste it beside the dry one: the same grape, two directions.

In the glass
Fresh, acidic, fuller than its colour suggests; dry or sweet
On the label
Vasilaki, alone or blended with Çavuş; drink the youngest vintage
Ask for
A dry Vasilaki with the fish, a sweet one with the pastanesi's biscuits
Karalahna and Kuntrathe glassTwo island reds: one dark and firm, one light and cherry-red, often in the same bottle

Karalahna is late-ripening and grown almost only on Bozcaada: deep-coloured, high in acid, firm in tannin. Even its name is argued over, black pumpkin or black cabbage. Kuntra is the same grape the mainland calls Karasakız (also Mavro Kuntra, Sakız Kara); light, with red cherry and redcurrant, it went mostly into the still for brandy until island producers revived it for reds, rosés and sparkling wines. Each covers the other's gap, which is why the cellars so often blend them, or set Karalahna beside Merlot. Corvus's Corpus is the famous one; ask the cellar door for the plain versions too.

Why go. Taste a straight Kuntra and a Karalahna blend side by side to understand the island's red.

In the glass
Karalahna: dark, tannic, high acid. Kuntra: pale, light, red cherry and redcurrant
On the label
Kuntra may appear as Karasakız; blends read Karalahna–Kuntra or Karalahna–Merlot
Ask for
A Kuntra rosé with the meze, a Karalahna blend with grilled meat

The meal's partner, and the late glass

The rakı poured with the meze, and the bar in the back lanes.

Rakıthe glassNot made on the island, but the drink the meze evening is built around

Bozcaada makes wine; rakı comes from the mainland. Yet under the vine canopies of the meyhanes it is the fixed partner to the cold meze, the raw fish and the grilled squid as they come out in waves. It is an anise spirit, clear in the bottle, that turns cloudy white the moment water goes in. The ritual is slow: a narrow glass, a finger or two of rakı, cold water to cloud it, then ice, and a long evening of small plates between sips. The old guide put it best: the wine is what you take home, the rakı is the meal.

Why go. Order it the island way, with the meze tray, and let the evening run at its pace.

In the glass
Clear anise spirit that turns milky white with water
Ask for
A tek (single) or duble (double), with a jug of cold water and a bowl of ice
How to drink it
Rakı first, water to cloud it, ice after; sip between mezes, never as a shot

Bakkal means grocer, and the name is the joke: this is one of the few places on the island that turn into a bar after dark. By day it is fresh juice and quiet tables on a cobbled side lane of the Rum quarter; on a summer night it fills with a young crowd, music up, the lane full. The cocktails lean on what grows here: Hürriyet Daily News sent readers for the ones made with corn poppy and with grapes. It is seasonal, so in late October expect it shut until spring.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Seven plates and jars and their honest passports: which are the island’s own, which it shares with the Aegean, and which came with the meyhane.

ORIGIN

Kalamar on the coals

Island squid laid over charcoal and grilled plain, with lemon and olive oil, rather than battered and fried as the mainland prefers it. It is the plate the meyhanes of the old bazaar streets are known for, and it arrives after the cold meze, before the fish. Origin. The fishing island’s own way with squid; the meyhane is shared with the whole Aegean coast

Eat it — Asmalı Meyhane, or Sandal
ORIGIN

Domates reçeli

Tomato jam: island tomatoes cooked down with sugar at the end of summer, when there are more than anyone can eat, and sold by the jar all year. Eaten at breakfast against a salty white cheese, the sugar cut by the salt. It carries no geographical indication: the island’s only registered product is the Çavuş grape. Origin. The island’s best-known preserve

Eat it — the weekly market, by the jar
ORIGIN

Mantı

Small dumplings of dough pinched by hand around a little filling, boiled, then dressed with garlic yoghurt and red-pepper butter. The island’s home-cooking kitchens make them by hand; the dish itself is Anatolian, far older and wider than the island. Origin. Anatolian, cooked here in the island’s family kitchens

Eat it — Dört Hanımeli
ORIGIN

Damla sakızlı enginar

Artichoke cooked in olive oil and scented with mastic, the resin of the Aegean lentisk tree, served cold as a meze. Mastic runs through the island’s sweets too, in the almond cookie and the ice cream: a flavour of the Greek Aegean kept in Turkish kitchens. Origin. The Aegean’s olive-oil kitchen; mastic from the islands

Eat it — Sandal
ORIGIN

Enginarlı ahtapot

Octopus and artichoke together, cooked in a cream sauce with rosemary: a cook’s plate rather than a canon, made on the island from two things the island has in season. Origin. A modern island cook’s plate

Eat it — Ada’m
ORIGIN

Bademli kurabiye

A small almond cookie flavoured with mastic, baked in a wood-fired stone oven at the island’s pastane and sold by the bag. The recipe is the Rum community’s, the Greek Orthodox families who made the island’s wine for centuries, and it is still baked on Gökçeada and in Kavala. Origin. The Rum kitchen of the north Aegean islands

Eat it — Çiçek Pastanesi, by the bag
ORIGIN

Rakı

The anise-flavoured spirit distilled from grapes, poured with cold water so it turns milky white, and drunk slowly through a long meyhane meal. It is not made on the island; it is how the island eats. Origin. Turkish; the meyhane’s drink everywhere

Eat it — under the vine at Asmalı Meyhane
