Kendwa

Unguja · Zanzibar · Tanzania
"The one beach on this island that keeps its water when the sea walks out — and the only one pointed at the sunset."
The names Kendwa keeps quiet. Everything around it — the wind, the island, the point, the light — says exactly what it is.
Honesty first: we could not find a documented origin for the name Kendwa in any source we trust, so we are not going to invent one. The village is on the far north-west of Unguja, looking out at the small island of Daloni and, beyond it, the long low shape of Tumbatu. The names around it are all working words. Zanzibar is generally derived from the Persian Zang-bār by way of Arabic — the coast of the Zanj — a name given from the sea, by people arriving. Unguja is what the island calls itself in Swahili, and its origin is not settled either. Kaskazini A, the district, is simply 'north' — the same root as kaskazi, the north-east monsoon that carried the dhows down from Arabia and India between November and March, against kusi, the southerly that blew them home from April to October. Two winds, two seasons, one economy. Mnarani, where the turtle pond and the lighthouse are, means 'at the lighthouse' — a place named after the thing standing in it. Forodhani, down in Stone Town, means customs — the gardens where the island now eats its street food were where its cargo was assessed. A coast whose oldest name is silent and whose newer ones are all wind, light, shade and duty: that is the north tip in one paragraph.

THE ESSAY

There is a moment, most afternoons, when the whole logic of this place shows itself at once. It is about half past five. The dhow yards at the north end have stopped for the day and the half-built hulls stand on the sand like ribs. The water is still there — it is always still there, that is the entire point of Kendwa — and it has gone the colour of hot metal. A ngalawa comes back along the shoreline with its single triangular sail set, and it is not doing this for you: it has fish in it. Behind you, two hundred metres inland, the muezzin starts, and a hundred metres the other way somebody is testing a sound system for tonight. Both of those are true at the same time and neither is winning.

That tension is the character of this cape and it is worth understanding rather than resolving. Nungwi is a Muslim fishing village of several thousand people that has been building boats and reading this water for centuries; the bar strip is a thin bright ribbon along its western edge, three decades old. The village did not become the strip. It sold it a frontage and kept everything behind. Walk the two hundred metres inland and the prices halve, the food improves, the noise stops, and the language changes from the tourist's English to Kiswahili — and, out on the island you can see from the beach, to Kitumbatu, which is its own dialect entirely.

The light here is a west-coast light, which on this island is rare and is the reason for everything. Unguja's famous beaches are on the east: white, enormous, and facing the sunrise across a reef that empties. Kendwa faces the other way. The sun goes down over open water past Tumbatu, and because the shelf is steep the sea does not retreat to meet it. There is no ceremony to it and no viewing platform. People simply stop what they are doing at about six and stand in the water.

Haraka haraka haina baraka.
Hurry, hurry has no blessing.Swahili proverb

The proverb is the practical instruction, not the decoration. Everything on this coast runs on cycles you cannot accelerate: the tide, which decides whether the walk to Nungwi exists this afternoon; the monsoon, kaskazi down and kusi back, which decided a thousand years of trade and still decides the diving; the moon, which sets the spring tides and therefore the landings; and the eight or nine months a clove tree needs before its buds are worth picking. A guide that gives you opening hours and not a tide table has told you almost nothing about this place.

And what it tells you about living is the thing the dhow builders demonstrate every morning without saying a word. They are shaping a hull out of a mango trunk with an adze, from no drawing, using a set of proportions carried in the head and the hands, on a beach where the same job has been done for longer than any record we have. It will take months. Nobody is filming it. The boat will work because the method is old enough to have had every wrong version removed from it. Set against a coast now measured in seven-night packages, that is not nostalgia — it is a claim about how good things actually get made, and this beach happens to have both arguments standing on it, two hundred metres apart, every single day.

The clove economy under the sunbeds
Nine-tenths of the world's cloves, a chamber cut into the coral, and the reason this island's food tastes of the Indian Ocean rather than of Africa alone
~90% of world supply

Every plate in this guide is downstream of a single agricultural order and the labour system that carried it out. The polite version is called the Spice Island. The ledger is harder, and you cannot read the menus honestly without it.

Around 1840, Sultan Said bin Sultan moved the seat of the Omani sultanate from Muscat to Zanzibar — the only time in modern history a state relocated its capital from Asia to Africa. He had first come to the island in 1828 and had already been buying clove-growing land. He then decreed that three clove trees be planted for every coconut palm on Unguja and Pemba. It worked with brutal efficiency: by the end of the nineteenth century something in the order of nine-tenths of the world's cloves grew on these two islands.

The plantations ran on slavery. That is not a footnote to the spice trade, it is its mechanism, and Zanzibar was the western Indian Ocean's clearing house for it. The 1873 treaty negotiated by the British consul Sir John Kirk closed the open slave market — the Anglican cathedral in Stone Town stands on the site — but the trade went underground rather than stopping. The slave chamber at Mangapwani, cut into the coral around 1880 by Mohammed bin Nassor Al-Alwi, was in use for something like another twenty-five years, until about 1905. It is a twenty-minute detour off the road you will drive to Stone Town. Take it.

Then the weather took a turn at the ledger. An island economy built on two tree crops and a fleet lost most of all three in one afternoon, on 15 April 1872 — the story is with the old sailors.

Why any of this is in a food guide. Because it answers the question a cook actually asks here: why does a Tanzanian island cook with cardamom, cumin, turmeric and tamarind, and serve biryani and urojo next to grilled reef fish? Because for a thousand years the monsoon made this a node of the Indian Ocean rather than an edge of Africa — kaskazi brought Omani and Gujarati traders down between November and March and kusi took them home from April — and then one sultan's decree turned the whole island into a single spice estate worked by enslaved people. The cooking is what that produced. Eat it knowing what it is.

If you understand one thing about this place before you arrive, make it this. Kendwa is not a better beach than the famous east-coast beaches. The shelf

Why the water staysthe whole pointOn the east coast the sea walks a kilometre out twice a day; at Kendwa it moves a few metres

On Unguja's east coast, at Paje, Jambiani and Michamvi, the fringing reef sits one to one and a half kilometres offshore over almost flat ground. When the tide drops, the sea retreats all the way to the reef crest and the flats dry into a shallow, walkable moonscape. Beautiful, and you cannot swim, twice a day, every day. Kendwa's seabed falls away steeply within a few metres of the sand. Why go. This is the honest reason to choose this coast over the prettier east: you can swim when you want to, not when the moon allows.

The old sailors — true stories of this water
Two thousand years of seamanship off this cape: the two winds that ran an ocean, the survey that cost half a crew, a hurricane that took a sultan's navy in an afternoon, and the shortest war in history. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement is told
11 true stories

The water in front of this beach is one of the oldest continuously worked sea roads on earth. These are the stories the old hands would tell about it — each with the seamanship truth inside it, and the place on this cape where you can still put a hand on it today.

The two winds

Kaskazi and kusithe six-month waitYou did not sail to Zanzibar and back. You sailed down, then lived here until the wind turned

From about November to March the north-east monsoon, kaskazi, carried dhows down from Oman, the Gulf and the Malabar coast. From roughly April to October the south-easterly, kusi, took them home. Two winds, six months apart, and no way to hurry either. So a crew that came in on the kaskazi could not leave for months. They lived here, traded here, married here. That is why Swahili is a Bantu language with Arabic and Persian layered through it, why biryani counts as a local dish, and why the cooking of a small African island tastes of Gujarat and Muscat. The monsoon moved more than cargo. It built a civilisation by making people stay put.

★ The old hand's point: a reversing monsoon is the safest long-distance wind system there is, because the return leg is guaranteed in advance, and that reliability, not bravery, made the Indian Ocean the earliest of the great sea trades, a thousand years ahead of the Atlantic.

A scholarly note on the Swahili names of Zanzibar's fishes records something easy to miss. Nguru, the kingfish, Scomberomorus commerson, once supported a substantial import trade, because the dhows caught it on their run south from Arabia and the Persian Gulf. The fish arrived with the monsoon fleet, on lines towed over the side on the way down. It is still the standard fish of this coast, firm and good grilled whole, and the one you will be offered at the Nungwi landing tonight. The only difference is that now it is caught here.

The chart makers

HMS Leven, and the price of a chart1821–1826Eighty-three charts of this ocean, bought with two-thirds of the officers and half the crew

In August 1821 the sloop HMS Leven sailed for Africa with the brig-sloop Barracouta under Captain William Fitzwilliam Owen, ordered to survey eastward from the Cape. The orders grew into the whole east coast of Africa, southern Arabia, Madagascar and several island groups of the Indian Ocean. By the time Leven came home late in 1826, thirty thousand nautical miles of coast had been surveyed and eighty-three charts drawn. The cost was appalling. When the expedition put back into Cape Town in July 1822 after seven months' work, two-thirds of the officers and half the crew were dead of malaria. And Owen, the detail worth carrying home, is recorded as having identified the mosquito as the carrier.

★ The old hand's point: the danger on a survey is not the sea but the anchorage, because a surveying ship lives stopped in shallow, sheltered water near river mouths, exactly where fever breeds, and every chart of this ocean was bought at that price.

The hurricane of 15 April 1872one afternoonOne afternoon took every ship in the harbour but one, and two-thirds of the island's plantations

On the afternoon of 15 April 1872 a gale rose to hurricane force over Zanzibar and blew until nightfall. By morning at least two-thirds of the clove and coconut plantations were gone, and since a clove tree takes years to bear, the loss kept compounding. Around a hundred and fifty Arab and Indian vessels were sunk or driven ashore, many still loaded. The sultan's small navy, the fleet Said had built and left to his heirs as personal property, was wrecked, and with it much of the family's grip on the mainland ports. Every ship in the harbour was lost, with one exception. The Abydos, bound for London, got out to sea under high steam.

★ The old hand's point: in a harbour full of sailing ships, the one survivor was the one that could make way against the wind and claw off into open water; sea room is safety, a crowded anchorage in a blow is the most dangerous place on a coast, and it cost a fleet to prove it.

Dhow chasing1873 onwardA Royal Navy blockade that ran to the monsoon calendar, because the slave trade did

In June 1873 a treaty with the Sultan of Zanzibar for the suppression of the slave trade was concluded, negotiated under the hand of the British consul Sir John Kirk, and the open slave market in Stone Town closed. What followed was a naval campaign that a captain of the period, G. L. Sullivan, wrote up under the plain title Dhow Chasing in Zanzibar Waters. It was seasonal by necessity. Through the slave-running months of March, April and May, ships of the squadron, the Briton, the Daphne, the Vulture, worked the coast north of Zanzibar, and most of the real interceptions were made by their open boats.

★ The old hand's point: a warship could not follow a shallow-draught lateen dhow inshore or through the reef passages, so the work fell to ships' boats under sail and oar, boarding at sea with no cover, and both sides were sailing the same monsoon calendar.

The shortest war in history27 Aug 1896Fire opened at 09:02 and stopped at 09:40. Around five hundred dead, one British sailor hurt

Sultan Hamad bin Thuwaini died on 25 August 1896. Within hours his cousin Khalid bin Barghash had taken the palace and declared himself sultan, which was not the succession the British had in mind. He barricaded himself in with some two thousand eight hundred defenders. At 09:02 on 27 August the ships offshore opened fire. At 09:40 they stopped, and the palace and the harem beside it were rubble. Around five hundred were dead, overwhelmingly Zanzibari civilians and palace guards; one British sailor was injured. Khalid and his closest advisers left when the first shells landed, ran to the German consulate, and were eventually taken out to German East Africa.

The working sea, now

Mwani — the seaweed women~30,000 farmersSome 30,000 seaweed farmers, four in five of them women, paid about a third of the world price

Seaweed, mwani, is one of Zanzibar's biggest employers: something like thirty thousand farmers, around eighty per cent of them women, growing it on lines staked across the reef flats for buyers in Japan, the United States, Denmark, China and Spain. It is one of the few cash incomes here that goes straight to women. The economics are hard. Zanzibari farmers get roughly thirty to forty per cent of the international price, against sixty to seventy in Indonesia and the Philippines, and they have asked the government to lift the indicative price for spinosum from 700 to 3,000 shillings a kilo. Warmer water and fierce sun have rotted much of the crop in bad seasons. Women who process it into soaps, oils and cosmetics earn something like US$250–300 a month, against about US$35 for raw farming.

★ The old hand's point: seaweed grows in the intertidal zone, so it is planted and cut bent double in hot shallow water on a day set by the tide, the only fishery here where the tide table is the shift roster; buy the soap from a women's cooperative and pay the asking price.

The pweza on your plate most likely never saw a boat. Octopus on this coast is taken on the exposed reef flats at low water, on foot, with a stick or a short spear. The gatherer walks the pools reading the ground for the tell-tale midden of empty shells piled outside a den. ★ The old hand's point: a fishery worked on foot at low water is ruled by the moon, since big spring tides bare more reef for longer, so the catch and the market price move on a fortnightly rhythm; buy octopus in the days around a full or new moon.

The ngalawaone mango treeA five-metre canoe dug from one mango trunk, with outriggers whose idea crossed an ocean

The mtepe — the ship with no nails1st c. AD – c. 1935Two thousand years of planks stitched with coconut cord, and not one iron fastening

Sewn-plank boats have worked the Swahili coast since at least the first century AD. The last of the type, the mtepe, had its planks stitched together with coir rolled from coconut husk and pegged with wood, with no iron anywhere in the hull. The last of them vanished in the mid-1930s, in the Lamu archipelago up the coast. In 2003 a research reconstruction, Shungwaya, was built in Zanzibar under Fundi Mohammed Bwana of Lamu, whose grandfather had built them. It was the centrepiece of the Dhow Cultures exhibition in the House of Wonders until part of the roof fell in 2020; it has stood outside in the weather since.

Why go. The one surviving full-size answer to how this coast sailed for two thousand years, and it is slowly going.

Before you go
What to skip, what is contested, and the four things that will actually spoil a week here
10 traps
  • Booking a week without looking at the moon. Two lunar facts decide more of your time here than any hotel choice. The Full Moon Party at Kendwa Rocks runs the first Saturday after each full moon and turns the beach into a several-thousand-person event; and spring tides at new and full moon give the widest low water, the biggest fish landings and the cheapest market. Whether you want the party or want to be a long way from it, the moon phase and the tide table are the two documents to check before you book. Instead: pick your week off a tide table.
  • Assuming the beach walk to Nungwi is always there. The three kilometres between the two beaches are firm sand at low water and impassable at high. People get caught by the returning tide on that headland. Instead: check the table, add margin for the way back, never walk it after dark, and take a ten-minute taxi on the inland road when the door has shut.
  • The Baraka aquarium, without knowing the argument. Baraka Natural Aquarium charges US$10 each to swim with, feed or view captive turtles, and its own site claims rescue, rehabilitation and release. Visitor accounts allege handling and riding of animals, a small crowded pond, turtles bought from fishermen, and no evidence of releases when asked. We could not resolve it and will not pretend to. Instead: Mnarani, village-run since 1993 in a tidally flushed pool with a public release each February — or better, see turtles wild on a Mnemba dive.
  • Any boat that promises you a landing on Mnemba. The island is private and you cannot go ashore. The water around it is a conservation area with a daily fee (two fee zones since September 2025 — see the Mnemba card), and it is superb. Instead: book a dive or snorkel through a dive centre rather than a beach tout, and be clear you are paying for water, not sand.
  • Turning up at Tumbatu. The island on your horizon is not an excursion: landing needs the elders' permission (the rule is on the Tumbatu card). Instead: look at it from the beach, or dive the reefs off it, which needs no permission.
  • The gift-box spice. A printed presentation box of Zanzibar spices costs several times the same spice loose on a scale, and the contents are usually older. Instead: buy at Darajani by weight, and press a clove with a thumbnail first — a fresh one is oily and marks the paper.
  • Booking a spice farm from the car. Every driver has a farm he is paid to deliver you to, and the difference between a working estate and a staged tasting is considerable. Instead: name Kizimbani and hold to it, and go in a dry-season month when there is actually picking and drying to see.
  • Anything on a tray at Forodhani. The seafood platters glistening under lights at the night market have been sitting there for hours. Instead: eat only what is cooked in front of you from raw — the Zanzibar pizza, the mishkaki off the coals, the sugar-cane juice pressed while you watch — and agree the price before it is made, because overcharging visitors here is routine.
  • Treating Nungwi village as an extension of the beach. Two hundred metres inland is a conservative Muslim fishing village of several thousand people. Beachwear, open bottles and volume are all fine on the sand and all wrong past it. Instead: cover shoulders and knees — men too — the moment you leave the beach, ask before photographing anyone or their boat, and keep the walk home quiet.
  • Trusting a stale listing on this cape. Aggregators disagree about who is open here, and the ones that disagree are not always the ones you would guess; every card in this guide says whether it was confirmed and when. Instead: call, message, or walk past and look before you build an evening around any single address here.

The two kite windskusi and kaskaziThe southeasterly kusi blows from June to October and the northeasterly kaskazi from December to March.

Zanzibar's wind year is two trade winds. The kusi blows from the south-southeast from about May or June to October, mostly at 15 to 20 knots. It is the strong season, and the kite schools at Paje on the east coast are busiest then. The kaskazi blows from the north-east from about December to March. It is lighter, around 12 to 18 knots, and suits beginners. The north tip is sheltered from the kusi, so it has calm water in those months, not good kiting. Experienced riders do kite deep water off Nungwi. Most visitors who kite go to Paje.

Why go. Whether you want wind or calm water decides which coast to stay on and when.

When
Kusi ~Jun-Oct; kaskazi ~Dec-Mar
Where
Paje (east coast) for schools; Nungwi for experienced riders

In the water

The humpbacks are leavingwhales, tail endWhale season is ending: August and September were the peak, and by November a sighting is luck.

Humpbacks come north from the Southern Ocean to calve in East African water in the southern winter, and turn back south as it ends. The sources agree on the shape, though not the exact edges. They put the season at roughly June or July to November, with the best odds in July to September, and they name September and October for Nungwi in particular. So early October still gives a fair chance, falling week by week. By November a sighting is luck. Book a dolphin or snorkel trip that might see a whale, not a trip sold as whale-watching.

Why go. In early October a sighting is still possible. By late November it rarely happens.

October is the changeover. The southeasterly kusi blows from about June and fades through October. The northeasterly kaskazi does not set in until about December. Why go. This is the calmest sea of the year here, and the best time for the deep, current-swept bank.

On the plate

Last weeks of the main clove harvestclovesThe main mwaka clove crop is picked July to November. In October there is still harvesting to see.

Zanzibar takes two clove crops a year. The main one is mwaka, from July to November, and its buds are the larger. The smaller vuli crop follows from December to March. The state trading company, ZSTC, has long been the only legal buyer. In October you can still see picking and the mats of buds drying by the road. By late November the main crop is ending, and the rain makes drying harder. If a spice tour is on your list, go early in this window.

Why go. Go in October and you see the harvest being worked, not just a tasting table.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The same canon, told properly: what each thing is, the history that put it on this island, and the one detail that separates the real version from the one made for visitors.

#1
DISH

Mchuzi wa nazi THE MOTHER SAUCE

Everything else here is a variation on this. A coconut is grated on a mbuzi — the 'goat', a low stool with a serrated iron blade that a cook sits astride and scrapes the halved nut against — and the gratings are pressed twice: a thick, fatty first milk and a thin second. The second goes in early with garlic, ginger, turmeric, cumin, tomato and often tamarind; the first goes in at the end. Fish, octopus, beans, greens, chicken: the sauce is the constant and the protein is the variable.
★ The tell: look at the surface. A sauce cooked long enough will break — the coconut oil separates and comes back up as a shine, and that shine is the whole difference. Coconut milk poured over fish at the last minute will never split, and never tastes of anything but coconut.
WhereMachnoo and Combo 1990 in Nungwi village; Lukmaan in Stone Town.
#2
DISH

Pweza wa nazi THE PLATE

Octopus in coconut curry, and the dish this coast is known for by name. Octopus is one of the north tip's real fisheries — it is taken on the reef flats by hand and by spear at low water, largely by women in some villages, which is why it is on every menu and why it is cheap when the spring tides have been good. It is simmered long and slow in mchuzi wa nazi until the muscle finally gives.
★ The tell: texture, and it is binary. Octopus is tender under about half an hour or after well over an hour, and rubber in between. If it fights you, the kitchen took it off the heat in the middle window — send the next one back and order the grilled version instead.
WhereMachnoo, Nungwi village · The Fisherman Local, Kendwa.
#3
DRINK

Kahawa chungu THE KETTLE

Bitter coffee — chungu means bitter, and the absence of sugar is the definition, not an omission. It is brewed strong and black over charcoal with cardamom, clove and often ginger or cinnamon, held in a tall brass kettle, and poured into small handleless cups holding two or three mouthfuls. You drink standing, you hand the cup back, and the next person gets it. Along this whole coast, from Zanzibar up through Lamu, it is the furniture of conversation: the thing men sit over in an alley for an hour.
★ The tell: the kettle should be on the charcoal, not off it, and the pour should come from height. If someone offers you sugar, you are being handled as a tourist — the correct accompaniment is a piece of halua, the sticky Zanzibari sweet, taken between sips.
WhereThe coffee men at the edges of Darajani market, Stone Town, from early morning.
#4
DISH

Urojo THE MIX

A thick, sour, turmeric-yellow soup built on mango and tamarind, served as an assembly rather than a bowl: broth first, then boiled potato, bhajia, crisp cassava, sometimes a skewer, then coconut chutney and a fierce red pilipili sauce spooned over the top. It is widely called Zanzibar mix and is unofficially treated as Tanzania's national dish — though the record puts its origin in Dar es Salaam rather than here, which is a useful corrective to the idea that everything good on this island was invented on it.
★ The tell: the sourness must come from tamarind and unripe mango, not vinegar, and it should make you wince slightly before the coconut chutney rescues it. A sweet urojo has been made for a palate that is not from here.
WhereForodhani Gardens at dusk; Lukmaan's counter by day.
#5
STREET

Zanzibar pizza THE INVENTION

Dough is stretched translucent on an oiled griddle; minced meat or seafood or vegetable goes on with onion and chilli; an egg is cracked over it; and then — the detail that offends purists and defines the dish — a squeeze of mayonnaise. The corners fold in to make a parcel and it fries on both sides until the egg sets inside. It exists nowhere in Italy and nowhere in the older Swahili canon. It was invented at the Forodhani stalls, for the crowd that walks past them, and it is now the single most recognisable street food on the island.
★ The tell: it must be made from raw dough in front of you. Every stall keeps a stack of pre-cooked ones under a light for the people who will not wait. Wait.
WhereForodhani Gardens night market, Stone Town, from about 18:00. Agree the price before it is made.
#6
DISH

Pilau & biryani THE TWO RICES

They are not the same dish and the difference is a technique, not a recipe. Pilau is one-pot: the spices — clove, cumin, cardamom, black pepper, cinnamon — are bloomed in fat, the meat browned in them, the rice added and cooked in that stock, so every grain comes out brown through. Biryani is two-pot: rice and a wet masala are cooked separately, then layered and steamed together, so the plate arrives streaked white and dark. Pilau is the Swahili coast's own; biryani came down the sea road with Gujarati and Omani traders and has been made here long enough to be native.
★ The tell: look at the colour distribution before you taste. Uniformly brown rice with spice cooked into it is pilau; streaked rice is biryani. Uniformly yellow rice with spice sprinkled on afterwards is neither, and you should eat somewhere else.
WhereLukmaan, Stone Town — both, at the counter.
#7
BREAD

Mkate wa kumimina THE POURED BREAD

'The bread that is poured'. Rice is soaked and ground to a batter with coconut milk, left to ferment until it is faintly sour and alive, then poured into a pot and baked over coals with fire above and below until it sets — soft, spongy, slightly tangy, somewhere between a bread and a set custard. No wheat. No oven. It is a household bread rather than a restaurant one, made at breakfast and heavily through Ramadan, and it is the clearest single statement of what this island's cooking actually is: rice, coconut and time.
★ The tell: the sourness. A properly fermented batter gives a distinct tang against the coconut sweetness. A version made in a hurry from unfermented batter is just a sweet rice cake, and you will only know the difference by having eaten the real one.
WhereLukmaan's counter when it is on; ask in Nungwi village kitchens — it is a morning and Ramadan bread, so you have to be lucky.
#8
SPICE

Karafuu THE CLOVE

A clove is not a seed or a bark — it is an unopened flower bud, picked green by hand before it opens and dried on mats in the sun until it darkens and rattles. Sultan Said's decree of three clove trees for every coconut palm made this archipelago the source of roughly nine-tenths of the world's supply by the end of the nineteenth century. Almost everything savoury on this island has one in it somewhere, and the ones in pilau are usually left whole so you can pick them out.
★ The tell: press one with a thumbnail before you pay. A fresh bud is oily and will leave a mark on paper; a dry, pale, weightless one has been sitting in a jar for two years and belongs in a gift shop, which is where it will be sold to you in a printed box for many times the price of the same cloves loose on the scale.
WhereGrowing at Kizimbani; bought loose by weight at Darajani market.
#9
DRINK

Madafu THE GREEN NUT

The immature coconut, still green, topped with three strokes of a panga and drunk straight from the husk. It is the coast's original soft drink and the only cold thing here that needs no cold chain at all — a litre of sterile, faintly sweet, mildly salty water sealed by the tree until the moment it is opened. On Kendwa sand it is a working trade, and the vendors walk the beach all day with a sack and a machete.
★ The tell: what you do afterwards. Hand the empty nut back and ask for it to be split — the vendor cuts a spoon out of the shell and you scoop the soft jelly inside. A madafu drunk and thrown away is half a madafu, and asking for the spoon is the fastest way to stop being treated as someone who does not know.
WhereKendwa and Nungwi beaches, from any vendor with a panga. Agree the price first; the opening number in front of a five-star gate is not the rate.
#10
DRINK

Konyagi, and the drinking question THE HONEST ONE

Tanzania's own spirit, distilled from sugar cane, sold as 'the spirit of the nation' and drunk long with tonic and lime; the beers are Kilimanjaro, Safari and Serengeti, all light lagers built for this heat. But the more useful fact is the geography of where you can drink them. This is a Muslim island: alcohol is served on the beach strip and in hotels and is essentially absent from the village two hundred metres inland, and during Ramadan it is restricted further and often only available after iftar.
★ The tell: Konyagi is stronger than it drinks — it goes down like a soft gin and does not behave like one. And the real tell is social: carrying an open bottle off the sand and into Nungwi village is the single most reliable way to mark yourself as someone who has not looked up where they are.
