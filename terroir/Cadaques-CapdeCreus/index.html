The name From the Catalan Cap de quers, the cape of crags — or, some say, from the prickly juniper of the slopes.
Quer is an old word for a rock or a crag, and medieval charters write the village Cadaqués and Cadaquers side by side from the eleventh century. A rival reading hears càdec, the prickly juniper that grows on the schist. Neither has won; both describe the place.

Cadaqués is a fishing village at the end of a bad road, and that is the whole secret: cut off by land for centuries, it kept its own Catalan, its own fish and a light that drew a century of painters. The soul of the place — the essay
A white village against the wind, a family of painters, a cove that changed cooking, and what the summer pays for
THE ESSAY

Cadaqués is a white village stacked on grey schist at the easternmost edge of the Iberian Peninsula, where the Pyrenees run into the sea. For most of its history the only ways in were by boat or a mule track over the Pení, and that isolation kept three things alive: an old Catalan still spoken in its lanes, a long habit of contraband, and a light so hard and clear that the painters of a century came down one bad road to paint it.

The wind made the village. The tramuntana, the dry north wind off the mountains, scours the cape and the bay, bends the olive terraces and files the folded rock into the shapes Dalí copied; the houses answer it with thick white walls turned away from the gale. Beauty here is not soft. It is earned against the wind, and what lasts is whatever learns to turn its back to it.

Then a single family changed the guest list. The Pichots of Cadaqués brought Picasso for the summer of 1910, and their canvases showed a local boy named Salvador Dalí what painting could do; he made his life at Portlligat, over the hill. Marcel Duchamp came back every summer from 1958 until his death in 1968, to see the Dalís and to play chess at a café on the front — the quietest celebrity the village ever had. The rest of the story, from Lorca to the summer of 1929, is on the colony’s card.

The quintessence. On a still summer night the havaneres are sung by the water and a clay pot of cremat — dark rum set alight with coffee, sugar and cinnamon — goes round the table. That flame, and the anchovy cured down the coast, are the village reminding you that it was a fishing port long before it was a painter’s shrine.

Where it comes from

Everything grew on the rock. The cape is the last knuckle of the Pyrenees, a schist far older than the animals with shells, and the village lived off the sea below it: the monks of Sant Pere de Rodes bought its fisheries in the eleventh century, corsairs burned it, and contraband with France kept it fed. The curiosities below have the dates.

The table answers the rock. Violet sea urchins prised from the schist in the cold months, the anchovy salted at L’Escala since the Greeks, the fishermen’s suquet bound with a picada of almonds and saffron — and half an hour over the headland, at Cala Montjoi, the kitchen that rewired modern cooking, told just below. Its afterlife cooks on the Riera at Compartir.

Where the money sits

Cadaqués runs on summer. The Dalí pilgrimage to Portlligat and the August crowd fund the year; the fishing fleet that once salted the anchovy is a shadow of what it was, and out of season the village shrinks back towards its residents.

So the tables on the front are priced for visitors, and the honest value sits one street back: the family houses that never moved with the market, the anchovy bought from the salters, the day-boat catch that rarely reaches a printed menu in August. A chef provisioning here spends on the cured anchovy and the wine of the slate hills, and wastes money on a harbourside platter in high season. In October the prices, and the cooking, tell the truth again.

And the clock is the season and the wind. The terraces fill from Easter and thin from late October; the sea urchin comes in with the cold; the tramuntana blows hardest from late autumn to spring. Whoever comes in May, June, September or October gets the swim, the open kitchens and a road that moves.

elBulli — the cove that changed cooking
Half an hour over the headland, a kitchen at Cala Montjoi rewired what food could be — and its afterlife cooks on the Riera
THE STORY

Half an hour over the headland, in a cove called Cala Montjoi above Roses, Ferran Adrià ran the restaurant that rewired what food could be.

He took sole charge of elBulli’s kitchen in 1987 and — with the manager Juli Soler and his pastry-chef brother Albert — turned it into a laboratory of foams, airs and “deconstruction.” Three Michelin stars in 1997; named the best restaurant in the world five times (2002, 2006, 2007, 2008, 2009); more than two million people a year chasing roughly eight thousand seats. It served its last dinner on 30 July 2011, then reopened in 2023 as elBulli1846 — a museum on the same cove, the number counting both the dishes invented there and the birth year of Escoffier.

The line runs straight back to this bay. Three of Adrià’s chefs — Oriol Castro, Eduard Xatruch and Mateu Casañas — opened Compartir in Cadaqués in 2012; their Barcelona room, Disfrutar, was named the best restaurant in the world in 2024. Eat at the middle of Compartir’s table and you are tasting elBulli’s afterlife, half an hour from where it began.

Adrià’s rule travelled with them: technique in the service of the product in front of you. The museum on the cove keeps a season and a timed ticket: its card is under Autour. The table on the bay is Compartir, in the grand lane.

Cadaqués reads as one white crescent round its bay, climbing from the water up lanes of slate set on edge to the church. Everything is on foot; only Portlligat over the rise and the cape, further east, need a walk or a wheel.

The white town

The slate lanes up to the church, the front where the village meets the bay, and the dry riverbed that carries the market and the cars.

Santa Maria and the upper townold townLanes paved with slate stood on edge, climbing to a white church that watches the whole bay

Leave the front by any alley and the ground changes under your feet. The upper town is paved in rastell: slabs of local slate set on edge in tight rows, so that storm water runs down the joints instead of tearing up the street. The lanes are barely three or four metres wide, whitewash close on both sides, doors and shutters painted, bougainvillea over the lintels. They wind uphill to the terrace of Santa Maria, where the roofs fall away below you and the bay opens out towards the cape. Walk it early, before the day visitors, when the loudest thing in the lane is a broom on slate.

Why go. Climb to the church terrace for the whole bay laid out under the roofs, then come down by a different lane.

The Riera de Sant Vicenç is a torrent bed that comes down through the middle of the village to the bay, and it is dry almost all year. It earns its name only in a cloudburst, when water off the hills runs down it fast. Along the bay

The little shore of boats the teenage Dalí painted, and the twin rock beaches where his family spent its summers.

Es Poalfishermen's shoreStand on the pebbles where a teenage Dalí set his easel, and line the bay up with his 1920 canvas

Es Poal is one of the small pebble shores strung along the bay, where boats are still hauled up on the stones. In 1920, aged about sixteen and still painting in an impressionist hand, Salvador Dalí set up here for 'View of Cadaqués from Playa Poal': the white village across the water, the church on its hump, boats in the foreground. Stand on the beach and the composition is still in front of you. Around it the promenade does what it always did at this end of the front: terraces, ice cream, the aperitif while the boats come in. It is free, and every walk along the bay passes it.

Why go. Pull the painting up on your phone and match it to the real view from the stones.

Es Llanérock beachesTwo flat-rock beaches five minutes south, where the Dalí family spent its summers before Portlligat

Walk five minutes south of the centre and the town loosens into Es Llané: two urban beaches, Gran and Petit, of pebbles and flat schist ledges you can stretch out on. Llané Gran is a strip of roughly 130 by 12 metres. Behind this shore was the Dalí family's summer house, the one the young painter set behind his father in 'Portrait of my father and the house at Es Llaner' (1920). The boy spent his summers here long before Portlligat, and Federico García Lorca stayed in the house at Easter 1925; the colony card tells the rest. This is simply where to swim off the rocks.

Why go. Swim from the flat rocks early, while the water is still and the beach belongs to the village.

Over the hill

The fishermen’s cove where Dalí made his house.

Portlligatfishing hamletOver the hill from town, a closed cove of moored boats, a few white houses and a very still evening

Take the road over the rise north of the village and in about 1.2 km Cadaqués is gone. Portlligat is a small, nearly closed bay with an island across its mouth, fishing boats moored in the shallows and a handful of whitewashed houses along the shingle. Dalí lived here for half a century; his house has its own card, and so does the beach. What is left for this one is the hamlet itself: nets and boats, the walk over and back, and the end of the day, when the wind drops, the bay goes flat and the boats sit on their own reflections.

Why go. Walk over in the late afternoon, sit by the boats as the bay stills, and walk back to town for dinner.

The rock, the wind, the tongue

Why the cape looks the way it does, the wind that decides every day, and the Catalan that kept its old articles.

The rock older than shellscuriosityThe cape's schist was laid down as the first shelled animals appeared, then folded as continents collided

The grey rock under Cadaqués began as mud on an ancient sea floor some 580 to 540 million years ago, around the time the first animals with hard shells were appearing. Then it was crushed, folded and partly melted in the Variscan collision of continents, roughly 300 million years ago. That heat drove white pegmatite veins through the dark mica-schist: the pale stripes you see in every cove of the cape. The tramuntana has been carving the folds ever since. Geologists come from all over to read this coast, and since 1998 it has been protected, land and sea, as the Parc Natural de Cap de Creus.

Why go. Put your hand on a white pegmatite stripe in the dark rock: that is a 300-million-year-old crack filled with crystal.

Touch it today
The white pegmatite veins in the grey schist at any cove of the cape; the Espai Cap de Creus at the lighthouse explains what you are looking at (its own card)
Where the sources argue
The old guide dates the folding to 310-280 million years ago in one place and 'some 300' in another; 'older than shells' is a round claim that holds only for the older end of the 580-540 million-year range, since shelled animals spread from about 540 million years ago. The park's area (13,886 ha, from Viquipèdia) was not re-checked
The tramuntanacuriosityThe north wind built the town: houses turn their backs to it, and it scrubs the sky clear as glass

The tramuntana is the cold, dry north wind that pours off the Pyrenees and down across the Empordà, and Cap de Creus, the last spur of the range, takes it head on. It can blow for days, hardest in winter and early spring. It shaped the town: low houses with thick walls, limewashed white, turning blank backs to the north. It shaped the cape, scouring the rock into the forms Dalí painted. And it makes the light. When the tramuntana has blown the haze out to sea, the air turns so clear that the far shore of the gulf seems close enough to swim to.

Why go. On a north-wind day, skip the boat and climb high: the clearest light of the year is the wind's doing.

Touch it today
Mid-October is the start of its season: on a tramuntana day, stand on the church terrace or at the cape and watch the sea go white offshore under a clean sky (boat days are the warning card's business)
Where the sources argue
Guides say it 'gusts past 100 km/h' here. Meteocat warnings for the Alt Empordà do announce gusts above 100 km/h (March 2026), and Portbou, up the coast, logged about 120 km/h in a February 2026 storm, but no published record figure for Cap de Creus itself was found
Parlar salatcuriosityHere the beach is 'sa platja': the salted article of the Balearics survives at the end of the cape

Listen on the quay and in the shops and you may catch it: es instead of el, sa instead of la. This is the 'salted' article, built from the Latin ipse where standard Catalan took ille. Medieval texts show it across much of Catalonia; on the mainland it retreated almost everywhere, and today it is above all the article of the Balearic Islands. Cadaqués, reachable for centuries mainly by sea, held on to it, and it is written all over the map: Sa Riba, Es Poal, Es Llané, Sa Conca, Es Portitxó. The place names keep it even where everyday speech has moved on.

Why go. Read the names of the beaches and streets aloud: you are speaking the village's own Catalan.

Touch it today
The beach and street names (Sa Riba, Es Poal, Es Llané, Sa Conca), and the talk at the Monday market
Where the sources argue
The old guide called Cadaqués the only mainland town where salat is everyday speech. Not so: Tàrbena and the Vall de Gallinera, inland Alicante villages resettled from Mallorca after 1610, kept it too, and Tàrbena holds a festival for it. Within Catalonia, Cadaqués is the best-known survival; no source found measures how many villagers still use it daily

Monks, corsairs and contraband

Who owned the fish, who burned the town, and what a cut-off village lived on.

The monks bought the fishcuriosityIn 1030 the abbey on the mountain bought its rights here, and for centuries the bay fed the monks

Cadaqués enters the written record as property. A donation of 974 names it, and in 1030 the abbot of Sant Pere de Rodes, the Benedictine monastery high on the Verdera ridge across the peninsula, bought rights over the place from the counts of Roussillon, sharing the lordship with the counts of Empúries. In the reading the old guide gives, those were rights to the fisheries and ports, and the bay's catch went up the mountain to the monks' table for centuries. The monastery visit is the Sant Pere de Rodes card's; here it is enough to know that the fish on your plate once had an abbot for a landlord.

Why go. Order the day's fish from the bay and think of the thousand-year-old deed behind it.

Touch it today
The day's catch at any table in the guide; the monastery itself is a day trip with its own card
Where the sources argue
A tourism history says the 1030 purchase was of rights over the place, bought from the counts of Roussillon and shared with the counts of Empúries; the old guide says rights to its fisheries and ports. The 974 donation comes from the old guide only and was not re-checked
Burned in 1444, sacked in 1543curiosityRaiders burned the town and its records in 1444; a century later Barbarossa's fleet came for the church

A town at the tip of a peninsula, with no garrison and a good harbour, was an easy mark. A document in the municipal archive records that in 1444 raiding galleys set Cadaqués on fire and burned its papers, one reason so little of its medieval past is written down. In 1543 it happened again: a fleet of some twenty-five ships under the corsair Barbarossa put the town to the torch, and the parish church of Santa Maria burned with much of the village. Cadaqués rebuilt its walls, and on the same site raised the larger church you climb to today, whose story is the Santa Maria card's.

Why go. Stand at Santa Maria knowing it is the church that rose from the 1543 ashes.

Touch it today
Climb to Santa Maria, the rebuilding that followed 1543 (its own card)
Where the sources argue
The old guide says 'Moorish galleys' burned the town in 1444, citing the municipal archive; a tourism history just says it was burned. For 1543, tourism histories name Barbarossa's fleet of 25 ships, while an article in the Annals de l'Institut d'Estudis Empordanesos speaks of North African pirates under 'Barba-roja'; whether Barbarossa came in person is not settled by these sources
A smugglers' villagecuriosityCut off by land and close to France, Cadaqués made contraband a trade, then lost its vines in 1879

For most of its history Cadaqués faced the sea because it had no choice: by land there was only a mule track over the Pení. The French border lies just up the coast beyond Portbou, and contraband became a settled trade, landed by boat in coves that had no road. In the 19th century the village grew rich another way, on wine from terraces cut into the schist, until phylloxera reached the Empordà in 1879 and killed the vines. Drive out towards the cape today and the dry-stone terrace walls still step up the bare slopes: a vineyard landscape with no vines left in it.

Why go. Look at the empty terraces on the cape road and picture the slopes green with vines.

Touch it today
The abandoned dry-stone terraces on the slopes along the road to Cap de Creus
Where the sources argue
When the road came: the old guide says both 'the first road in the late 19th century' and 'no easy road until the 20th century', and no source found this week dates the road over the Pení. The 1879 phylloxera date is the old guide's, not re-checked

Once a week the car park becomes a village market again. The anchovy salters of L'Escalasalt-cured anchovyForty kilometres down the coast, two family houses still fillet salted anchovies by hand

L'Escala, about forty kilometres south of Cadaqués, has salted fish since Greek traders landed at Empúries in the 6th century BC, and in 1697 it finished the Alfolí de la Sal, its warehouse for the salt. Two family firms keep the trade: Fills de J. Callol i Serrats, founded 1847 and the oldest in town, and Solés, from 1888. The anchovies come in on the spring run, roughly March to June, sit packed in salt for months and are then filleted by hand. At Callol i Serrats you phone ahead and are walked through the salting before a tasting with local wine; Solés runs a factory-museum with an audioguide.

Why go. Buy the 'Anxoves de l'Escala' at the source: hand-filleted anchovy is the one thing worth spending on for the galley here.

The wine on the slate

The estate inside the natural park, the biodynamic valley of La Selva de Mar, and the big house of Vilajuïga.

Martín Faixócape slate wineThe one winery with vines on the cape itself: slate, tramuntana, and a view down to the bay

Rafa Martín and Carme Faixó opened Can Rafa on the Passeig in the early 1980s (sources say 1981 or 1982); in 2000 the family planted vines at Mas Sa Perafita, on the slate hill above the road into Cadaqués and inside the Cap de Creus natural park. The first wines came in 2007, made by their daughter Ester. Garnatxa, Cabernet, Merlot, Picapoll and Muscat of Alexandria grow on wind-scoured slopes, sold as Perafita and Cadac. For twenty years the family fought for permits to dig an underground cellar into the hill; Ara reported in October 2025 that building would begin in 2026.

Why go. Taste the Perafita white on the hill where it grows, then order it at Can Rafa (its own card is in the tables).

The Dish — where the list comes from

Seven plates and a glass, with their honest passports: what the rock gives, what the coast claims, and what belongs further south.

ORIGIN

Garotes

The violet sea urchin clamped to the schist, called garota in the Alt Empordà and garoïna further south. The shell is cut open across the mouth side, the dark insides rinsed out, and the five orange tongues of roe eaten raw on bread with a spoon — or folded into eggs as ous amb garotes. A cold-month harvest, when the roe is full. Origin. The rocks of Cap de Creus and the whole Costa Brava

Eat it — La Sirena, January to March
ORIGIN

Anxoves de l'Escala

Anchovy packed in salt for months and filleted by hand, never cooked: desalted briefly and dressed with Empordà oil, eaten on toast. The salting trade at L’Escala is traced to the Greeks of Empúries; two family houses still cure under the town’s quality mark. Origin. L’Escala, forty kilometres down the coast

Eat it — Casa Anita, salted in the house
ORIGIN

Suquet de peix

The fishermen’s stew: the day’s rockfish and potatoes over a slow sofregit of onion and tomato, finished in the last minutes with a picada of toasted almonds, garlic, fried bread, parsley and saffron pounded in the mortar — the picada thickens and seasons at once. Origin. Claimed by the Empordà and by Tarragona

Eat it — Es Baluard, with monkfish
ORIGIN

Arròs negre

Rice blackened by cuttlefish ink and by an onion sofregit cooked almost to burning, in an earthenware cassola, served with allioli. The Empordà’s flagship rice; the textbook version is from the Baix Empordà. Origin. The Empordà

Eat it — Es Balconet, the black paella
ORIGIN

Peix de roca

Scorpionfish, bream, John Dory and the small fish that live in the slate, griddled or baked whole with oil, garlic and parsley so the firmness of a line-caught fish carries the plate. Priced by weight; ask what came in. Origin. Cadaqués, catch-dependent

Eat it — Es Baluard or Casa Anita, by weight
ORIGIN

Es niu

A five-hour winter stew of cod tripe, stockfish, hard egg and potato over a long sofregit, later with cuttlefish and game birds: a Lenten fishermen’s dish of the cork country. It belongs to Palafrugell, not to Cadaqués, but it is the same coast’s winter table. Origin. Palafrugell, Baix Empordà — not a Cadaqués dish

ORIGIN

Cremat

Dark rum heated in a clay pot with sugar, cinnamon, lemon peel and coffee beans, set alight and stirred until about a third of the alcohol has burned away, then doused with coffee and served hot. It came home with the Catalan traders of the Cuba run. Origin. The Costa Brava; heartland Calella de Palafrugell

Eat it — the Marítim, at the table
