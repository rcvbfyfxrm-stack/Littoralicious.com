The name The Algarve is al-Gharb, “the west” in Arabic; Faro’s own name is still argued.
For five centuries this coast was the western edge of the Islamic world, al-Gharb al-Andalus, and the name stayed after 1249. Faro is less certain. One story says it is the Portuguese for a lighthouse; the more often cited one says it comes from Harune, the family who held the town before the Reconquest, through a Christian name, Santa Maria de Harune. The Romans called it Ossonoba. The sources argue; the lagoon does not care.

Faro is the Algarve’s capital and its airport, a walled town on a lagoon that most visitors drive straight past: the city market’s bifana in the morning, the fish hall of Olhão, clams and razor clams from the Ria Formosa, a cataplana sealed in its copper, Negra Mole by the glass and medronho after. The soul of the place — the essay
A Roman fish port, a Moorish town, a bishop’s city rebuilt after the earthquake, and a lagoon that still feeds it
THE ESSAY

Faro was a port before it was a town. The Romans called it Ossonoba and shipped fish sauce and amphorae from this lagoon; at Milreu, ten kilometres inland, a rich man paved his villa with fish in mosaic. The lagoon has fed the place ever since.

The Moors held the town from the eighth century until 1249, when Afonso III took it and finished the Portuguese reconquest of the Algarve; they left the region its name and the town its walls. What happened inside those walls next — a Hebrew book printed in 1487, an English earl who carried the bishop’s library to Oxford in 1596 — has cards of its own, below. What matters at table is that Faro grew as a market for the lagoon: salt, clams, oysters and fish carried in from the islands and sold at its doors.

The earthquake of 1755 brought down much of the town, and the bishops rebuilt it, which is why the gate you walk through is a neoclassical arch of 1812 set over an older one. Then came the railway, the canneries along the coast, and, from the 1960s, the airport and the resorts to the west, which turned the Algarve into a holiday and left Faro to be the place you land.

The quintessence. The kitchen is the lagoon and the hills behind it. The Ria Formosa gives clams, cockles, razor clams and oysters; the boats of Olhão bring sardines, octopus and cuttlefish; the barrocal behind the coast gives almonds, figs, carob and oranges, and the Serra de Monchique the berries of the strawberry tree. So the canon is short: grilled fish and sardines in summer, clams with garlic and coriander, a wet rice of razor clams, the cataplana when someone has time, octopus, and a convent sweet of egg and almond to finish.

Where it comes from

The old town is small — fifteen minutes from one gate to the other — and quiet after dark, when the people who live in it come out to the Largo da Sé. The life of the town is a few streets north, in the Baixa: the pedestrian Rua de Santo António, the cafés of the Jardim, the market hall, and the tascas a street back.

The wine nearly died here. The cooperatives of the twentieth century turned the Algarve into a producer of anonymous bulk, the old grape Negra Mole fell out of favour, and the land went to hotels. The recovery has come one estate at a time; the bottles to ask for are on the cards at the glass.

And for centuries the richest catch was the one that came past in spring: the bluefin tuna, taken in great net traps off Tavira. That story is told in full in the fold below.

Where the money sits

The money on this coast sits west of Faro, in the resorts and golf estates between Almancil and Albufeira, where the starred kitchens are. Faro itself lives on the airport, the university and the region’s offices, and its marina menus are written for people waiting for a flight.

That is the line you can taste. The value is in the market hall before ten, the fish hall of Olhão on a Saturday, a tasca with a chalk board, the boat across the lagoon to a lunch of grilled fish, and a glass of Negra Mole poured by someone who knows whose vines it came from.

And the clock is the season. From June to September the boats run full, the sardines are grilled in the street and the beaches fill; from November the island boats go on winter timetables, a few kitchens close until spring, the stills of Monchique run and the town has its streets to itself. Whoever comes in June gets the sardines and Santo António; whoever comes in late autumn gets the clams at their best, the first oranges and a lagoon with almost nobody on it.

1966 — The year the Barril tuna trap went quiet
How the Algarve caught the bluefin on its run past the coast, canned it in Vila Real and Olhão, and what is left on the beach at Barril.
THE STORY

For centuries the Algarve set walls of net across the bluefin tuna's path as it ran along the coast to spawn. At Praia do Barril, near Tavira, the Armação dos Três Irmãos worked from 1841 to 1966. Its fishermen's houses still stand behind the dunes, and some 250 anchors lie in the sand where they were left.

The armação, called almadraba in Spain, is a fixed trap: a long net wall moored by anchors that steered the passing tuna into a chain of chambers, where the fish were hauled up by hand. Sources trace it to Phoenician and Carthaginian settlers, with fixed traps worked under Arab rule. A royal order of 1499 told Lagos to build salting tanks for 3,000 tuna. Traps lined the coast from Vila Real de Santo António to Aljezur and stayed an important income until the catches collapsed in the 1960s and 1970s.

The trap at Barril, the Armação dos Três Irmãos, worked from 1841 to 1966; its arraial, the fishermen's camp, still stands. Up the lagoon, the Arraial Ferreira Neto at Tavira was designed by the engineer Sena Lino in 1943 and opened about 1945. Its archive records the last tuna caught there in 1972, so Barril and Ferreira Neto both claim the last trap. The camp is now a hotel with a small tuna-fishing museum. In 2026 a modern operator, Real Atunara, reported bluefin captures off Barril again.

Tuna also built an industry. In Vila Real de Santo António, founded in 1774, the Andalusian Sebastián Ramirez began in 1853 what his family calls Portugal's first cannery; it moved to Matosinhos in the 1940s. A local writer credits the Genoese Ângelo Parodi with the first tuna-in-oil factory there, the Santa Maria. RTP counted about 200 Algarve canneries in the 1940s and three survivors; Olhão once had 37. Conserveira do Sul, founded there in 1950 or 1954, still cans fish; Portimão's museum fills the old Feu Hermanos cannery.

Taste what is left of the catch where the boats still land: the fish hall of the Mercado de Olhão, in the morning.

The record, corrected

A Hebrew book, an English earl's loot, and a fried bean.

Portugal's first printed bookcuriosityPrinting in Portugal began in Faro in 1487, in Hebrew, nine years before the Jews were expelled

Ask where Portuguese printing began and most people say Lisbon or Coimbra. The answer is Faro, and the language is Hebrew. On 30 June 1487 the press of Don Samuel Gacon finished a Pentateuch, the five books of Moses, set in square Hebrew letters with vowel points. One copy survives. It passed through Jewish hands in 16th-century Mantua, then the Almanzi collection in Padua, and reached the British Museum in the 1860s; it is now at the British Library. In December 1496 King Manuel I ordered the Jews out of Portugal, and Faro's press fell silent. In 2017, 530 years on, a facsimile was launched in Faro.

Why go. It turns the usual story upside down: the first book printed in Portugal was not in Portuguese and not printed in the capital.

Touch it today
The Centro Histórico Judaico de Faro, with the Jewish cemetery of a later community and the Isaac Bitton museum, on Rua Leão Penedo by the hospital; listed open weekdays 09:30-12:30 and 14:00-17:00 (Oct 2026 listings): phone ahead. The book itself is in London, at the British Library
Where the sources argue
A 2004 auction catalogue suggests the press belonged to Don Samuel Porteiro, with Gacon as backer. And 'first book printed in Portugal' is not 'first book in Portuguese': that title usually goes to the Tratado de Confissom, printed at Chaves in 1489, itself debated

The bishop's library at OxfordcuriosityEssex sacked Faro in 1596 and carried off the bishop's books; they ended up on the Bodleian's first shelves

In July 1596, returning from the raid on Cádiz, the Earl of Essex landed at Faro and burned it. Among the loot was the library of the bishop, Fernando Martins Mascarenhas: Latin theology and law, many volumes stamped with his arms on the binding. Essex gave the books to his friend Thomas Bodley, who was restocking Oxford's university library, and they sat among the first books when the Bodleian opened in 1602. They are still there; some have been digitised and can be paged through online. In Faro, the Faro 1540 association keeps asking for them back.

Why go. Few cities can point to their own stolen library on the founding shelves of one of the world's great libraries.

Tempura went from herecuriosityJapan's tempura may carry a Portuguese word in its name: whose word, and which one, is still argued

Portuguese ships reached Japan in the 1540s; Jesuits and traders stayed until 1639. The story told in every Portuguese tasca is that they brought frying in batter with them. On the têmporas, the Ember days of the church calendar, Catholics went without meat and ate fried vegetables and fish instead; the Japanese, the story goes, heard the word and kept it as tempura. The Portuguese dish usually named as the ancestor is peixinhos da horta, 'little fish from the garden': green beans dipped in batter and fried. A good story with a real core: the Portuguese presence is certain, the exact route is not.

Why go. Order peixinhos da horta in Faro and taste the argument for yourself: thin batter, a green bean, a little salt.

Touch it today
Peixinhos da horta on the petiscos list of a Faro tasca; the dish itself is The Dish's card
Where the sources argue
Têmpora (Ember days, Latin tempora) is the most cited source of the word; tempero or temperar (seasoning, to season) is the main rival, and some say the question cannot be settled. The link to peixinhos da horta specifically is popular but thinly documented

The storks of the ArcocuriosityAn 1812 arch, an Arab horseshoe door inside it, and storks that have stopped flying to Africa

Look up at the old town gate: a white stork stands on an enormous nest on top of the Arco da Vila. The neoclassical arch below was opened in 1812, commissioned by Bishop Francisco Gomes do Avelar from Francisco Xavier Fabri, the Italian architect who rebuilt much of the Algarve after 1755; a marble Saint Thomas Aquinas stands in its niche. Walk through and you meet the older gate behind it, a horseshoe-arched door of Arab origin, beside a niche recalling a Marian miracle legend. The storks were once summer visitors; since the mid-1980s more and more stay all winter, some on their nests in December.

Why go. Stand in the passage and look both ways: neoclassical marble on the town side, the Islamic gate within, and a stork above both.

Ask for it by name

The Algarve's own red, the white that keeps its acid, and the late-ripening white.

Negra Molethe Algarve redAn old grape with pink, red and black berries on one bunch, poured pale, almost as a rosé

Negra Mole is the grape the Algarve kept. The regional wine commission calls it the second-oldest Portuguese variety after Sercial, and its bunches ripen unevenly, pink, red and near-black berries side by side. That gives a pale, low-tannin wine of fresh red fruit, the old palhete and clarete of Algarve tables. In the late 20th century it disappeared into anonymous blends, and many vineyards were pulled up for resorts and golf. From about 2010 growers began bottling it alone again, now as light reds, rosés and traditional-method sparklers: Cabrita in Silves makes a blanc de noirs and a rosé from it, Morgado do Quintão an espumante.

Why go. Ask for it slightly cool with grilled fish: it is the red that does not fight the sea.

The spirit of the strawberry tree, and the marc.

Medronhomountain spiritClear and strong, distilled from the wild red berries of the strawberry tree in the Monchique hills

The strawberry tree, medronheiro, grows wild on the schist hills of the Serra de Monchique, and in autumn its round berries turn from yellow to deep red. Families pick them, leave them to ferment for weeks, about two months for one Monchique maker, then distil the mash in copper stills over the winter, often into February and March. The result is clear, very strong and smells of ripe fruit and the hillside; aged in wood, it turns gold. The old guide, citing DGADR, gives it a protected name, Medronho do Algarve IGP; the regional wine commission was named its certifier in 2016. The still itself: see Casa do Medronho.

Why go. A small glass after coffee, at room temperature, in a tasca that buys from a hill family.

On the water

The engines in the lagoon.

On the water

The Atlantic is cooling from about 20C, and the lagoon boats are on short winter timetables.

The sea in late October and Novembersea temperatureAbout 18–20C in November, down from about 21C in October: an Atlantic swim, not a Mediterranean one

Faro's sea is Atlantic water, and it cools through the autumn. Holiday-weather gives an average of 21C for October falling to 20C in November; other climate guides put mid-November nearer 18 to 19C, with a range of about 18 to 21C. These are long-run averages from travel and forecast sites, not a buoy reading. The ocean beaches of the barrier islands, Ilha de Faro, Deserta, Culatra and Armona, face the open Atlantic. The air drops fast once the sun goes, and from 25 October it goes an hour earlier.

Why go. Swim at midday on a calm day in the sun, and keep a wetsuit aboard for anyone who feels the cold.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates and their honest passports: which are the lagoon’s own, which the coast shares, and which the convents and the rest of the country sent in.

ORIGIN

Cataplana

Clams, prawns, a white fish, sliced chouriço, peppers, onion, garlic, white wine and coriander, sealed inside the domed copper pot — two halves hinged like a shell — and steamed over a single flame for about half an hour. The lid is lifted at the table and the steam carries the lagoon. The vessel and the dish share a name; if it arrives open in fifteen minutes, it is a stew. Origin. The Algarve; the pot itself is hammered by the coppersmiths of Loulé

Eat it — Tasca do Ricky, Rua do Forno
ORIGIN

Arroz de lingueirão

The razor clam of the Ria Formosa lives upright in the sandy banks; the harvesters pour salt into its breathing hole and it comes up out of the sand. The rice is cooked in a deep shellfish stock and served wet, the broth pooling in the dish, the clams giving it a long mineral finish. Origin. The Ria Formosa

Eat it — Estaminé, on the island, or Tasca do Ricky
ORIGIN

Amêijoas à Bulhão Pato

The good lagoon clam, opened in seconds in olive oil with a lot of crushed garlic, fresh coriander, lemon and a splash of white wine. Nearly the simplest dish on the menu and the clearest test of whether the clams are good. It carries the name of the 19th-century poet Raimundo António de Bulhão Pato. Origin. Portugal-wide; the clams are the Ria Formosa's

Eat it — Tasca do Ricky; the clams themselves at the Mercado de Olhão
ORIGIN

Polvo

Octopus taken in traps in the lagoon and off the coast — once in clay pots, now mostly in cage traps baited with sardine — and served grilled with potatoes, cooked in a wet rice, or cold as a salad with onion, peppers and coriander. Santa Luzia, by Tavira, calls itself the octopus capital, and its lagoon front is stacked with the traps. Origin. Santa Luzia and the eastern lagoon

Eat it — the fish hall of the Mercado de Olhão, to cook aboard
ORIGIN

Sardinhas assadas

Atlantic sardines grilled whole over open charcoal, bone in, outside the tascas in summer, and laid on bread with a salad of tomato and pepper so the bread takes the oil. They are at their fattest from June, the month of Santo António; the smoke is part of the dish. Out of season they are frozen: do not order them in winter. Origin. The whole Portuguese coast

ORIGIN

Peixinhos da horta

Long green beans dipped in a light batter and fried — ‘little fish from the garden’, a dish for the days without meat. The story that Portuguese missionaries took it to Japan, where it became tempura, is told on its own card, with what the sources argue. Origin. Portugal-wide

Eat it — A Venda, Rua do Compromisso — call in winter
ORIGIN

Choco frito

Cuttlefish cut in strips, briefly marinated, floured and fried fast. The test is the texture: tender means it was cooked a minute, rubbery means it was cooked five. It is the civic dish of Setúbal, to the north, and has travelled south along the coast. Origin. Setúbal, adopted down the coast

Eat it — Adega Nova, by the station
ORIGIN

Dom Rodrigo

Threads of egg yolk cooked in syrup, bound with almond, sugar and cinnamon, rolled and wrapped in coloured foil twisted at both ends. A convent sweet of the Algarve, said to be named for a governor of the region; Lagos claims it. In December 2025 it was given a national protected geographical indication, and the European registration is pending. Origin. The Algarve's convents; Lagos claims it
