The soul of Vienna — the essay
A city that sits down, a kitchen whose legends are audited, and a river that was moved
THE ESSAY

A Viennese afternoon is a table you do not have to leave. You order one coffee, a glass of cold water comes with it, and the room is yours until you ask for the bill. The country put its own name to that in the inventory of living heritage, and the sentence its commission chose is the best description of the place:

A place where time and space are consumed, but only the coffee is on the bill.Austrian Commission for UNESCO, on Wiener Kaffeehauskultur in the national inventory

Time is part of the price, everywhere. The wine taverns on the northern slopes open by calendar, when the growers hang the pine bush out; the sausage stand stays open for the late crowd and keeps a champagne list; the Beisl runs a lunch plate for the price of a sandwich and a card that is rewritten every day. Eat where the Viennese sit down, and sit as they do.

The kitchen is an empire's, and its legends are being audited. A Viennese menu is a map of the old crown lands, and the great houses argue over who invented what. The city's own historians have spent years checking: several of the famous origin stories do not survive the paper trail. This guide tells each one, the legend first and the record after.

The river was moved. Vienna is landlocked, and a sailor should be exact about what that leaves: a Danube dug into a straight channel in the 1870s, the old arm turned into a dinghy lake, a canal with a lock, and a steppe lake an hour south that is about a metre deep. The old hands' stories are told where you can still touch them, from a column of bronze ships' beaks to a steamer line that is on a low-water timetable this autumn.

Then the stove. Three MICHELIN stars in a park pavilion and in a winery, a class of new one-star rooms in 2026, and under them a layer of houses, Beisln and counters where the dish is the point.

Come for St Martin's week, when the young wine is poured and goose is on the card; the Christmas market opens on 13 November.

The city's food history is easiest to read as the history of who fed it: a Roman garrison, a court that pulled cooks from five crown lands, and a municipality that built homes. The stories below are the ones the table still carries.

From camp to Ring

Vindobona: the legion camp under the Old TownRomanThe Old Town street grid still carries the shape of a Roman camp.

Vindobona grew from a small outpost into a legionary camp; bricks and a commander's gravestone name the Tenth Legion. Its wall was later reused as the first medieval city wall, and the outline can still be read on the east, south and west sides in the street pattern around Hoher Markt, Michaelerplatz and the Ruprechtskirche. The Danube side has been lost to later terracing. A Roman mass grave found in 2025 is dated mid-first to early second century AD. The city wiki gives no single founding year, so say 'late first century' when you tell it.

The k.u.k. kitchen: Bohemia, Hungary, Galicia, Italy on one plateEmpireA Beisl menu is a map of the old empire, and every dish has a foreign parent.

The Habsburg monarchy pulled cooks and ingredients to its capital. Viennese menus carry Bohemian dumplings (Knödel) and pastry, Hungarian goulash and paprika, Galician and Jewish dishes, and Italian influence through Milan and Venice, which were Habsburg territory in the nineteenth century. Palatschinken (from Hungarian palacsinta) and Gulasch kept their names; the Wiener Schnitzel kept an Austrian one.

The Ringstrasse: one signature in December 1857RingA boulevard 5.2 km long and 57 m wide, laid out on the ground of the old fortifications.

The Handschreiben ordering the fortifications replaced was signed on 20 December 1857 and printed in the Wiener Zeitung on 25 December. Demolition began on 29 March 1858, a first stretch opened on 1 May 1858, and Franz Joseph opened the main section on 1 May 1865. The route is 5,200 m long and 57 m wide. Traffic has run one way, clockwise, only since 12 July 1972.

The last century

Red Vienna and the Karl-Marx-HofSocialAlmost 1,200 m of façade and 1,382 flats, built to put balconies into working-class life.

Between 1919 and 1934 the Social Democratic city built municipal housing on a large scale. The Karl-Marx-Hof, by Karl Ehn, was approved by the city council on 10 June 1927 and opened on 12 October 1930. Its front is almost 1,200 m long; it had 1,382 flats originally and 1,272 today. In 1933, 899 of its 1,325 flats had a balcony or loggia, for about 5,500 residents. In the February fighting of 1934 the army used artillery against it.

What Vienna argues about at the table
The cutlet and Milan, the crescent and the siege, and how to order at a sausage stand
3 to know

Almost every famous Viennese dish arrives with a legend attached, and the Vienna historians have been taking them apart. Each card says what is told, then what the record shows.

The legends and the rules

Two stories that do not survive the paper trail, and the one rule of the stand.

Wiener Schnitzel: the Milan story against the paper trailDisputeRadetzky, Milan and 1848-49 against a cookbook of 1831.

The legend: Field Marshal Radetzky met the cotoletta alla milanese in Milan in 1848 and had the recipe passed to the Viennese court cook in 1849. The Vienna history wiki rejects it: a diary of 1844 already records eating 'Schnitzel' in Lambach, and no reliable source for the Radetzky story can be found. The name 'Wiener Schnitzel' first appears in Maria Anna Neudecker's Allerneuestes allgemeines Kochbuch of 1831, and Magdalena Rettigová's 1827 book has a similar 'Gebackene Kälberschnitzeln'. Milan's own supporters say the cotoletta is older. In Austria 'Wiener Schnitzel' means a flat cut of veal from the topside, shoulder or fricandeau.

Ask for veal (Kalb) and check that the menu says so.

The Kipferl and 1683: a bakery legend that does not holdDisputeThe crescent as mockery of the Ottoman flag is credited to one writer, not to 1683 records.

The legend: the crescent roll mocked the Turkish crescent and so belongs to the Second Siege of 1683. The Vienna history wiki calls this historically untenable, credits the story to Moritz Bermann and names bakers Peter and Eva Wendler as its supposed inventors; a Wendler did own a bakery in 1683-91, but the origin tale is not supported. A Kipfel is already in Vienna in Jans Enenkel's thirteenth-century Fürstenbuch, in a passage about Duke Leopold VI's arrival at Christmas 1227, though the shape is not described. Believers cite local tradition; the documents do not back them.

What the listing says

Heritage status: Würstelstand and KaffeehausHeritageBoth are on Austria's national inventory of intangible heritage, not on UNESCO's international list.

The Austrian UNESCO Commission keeps a national inventory of intangible cultural heritage. Wiener Kaffeehauskultur was entered in 2011 and Wiener Würstelstandkultur in 2024. The commission's pages for both entries describe a national listing and mention no inscription on UNESCO's international lists, so the common wording 'UNESCO World Heritage' for either is wrong. The Würstelstand entry traces the culture to the early 1700s, when Bratlbrater sold hot sausages, and says imperial-era stand licences gave war invalids an income, as tobacco shops did. The Kaffeehaus entry ties the tradition to the late 1600s and to Deodato, an Armenian agent at the Viennese court who opened the first coffeehouse; women were admitted in 1856.

The stories

The Kolschitzky legend versus the Armenian licenceDisputeWho really opened Vienna's first coffeehouse?

The popular story says Georg Franz Kolschitzky, a spy for the relief army in 1683, received sacks of coffee left by the Ottomans and opened the first Viennese coffeehouse. A competing account, preferred by historians who cite the research of Karl Teply, gives the first licence to Johannes Diodato, an Armenian trader, a few years later. Kolschitzky is commemorated by a statue and a street; the facts are not settled in the popular record. The river

How the Danube was moved, and what ran on it.

The river that was movedtrue storyThe Danube regulation of 1870 to 1875.

Until 1870 the Danube at Vienna was a braid of arms, gravel bars and islands, and its main flow ran through what is now the Alte Donau. Floods in 1830, 1849-50 and an ice-jam flood in 1862 kept the question open, but the commission split: one camp wanted to improve the existing main stream, the other, like Josef Schemerl von Leytenbach in 1810, wanted a straight cut. Four foreign experts voted two against two. The cut won partly because it created a long city-side quay, today's Handelskai. At the end of 1869 the job went to the French firm Castor, Couvreux et Hersent, fresh from the Suez Canal. Emperor Franz Joseph turned the first sod on 14 May 1870. Two cuts of 6,640 m and 2,550 m were dug with a 475 m flood plain beside them, 16.4 million cubic metres of earth moved, the first large steam excavators used in Austria. Near Nußdorf the diggers hit the debris of centuries of river works: about 163,000 cubic metres of old structures, thousands of timber piles and 18,400 running metres of sills had to be removed. On 15 April 1875 the dam between old and new bed was opened under the geologist Eduard Suess; shipping was formally opened on 30 May 1875, with the guests aboard the steamer Ariadne. The cost was about 25 million Gulden, a third each from the state, Lower Austria and Vienna, plus 6 million in extras.

★ The old hand's point: Eduard Suess remembered that the river fought the closing of its old arm: it shoved the sunk stone barges aside and ripped out fascines bound with wire, until the engineers pinned them down with upright railway rails. The lesson is a pilot's one: current moves whatever you sink, and the part of a river you can still sail after a straightening is the old arm you cut off. Vienna got a deep, fast, straight channel and a quay; the slack water became a dinghy lake.

Touch it today
Walk onto the Reichsbrücke and look along the straight bed and the Handelskai the cut was designed to create. Then take the U1 to Alte Donau station to see the old main arm behind the Hubertusdamm (raised since the 1930s to 9.75 m at the Reichsbrücke). The steamers that run from the Reichsbrücke landing are in Sailing practical.
Where the sources argue
Dates and money are told differently. The Wien Geschichte Wiki dates the event 1870 to 1876 and says work ran 1870 to 1875 with finishing work to 1881; it gives 'rund 25 Millionen Gulden' plus 6 million extra, where an older library source gave 32.3 million for 1869 to 1882, a wider scope. It gives the cut-and-flood-plain width as about 759 m; a 280 m main-channel figure from a BOKU source was not re-read and is left out. Inside the 1860s commission the dispute was real: Pasetti's improve-the-stream plan lost to the cut.

Franz I, 1830true storyThe first steam on the Danube.

The Erste Donau-Dampfschiffahrts-Gesellschaft was founded in 1823 and re-founded on 13 March 1829; the English shipbuilders John Andrews and Josef Prichard had held an Austrian patent for an improved Danube steamer since 11 April 1828. Their Franz I was launched on 26 July 1830 and made her first trip to Pressburg and Pest on 4 September 1830. Regular service as far as the Moldau began on 1 February 1831. The landing place lay on the right bank of the still unregulated river, opposite today's Gänsehäufel. The privilege granted on 1 September 1830 ran 15 years. The hard direction was upstream: on 12 September 1837 the Maria Anna made the first steamer trip up to Linz and took 55 hours 22 minutes with 250 passengers. By 1852 the company owned 71 steamers and 233 towed barges, and by 1894 it was the largest inland shipping line in the world. The year of the first launch, 1830, was also the year of the catastrophic ice flood that started the argument over a new river bed; forty years later steam excavators dug that bed.

★ The old hand's point: Steam turned the Danube from a downhill road into a two-way one. Before 1830 a cargo boat rode the current and horses or men hauled what had to go back up; the Linz run of 55 hours against the stream is the figure that shows what the engine bought. For anyone planning a passage, the river is two different distances: the way down and the way up.

Touch it today
The company still runs. The DDSG seat is the Schifffahrtszentrum at Handelskai 265. What is sailing this autumn is in Sailing practical.
Where the sources argue
Dates for the first voyage differ by source: the Wien Geschichte Wiki gives launch 26 July and first trip 4 September 1830; a Wienbibliothek pamphlet dates the trial run to 17 September 1830; other secondary sources give launch 11 July. Journey times for the first voyages also differ between accounts (about 14.5 to 15.5 hours down to Pest, 48 hours up). The Wiki is used as the anchor.

Strudentrue storyThe gorge with a draught gauge made of stone.

Until September 1957, when the 30 km reservoir of the Ybbs-Persenbeug power station was filled, the Danube between Grein and Ybbs ran over the Greiner Strudel and the Wirbel, reefs and whirlpools in the Strudengau gorge. The first written mention is a drowning: Bishop Dracolfus of Freising in 926. Boats coming downstream above Grein had to stop and ask what the March showed. The Marchkugel, a stone in the channel, served as the draught gauge; a boat sitting too deep was 'geschiftet', unloaded to the allowed level. From 1777 Joseph Walcher led the great blasting works under Maria Theresia. Alois Topitz, born in St. Nikola and a Zille man himself, records that when a Vienna commission arrived in November 1777 a downbound boat of empty barrels had sat three days on the Wolfskugel with a leak, and a wine boat was stuck on the Meißenkugel; those two and the Marchkugel were the first stones blasted away entirely. Walcher had exact copperplate views made because his success would be only 'the absence of danger'. Afterwards the channel was about 20 m wide, but the sharp right-hand bend stayed. At the Struden toll house every upbound boat had to be reported and downbound traffic was held; from 30 November 1853 a signal service replaced the toll office, and from 1856 a telegraph line ran from Isperdorf to Struden-Baumgarten to announce upbound steamers.

★ The old hand's point: Topitz notes that blasting the stones lowered the water above them: the stone balls were a natural weir, and each one removed dropped the level and exposed rocks nobody had charted. Even the old Marchstein lost its meaning after that. A depth mark is only true for the river its obstacle creates; fix one hazard and the reference changes. The other lesson is the gauge itself: you lighten ship before the gorge, not in it, and downbound and upbound traffic share one channel by a reporting system, not by courtesy.

Touch it today
The reef itself lies under the reservoir; nothing of the rapids is visible. The museum in Spitz (see Day trips) has the story; DDSG's Wachau boats run between Krems and Melk, downstream of the gorge, so they do not pass it.
Where the sources argue
Topitz's idea that the Tyrolean Lergetporer's blast of 1768 (paid 900 Gulden) failed because it lowered the water is his own inference, not a document. The Worth island cross is told two ways: a legend of a Tyrolean count who built it in 1452, and another version of a knight who was wrecked in 1540; Topitz gives both and decides neither. Wikipedia adds an emergency landing of Franz Joseph and Elisabeth at Struden on 20 September 1854 that no primary source was found for, so it is left out.
The sturgeon market of Viennatrue storyBeluga sold by the dozen on Fridays, and a swim bladder worth more than the fish.

Vienna's fish market once sold beluga sturgeon, the Hausen, by the dozen. Already in the Middle Ages the upper Danube could not meet the demand and the fish came from far away, mostly from Hungary. Conrad Gesner wrote in 1558 that on Fridays 50 to 70 Hausen, sometimes 100, were sold in Vienna; the catch season ran from autumn to January. A trade of its own, the Hausenschroter, cut the fish up, and the fish had to be weighed on a scale set aside for it. A toll order of 1672 for Vienna, Linz, Krems and other towns lists fees for caviar, salted Hausen and Hausenblase, the swim bladder (isinglass), which was used to make glue, to clear cloudy wine and beer, as a sticking plaster, and instead of gelatine for aspics and jellies. Caviar from the Danube lands was judged poorer than Russian until the mid-19th century. In 1796 about 43,000 kg of sturgeon were still traded in Vienna; 1810 to 1823 the yearly figure ranged between roughly 3,000 and 17,000 kg; around 1830 some days brought only 10 to 15 sturgeons, some of 100 to 200 kg. Statistics from 1874 to 1913 show Hausen still reaching the market in some years until about 1890, and after 1900 only small amounts. The last upstream capture documented near Bratislava is from 1910, at Vojka nad Dunajom.

★ The old hand's point: The river is a food road. The Friday sales and the autumn-to-January season in the sources line up with the fish-day calendar (a reading of the pattern, not a statement of the study), so the fish arrived on the same water, on the same schedule, that the hulls did. And the galley lesson is in the by-product: the swim bladder that cleared a cask of wine and set a jelly was a sturgeon's, which is why a sturgeon was worth more cut up than whole.

Touch it today
No Danube sturgeon is on a Viennese menu: the species is almost extinct in the upper river, per the study. What you can eat is the river fish the city turned to, at a table that is verified trading now: Gasthaus Birner on the Obere Alte Donau serves carp, Zander and the fish-offal soup; Motto am Fluss on the Donaukanal has a Zander too. Wholesale fish is at Eishken in the Großmarkt Wien (Mon-Fri 06:00-13:00, Sat 07:00-12:00 per the market's page).
Where the sources argue
The authors say plainly that the date of the last Hausen taken in the upper Danube cannot be fixed. Gesner's 1558 figure of 50 to 70, sometimes 100, is a reported Friday count, not a measured one. The causes of the decline are given as overfishing first and then power plants and dams that blocked spawning runs; other writers add the Vienna regulation, which the study does not mention and this guide does not claim.

The fleet

Vienna's naval history, from the Adriatic to the ice.

Lissa, 20 July 1866true storyThe ram and the monument at the Praterstern.

On 20 July 1866 off the island of Lissa (Vis) in the Adriatic, Vice-Admiral Wilhelm von Tegetthoff led the Austrian fleet against the Italian admiral Carlo Pellion di Persano. The Austrian ships were painted black and the Italians light grey, and the Austrians painted their funnels in different colours so they could tell their own armoured ships apart. The order recorded in popular accounts is 'Ram everything that is grey'. When a shot tore away the rudder of the Italian armoured frigate Re d'Italia, Tegetthoff ordered his flagship Erzherzog Ferdinand Max to ram her at full speed. She heeled to starboard and sank in about three minutes. Austria lost 38 killed and 138 wounded; Italy 612 killed, 36 wounded and 19 captured. Vienna made Tegetthoff an honorary citizen on 24 July 1866. He died in Vienna on 7 April 1871. The monument was planned the year he died, the city council voted 1,000 Gulden on 26 May 1871, and the site was argued over for years; the Votivkirche was one candidate. Carl Kundmann modelled it and Carl von Hasenauer designed the architecture. It was unveiled on 24 September 1886 on the Praterstern: an 11 m marble column studded with bronze ships' beaks carries a 3.5 m bronze admiral.

★ The old hand's point: Two of the Italian armoured frigates, the Re d'Italia among them, were built in New York and had unprotected rudders; one hit on the sternpost left her unable to turn from the ram. Steering gear is the soft part of any hull. The other lesson is the paint: black hulls against grey, and coloured funnels, let the Austrian captains pick out friend from foe in the smoke, which a heroic order alone would not have done.

Touch it today
The monument stands free to see on the Praterstern (Wien 2), and its column of bronze ships' beaks, the columna rostrata of Roman sea-victory monuments, is the thing to look at.
Where the sources argue
The unveiling date is 24 September 1886 in the Wien Geschichte Wiki (an earlier pass gave 21 or 24); an earlier figure of 22 m total height is dropped for the Wiki's 11 m column plus 3.5 m statue. The wording of the signal is not settled: 'Ram everything that is grey' appears on English Wikipedia, which cites no primary source for it, and the original signal text was not found. Whether the ram or the close melee decided the day is argued by naval historians; Wikipedia's account credits the ramming of the Re d'Italia as the climax. Casualty figures are from Wikipedia's infobox.

The Tegetthoff in the icetrue storyPayer, Weyprecht and Franz Josef Land, 1872 to 1874.

The Austro-Hungarian North Pole expedition of 1872 to 1874 sailed on the Admiral Tegetthoff, a 220-ton sailing ship with an auxiliary engine, financed by Count Hans Wilczek and led jointly by Julius von Payer on land and the naval officer Karl Weyprecht, who had fought at Lissa in 1866, at sea. The ship left Tromsø on 13 July 1872 with 24 men. The ice closed round her north of Novaya Zemlya and carried her north-west for more than a year. On 30 August 1873 the drift brought into view a group of islands nobody had charted, named Franz Josef Land. The mechanic Otto Krisch died of tuberculosis, the only death. On 20 May 1874 the party left the ship, hauling boats on sledges over the ice. They reached open water on 14 August, met Russian fishermen near Novaya Zemlya on 24 August and reached the mainland of Norway at Vardø on 3 September 1874. Mayor Cajetan Felder greeted Payer and Weyprecht in Vienna on 25 September 1874.

★ The old hand's point: This was not a passage but a captivity: the ship's engine was useless in pack ice and what mattered was the decision to leave her and the boats on runners. The crew dragged their boats for nearly three months, 20 May to 14 August, before they could row. From the day they left the ship, 20 May 1874, until open water on 14 August, boats were sledge loads. For a working chef the other fact is the larder: a drift of 21 months turns provisioning into the entire plan.

Touch it today
The Wien Geschichte Wiki lists a memorial plaque to Weyprecht on Simmeringer Hauptstraße 54 in the 11th district beside the Weyprechtgasse (not checked on site, so confirm before travelling). The Vega-Payer-Weyprecht-Kaserne carries the names but is not open to visitors. For the ship's namesake, the Tegetthoff monument on the Praterstern is a short walk from the Prater.
Where the sources argue
The accounts do not agree on when the ship was beset. The Wien Geschichte Wiki says she was gripped by ice a week after the start, north of Novaya Zemlya; Wikipedia says late August 1872. Leadership was split between Weyprecht (ship) and Payer (land), and each later wrote the story with his own emphasis. Those accounts were not checked. The ship is named 'Tegetthoff' in Wikipedia and 'Admiral Tegetthoff' in the Wien Geschichte Wiki.
Novara, 1857 to 1859true storyThe frigate that carried coca to Göttingen.

The frigate Novara left Trieste on 30 April 1857, under Commodore Bernhard von Wüllerstorf-Urbair, with scientists prepared by the Vienna Academy of Sciences: the geologist Ferdinand von Hochstetter, the zoologist Georg Frauenfeld, the preparator Johann Zelebor, the botanist Eduard Schwarz and the diplomat Karl Scherzer. Becalmed at the start, she was towed by a steamer as far as the Strait of Messina; after that she sailed by Gibraltar, Madeira, Rio, the Cape and the Indian Ocean, and came home round Cape Horn. She was back in Trieste on 26 August 1859 after 551 days under sail and 51,686 nautical miles, an average of about 94 nautical miles a day. The collections, among them 26,000 zoological specimens, enriched Austrian museums, the Naturhistorisches Museum above all, and the results filled a 21-volume work published by the Academy between 1861 and 1876. Scherzer also took coca leaves home. Friedrich Wöhler in Göttingen gave them to his student Albert Niemann, who isolated cocaine from them in 1859 and published it in his 1860 thesis 'Über eine neue organische Base in den Cocablättern'. Niemann died on 19 January 1861, aged 26.

★ The old hand's point: An average of about 94 nautical miles a day over 551 days is a good sailing-ship passage, and it began with a calm and a tow: you plan the way out of a narrow sea before you plan the ocean. The Novara also shows the value of cargo that stays dry and labelled for two years; the leaves that became cocaine were a sailor's souvenir that only a laboratory could read.

Touch it today
The Naturhistorisches Museum Wien (Maria-Theresien-Platz, Wed 9:00-20:00, Thu to Mon 9:00-18:00, closed Tuesday; adult €18) is the institution the Novara collections fed. No dedicated Novara display was found, so ask at the desk before you go for it.
Where the sources argue
The cocaine year was the question: Niemann isolated it in 1859 and published it in 1860, so both years are right for different things; Paolo Mantegazza worked on coca at about the same time. The Wien Geschichte Wiki calls the Novara 'the last sailing ship to circle the globe', a claim not repeated elsewhere, and German Wikipedia calls the voyage the Austrian navy's first circumnavigation. The return is 26 August 1859 in the German Wikipedia, not 30 August.

The water today

Nußdorftrue storyA ship hung across the canal.

In December 1873 the engineer Wilhelm Engerth's Sperrschiff, a barrier ship, was put into service at the head of the Danube Canal at Nußdorf. It lay along the left bank and in a flood or ice drive was swung across the canal to shut it, to protect the districts along the canal. Between 1894 and 1898 the permanent weir and lock were built for nine million Kronen, credited to Otto Wagner (see the dispute note), and the barrier ship was hung in place on 11 November 1902. A chamber lock lowers boats from the main river to the level of the canal. The weir looks like a bridge, with the barrier gear beneath it, and takes the name Schemerlbrücke from the 1810 river planner Joseph Maria Schemerl von Leythenbach; the two bronze lions are by Rudolf Weyr. Rebuilding began in September 1964 and replaced the hand-operated mitre gates with lift-and-swing gates; the whole plant reopened on 16 December 1975. The lock works in summer; in winter the plant supplies cooling water to the Simmering power plant and helps flood protection. Since late June 2017 the Vienna waters authority MA 45 has its head office in Wagner's lock building.

★ The old hand's point: A lock is a level machine, and the Nußdorf weir is the reason the Danube Canal is a quiet berth in a river that floods and carries ice. The entrance is a gate that closes against the river; the 1873 solution was a whole ship laid across it, the 1890s solution was a bridge with a barrier beneath. If you ever lock through from the main river, you are stepping down to a level the engineers keep deliberately below the flood.

Touch it today
The weir, lions and Schemerlbrücke are free to see on the Nußdorfer Lände (Wien 19 and 20). The building is a working office, not a museum. DDSG lists its evening cruise as going to the Nußdorf lock and back; whether it still reaches the lock during the low-water notice is not confirmed.
Where the sources argue
Who designed it is contested. The Wien Geschichte Wiki, drawing on Czeike, says the weir and lock were built 'durch Otto Wagner'. German Wikipedia says Wagner drew the architecture of the weir, the Schemerlbrücke and the outbuildings and, possibly, the lock, while the technical planning is by Siegmund Taussig, whom it cites to two references that were not opened. Both agree on the 1894 to 1898 build and on Wagner's look.

The sailing laketrue storyThe Alte Donau and the Wiener Yacht Club.

The Alte Donau is the old main arm of the Danube, cut off since 1875 by the Hubertusdamm and now fed by groundwater. Rowing clubs have had boathouses and there have been boat hirers on it since 1880. The Wiener Yacht Club on the Obere Alte Donau (An der Oberen Alten Donau 92) was founded in 1924 as the Floridsdorfer Segelverein, the second sailing club in the Vienna area; a bomb destroyed the clubhouse in the war, the members rebuilt it, and in 1951 the club restarted and took its present name. Its strongest class today is the Finn dinghy, and every second year it runs a nostalgia regatta for boats nearly a hundred years old. The lake itself tipped in 1992 and 1993, when filamentous cyanobacteria bloomed; the causes included nutrients from a former landfill, leaking cesspits and heavy bathing. A recovery programme began in 1993. Every spring the level is lowered on purpose by 20 to 25 cm, and since autumn 2016 about 45 litres per second from the Neue Donau pass through a soil filter. From 1 January 2026 a city ordinance limits motor boats to 10 km/h and combustion engines to 4.4 kW, requires registration with a plate (small pedal boats and rowing boats under 4.5 m excepted), and caps every boat at 7 m by 2.55 m. Sailing and rowing boats are exempt from the speed rule.

★ The old hand's point: The 7 m by 2.55 m cap makes the lake's character plain: it is a dinghy and daysailer water, and most cruising yachts of 10 m and more are simply outside the rules. What it teaches is wind: a shifting breeze between built banks, no tide and no sea. It also teaches that a level can be managed: the city draws the lake down every spring so groundwater can flush it.

Touch it today

Neusiedler Seetrue storyA sea a metre deep, and now almost at its record low.

The Neusiedler See is a steppe lake of about 320 square kilometres, roughly 230 in Austria and 90 in Hungary, half of it reed. Its mean depth is about 1.0 m and its maximum about 1.8 m. It is the largest lake in central Europe without a natural outflow and dried out almost completely between 1864 and 1870. The long-term mean level is about 115.45 m above the Adriatic with a seasonal swing of 60 to 80 cm. On 1 October 2026 Austrian press agency APA reported the level at 114.93 m, just four cm above the record low of 2022, the lowest measured since 1965; the water manager at the Burgenland government hoped for winter rain, and ferry traffic had been restricted at Breitenbrunn because wind pushed the shallow water. A storm-warning system of flashing lights stands at Illmitz, Podersdorf, Weiden, Neusiedl am See, Breitenbrunn, Purbach, Oggau, Rust and Mörbisch: 40 flashes a minute for a strong-wind warning from Beaufort 6 and 90 flashes a minute for storm from Beaufort 8, according to a Burgenland police brochure.

So the 2020 and 2024 dates are two different steps, not a conflict. Only the Wikipedia summary was read, not the RIS decree or the ORF report. For a tasting by the lake, Weingut Kracher in Illmitz is the verified stop (see venue).

The Dish — where the list comes from

The stories behind the canon, and where they are argued rather than settled.

#1
DISH

Sachertorte versus Demel: settled in 1963, not in court

The popular story says Franz Sacher invented the torte in 1832 as a 16-year-old apprentice for Prince Metternich; the Vienna history wiki thinks this unlikely and points to his time in Hungary in the 1840s. After Anna Sacher died the hotel ran into trouble and was sold in 1934, and her son Eduard sold the recipe to Demel. The fight over the name 'Original Sachertorte' ran for decades and was settled out of court in 1963: the hotel may use 'Original Sachertorte', Demel sells 'Eduard Sacher Torte' or 'Demel's Sachertorte'. The Sacher cake has apricot jam under the glaze and a second layer in the middle, Demel's only under the glaze; the Sacher seal is round, Demel's triangular.
WhereCafé Sacher, Philharmonikerstraße 4, and Demel, Kohlmarkt 14 · Maps →
#2
DISH

Imperial torte

The Hotel Imperial's house cake is, as the legend goes, invented in honour of Emperor Franz Joseph in 1873 for the hotel's opening. Strictly it is a candy: lightly whipped chocolate cream layered with almond flakes, wrapped in fine marzipan and glazed with chocolate. It is eaten in Café Imperial on the Ringstrasse.
WhereCafé Imperial, in the Hotel Imperial · Maps →
#3
DISH

Anton Dreher and Vienna lager, 1841

Anton Dreher brewed a reddish-copper lager from a lightly caramelised amber 'Vienna malt', which he developed after a British study trip with Gabriel Sedlmayr of Spaten; the beer was released in 1841 as 'Lager Vienna Type' (Mautner Markhof page). Sources disagree on details: the Klein-Schwechat brewery is said to date from 1632, the Dreher family bought it in 1796, and the switch to bottom fermentation is dated 1839 in one source and 1841 in others. No brewing logs survive, so the original recipe is reconstructed. In 2016, on the 175th anniversary, the Schwechat brewery relaunched Wiener Lager.
WhereSalm Bräu, Rennweg 8, or Siebensternbräu, Siebensterngasse 19 · Maps →
