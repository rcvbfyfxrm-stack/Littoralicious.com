The name From the Parisii, the Celtic tribe on the river crossing — not from the Roman town.
Julius Caesar recorded the Parisii and their island oppidum on the Seine; Rome built its own town, Lutetia, around it and up the Left Bank towards the Montagne Sainte-Geneviève. When the empire fell and Clovis I made the place a Merovingian capital in the sixth century, the name of the tribe outlived the Roman name of the town. Paris is still, at its centre, an island in a river: the Île de la Cité, where every distance in France is measured from a bronze star in the paving in front of Notre-Dame.

The soul of the place — the essay
An island in a river, Haussmann's boulevards over the medieval tangle, the zinc counter where a worker and a philosopher paid the same price, and the two maps of where Paris eats
THE ESSAY

Paris began as an island. The Parisii, a Celtic tribe, held a river crossing on the Seine. Everything else — the boulevards, the museums, the myth — grew outward from the Île de la Cité.

Then the city was rebuilt on top of itself. Between 1853 and 1870 Baron Haussmann drove some 140 kilometres of boulevard through the medieval tangle for Napoleon III — light, air, sewers, stone. But the thing Paris actually gave the table is smaller than a boulevard: the cafe-comptoir and the bistro, the public living room where a worker and a philosopher paid the same price to stand at the same zinc.

The tension is the whole story. Paris is a museum-city — the Louvre, the Orsay, a cluster of three-star kitchens — that stubbornly refuses to become a museum. The boulangerie still opens before dawn, the espresso is still drunk standing, and the natural-wine bar in the 11th is louder than the guidebook. The trick, as Bourdain knew, is to walk past the monument and eat where the neighbourhood eats.

The living city. The unit of Parisian life is the terrasse: an espresso or a glass of wine, a chair turned toward the street, and the unhurried watching the nineteenth century named flânerie. The daily anchor is bread — "boulangerie" is a protected term, meaning the loaf must be kneaded and baked on the premises. And running under all of it, the grève — the strike, the barricade, the argument in the street — is as Parisian as the croissant.

The quintessence. The truest ritual is the smallest: the morning walk to the boulangerie for a warm baguette de tradition, and the quignon — the heel — broken off and eaten on the way home. At the café, the initiated take their coffee standing at the counter, where it costs less than at a table. And at dusk comes the apero, the drink-before-the-meal that is really an excuse to sit down and let the day end slowly.

Where it comes from

The medieval city thickened around the island and its markets, and in 1789 the Revolution began here. The Paris we walk now, though, is largely Napoleon III's — Haussmann's order was to "aérer, unifier, embellir," to give the city air (Wikipedia; Britannica) — and the Belle Époque, roughly 1870 to 1914, gilded the result.

Food history rode alongside: in 1782 the Palais-Royal gave the world the restaurant (the story is told just below), and the word bistro, the market of Les Halles and the zinc counter each have their own card among the curiosities. And in May 1968, students tore up the Latin Quarter's cobbles for the Night of the Barricades, touching off a general strike of some ten million workers (Britannica).

Where the money sits

At the top, MICHELIN still clusters more thickly here than in any other French city. But the honest map runs the other way: the tourist-trap belt hugs the big monuments, and Parisians increasingly eat east and north, in the neo-bistros and wine caves of the 10th, 11th and 20th. This is the Bourdain principle in practice — walk away from the postcard.

Underneath sits the plumbing — Rungis, south of the city, provisions the whole region before dawn — and the shift in the glass: the caves à manger, wine shops you eat in, that began around 2000 on the Canal Saint-Martin are now the default of a whole generation of Paris tables.

And it is a hinge moment. The Centre Pompidou has gone dark for a rebuild due to last until about 2030, while the fringe of 2010 — third-wave coffee, the natural-wine cave, the neo-bistro without a white tablecloth — has become the city's mainstream. Paris is quietly recomposing around the bistro, again.

1782 — the year Paris learned to dine out
Not a Revolution story but a garden full of gamblers: the room with private tables, a printed card and a fixed price, invented in the Palais-Royal
THE STORY

The restaurant — a room of private tables, set prices, a printed card and food on demand — was not invented anywhere else. It was invented here, in a garden full of gamblers.

The tidy version says the French Revolution created the restaurant, freeing the aristocracy's private chefs to cook for the public. The historian Rebecca Spang spent a whole book dismantling that: the word came earlier, from the restorative bouillons called 'restaurants' sold to the delicate in the 1760s, and the soup-seller Boulanger who supposedly opened the first one in 1765 may never have existed at all. But the setting where the restaurant became the restaurant is real, and it was the Palais-Royal. The Duc d'Orléans, chronically short of money, had ringed his garden with arcades and let the shops to cafes, gambling dens and traiteurs, making a walled pleasure-quarter that, as royal private property, the police could barely enter.

In 1782, into those arcades, Antoine Beauvilliers, former chef to the future Louis XVIII, opened La Grande Taverne de Londres: mahogany tables, crystal, fine linen, a deep cellar, waiters in livery, and, radically, a menu that let a stranger walk in, sit at a table of his own, and order a named dish at a fixed price. When the Revolution came and the guilds were abolished in 1791, the great houses' cooks poured into the same streets and did the same thing. Within a decade Paris had hundreds of restaurants; the aristocratic kitchen had escaped into public life, and it never went back.

Almost none of those first rooms survive. One does, in the same arcades: Le Grand Véfour opened as the Café de Chartres in 1784 and took its present name when Jean Véfour bought it in 1820; Colette, who lived upstairs on the square, and Cocteau had their own tables. It reopened on 12 May 2026 under a new owner, and its card in the tables has the rest. Then the bistro took the idea downmarket and made it Parisian: a short card, a zinc, a carafe. The tables of this guide are its heirs, and La liste at the foot takes the plates one by one.

The Right Bank villages

The old Jewish quarter and its galleries, the canal of the caves à manger, and the hill of the neo-bistros.

Le Maraismansions · PletzlA drained marsh that became the court's address, was left to rot, and was saved by being too poor to rebuild

Marais means marsh, and it was one: wet ground on the Right Bank that the Knights Templar and the religious orders began draining after Louis VII granted the Templars land here in 1139. Nobles moved in once the royal square went up in 1612, built stone mansions round private courtyards, then followed the court to Versailles and left it to workshops and decay. Too poor to rebuild, it kept its houses. In 1964 André Malraux made the Marais France's first protected 'secteur sauvegardé'. Push a carriage gate and you find a 17th-century courtyard; Rue des Rosiers is still the Jewish Pletzl.

Why go. Walk with your head up and push every open carriage door: the mansions show their faces to the courtyard, not the street.

Canal Saint-Martincanal · aperitifNine locks and iron footbridges on a canal paid for by a tax on wine

In 1802 Paris drank from the same Seine it emptied its sewage into, and cholera and dysentery followed. Napoleon ordered a canal to bring clean water from the Ourcq and float in grain and stone, paid for with a tax on wine. The canal opened in 1825, four years after his death. Under Napoleon III about half of it was roofed over; Boulevard Richard-Lenoir runs on top. In the open stay 4.5 km of locks and footbridges, where Amélie skips stones in the 2001 film, and the Hôtel du Nord of the 1938 film and its cry of 'Atmosphère !'.

Why go. Come on a fine Sunday evening and sit on the stone edge with the neighbourhood while a boat works up through a lock.

Bellevillehill · many citiesThe steep village Paris annexed in 1860, now Chinatown, North Africa and street art on one slope

Belleville was vineyards and open-air guinguettes, drinking gardens beyond the city's tax wall, until Paris annexed it in 1860. Haussmann's demolitions pushed the workers of the centre up here, and wave after wave of newcomers followed: Jewish, North African, then Chinese, whose restaurants and grocers now line the boulevard at the foot of the hill. Édith Piaf grew up singing in these streets. Climb on through the painted walls of Rue Dénoyez to the terraced Parc de Belleville, opened in 1988 on a hill of about 108 m, where the whole of Paris lies flat below you to the west.

The hill and the Left Bank

The village on the Butte, and the café quarter of the Left Bank.

Montmartrehill · gypsumParis's highest hill at about 130 m: a martyr's name, gypsum mines and a basilica built as penance

The hill's name is a legend: Saint Denis, first bishop of Paris, is said to have been beheaded up here around 250 AD, and Mont des Martyrs stuck. The ground is real enough: Montmartre was quarried for gypsum, the stone behind 'plaster of Paris', until the mines wound down around the time Paris annexed the village in 1860. Cheap rents then brought Renoir, Toulouse-Lautrec, Van Gogh and Picasso. Sacré-Cœur was begun in 1875 as national penance after the defeat by Prussia and the bloodshed of the Commune. Climb the stairs from the Abbesses side, skip the easels of Place du Tertre, and keep to the back lanes behind the basilica.

Why go. Come up by the stairs and the back streets, not the funicular crowd, and look north from behind the basilica as well as south.

Saint-Germain-des-Présabbey · publishersA quarter grown round a 6th-century royal abbey, long before its cafés became famous

The quarter is named for an abbey. Childebert I, son of Clovis, founded it in the mid-6th century to hold the relics he brought back from the siege of Zaragoza, among them a fragment of the True Cross and Saint Vincent's tunic. The church was consecrated in 558, the year the king died, and it became the burial place of the Merovingian kings. The present church, its nave Romanesque, stands on that foundation. What most visitors never hear is that for centuries the abbey's annual fair, the Foire Saint-Germain, made this one of Europe's great marketplaces. Publishers still cluster around it.

Why go. Go inside the abbey church before you take a café terrace: it is the reason the quarter exists at all.

Three royal squares

A square of brick and stone from 1612, a triangle on the island, and a circle round a king.

Place des Vosgesroyal squareParis's first planned square, built where a king died of a lance through his visor

Here stood the Hôtel des Tournelles, the royal residence where Henri II died in 1559 after a lance splinter went through his visor in a tournament; Catherine de' Medici had it pulled down. There Henri IV built a square from 1605 to 1612 and ordered every front to follow one design: 36 pavilions of red brick and pale stone over vaulted arcades round a garden of limes and four fountains. Opened in 1612 as the Place Royale, it took the name of the Vosges in 1800, the first department to pay its taxes to the Republic. Victor Hugo lived at no. 6 from 1832 to 1848.

Why go. Walk the whole arcade round once, then sit in the garden: the matching fronts only read as one design from the middle.

Place Dauphineroyal squareA quiet triangle of houses on the prow of the Île de la Cité, shaped by a sandbank

Henri IV's second royal square is a triangle for a plain reason: it fills the silt spit at the downstream tip of the Île de la Cité, next to his new Pont Neuf. On 10 March 1607 he handed the land, about three acres, to Achille de Harlay on one condition, that every façade repeat the same model, and named it for the Dauphin, his six-year-old son and the future Louis XIII. The last house was finished in 1616. Many fronts have been altered since, but the shape and the quiet survive: step in from the bridge and the traffic noise drops away.

Under the street, and on it

Six million under the pavement, the iron of 1900, and the water that is still free.

The empire of the deadcuriosityOver the door a line still warns: 'Arrête ! C'est ici l'empire de la Mort'

Paris's cemeteries were overflowing by the 1780s, and from 1786 the bones were carted, by night, into the old limestone quarries under the Left Bank. Workers stacked femurs and skulls into walls, and over the threshold of the ossuary they carved a warning: 'Arrête ! C'est ici l'empire de la Mort', Stop, here is the empire of the dead. Today you go down about 20 m and some 130 steps, walk roughly 1.5 km of cold, damp galleries, and come up again by another stair. The site reopened in April 2026 after renovation work, with a headset sound guide replacing the old audioguide.

Why go. Book a timed slot before you go; there is no walk-in, and reading the carved warning in the dark is the moment.

The counter and the café

A bar named for a metal, a philosopher's cups, and a word everybody gets wrong.

Le zinc is often pewtercuriosityParisians meet 'au zinc', at the bar, though many of the old counters are tin alloy

To meet au zinc is to meet at the counter: the metal named the bar. The catch: Many of the old counters you lean on are not zinc but étain, pewter, a tin alloy that 19th-century makers used for bar tops; specialists say they are called zinc wrongly. The second thing to know is on the window. Since a 1987 decree, a café must post outside the prices of its most common drinks both au comptoir and en salle, and the terrace price must be shown as a price, not as a supplement. Standing at the bar is meant to cost less.

Why go. Read the price board at the door, then drink your coffee standing at the counter: same cup, lower price.

Le Procope opened in 1686 under Procopio Cutò, an Italian who fitted his rooms with chandeliers, wall mirrors and marble-topped tables, the look Paris cafés would copy. Bistro is not RussiancuriosityThe Cossacks shouting 'bystro!' in 1814 is a tale: the word turns up in print some seventy years later

Every waiter in Montmartre will tell you: in 1814 Russian soldiers occupying Paris banged the tables and shouted bystro, quickly, and the quick little wine shop got its name. The word bistro, or bistrot, does not appear in written French until the 1880s, about seventy years after the Cossacks left. The city's belly and its vineyard

The market that moved out in 1969, the vineyard on the hill, and the arcades that survived.

The move of the centurycuriosityLes Halles did not leave in one night: the 1969 move took several days, and the meat stayed until 1973

Zola called Les Halles 'the belly of Paris', and for eight centuries the city's food came through here. The story told everywhere is that it left in a single night. It did not. The 'move of the century' ran over several days at the turn of February and March 1969, some 20,000 people and 1,500 lorries shifting about a thousand wholesalers to Rungis, which opened to buyers on 3 March. The meat traders stayed on until 1973. Baltard's iron pavilions came down in the early 1970s. Today the Forum des Halles and its canopy fill the site, and Saint-Eustache, the market's parish church, still stands over it.

Why go. Stand by Saint-Eustache and picture the iron halls: the church is the one thing the market porters would still recognise.

15 to know

Paris drinks standing before it drinks sitting. Learn the grower Champagne, the bistro whites and the Beaujolais by name; then the counters of the 10th and 11th, a roof at dusk, and one drink under a palace ceiling.

Ask for it by name

The nearest vineyard is Champagne; the bistro whites; the gamay you drink cool.

Champagne, grower bottlesthe fizz with a villageTwo small letters near the cork, RM, tell you one family grew the grapes and made the wine

The big Champagne houses buy grapes from hundreds of growers across the region and blend them into a house style that tastes the same every year. A récoltant-manipulant does the opposite: one family farms its own vines, often in a single village, and makes and bottles the wine itself. The code is printed in small type on the label, usually near the bottom: RM for a grower, NM for a négociant house, CM for a cooperative. Champagne is the nearest serious vineyard to Paris, a short train ride east from the Gare de l'Est, so a grower bottle is about as local as the city's wine gets.

Why go. Ask what grower fizz they have open by the glass; a good wine bar usually has one, and it is rarely dearer than the house brand.

In the glass
Finer, more precise bubbles than the brand-name blends; chalk, bread and green apple, with the village showing through
On the label
RM in the small print near the bottom; brut nature or extra brut means little or no added sugar
Ask for
Une coupe de champagne de vigneron. Names to look for: Chartogne-Taillet, Pierre Péters, Larmandier-Bernier, Egly-Ouriet, Vouette et Sorbée
Chablis & Sancerrethe bistro whitesOrder un verre de blanc and you will usually be offered one of these two: ancient seabed or Loire flint

Most bistro white lists open with these two. Chablis is chardonnay from the far north of Burgundy, grown on Kimmeridgian marl, a seabed some 150 million years old still packed with tiny fossil oysters; in its plain village form it is made without new oak, so it tastes of lemon and wet stone rather than butter. Sancerre is sauvignon blanc from the upper Loire. On its flint soils it gives a struck-match, gunsmoke smell the French call pierre à fusil. Either one should taste sharp and saline. Vanilla or toast on the nose means it is not the classic style. The Chablis vineyards belong to the Burgundy guide.

Why go. With oysters or a plate of charcuterie, ask for either one by name instead of house white; the jump in quality costs a few euros.

Beaujolais crusthe cool redNot the November nouveau: cru gamay from Morgon or Fleurie, low on sulphur and served cool

Beaujolais has two lives. One is the nouveau, rushed out every November. The other is the ten crus, villages like Morgon, Fleurie and Chiroubles, whose granite slopes give gamay real depth. In the 1980s a handful of Morgon growers took advice from the chemist and négociant Jules Chauvet and began making wine with whole bunches, native yeasts and little or no added sulphur. Marcel Lapierre, Jean Foillard, Jean-Paul Thévenet and Guy Breton became known as the Gang of Four, and many trace the natural-wine movement back to them. Their wines are what Paris's natural-wine counters pour most; those rooms have their own cards below.

Why go. Ask for it slightly cool, about twenty minutes in the fridge; the fruit tightens and it drinks like a cold glass of cherries.

Summer: music, the fourteenth and the river

A night of art, a day of free music, the national holiday and the Seine opened for swimming.

Fête de la Musique, 21 Junefree musicOn the longest day the whole city becomes the stage, from midday to the small hours

Every 21 June, the summer solstice, France holds the Fête de la Musique: free concerts by anyone who wants to play, on street corners, in courtyards, café terraces and the city's institutions, in all twenty arrondissements. In 2027 it falls on a Monday, so it is a working day and the crowds come out after work and stay late. The metro runs late that night and many streets become pedestrian. Earlier in June, Nuit Blanche, the free all-night art event, was held on Saturday 6 June in 2026.

Why go. Eat early, then walk; the best sets are the ones you stumble on.

Le Quatorze Juilletnational dayThe military parade down the Champs-Élysées in the morning, fireworks at the Eiffel Tower at night

France's national day, 14 July, keeps its fixed shape in Paris: the military parade on the Champs-Élysées in the morning, and fireworks over the Eiffel Tower and the Trocadéro at night, with the firemen's balls (bals des pompiers) in the fire stations around it. Beaujolais Nouveau, the third Thursday of Novembernew wineFixed by decree since 1985: Thursday 19 November 2026, Thursday 18 November 2027

The release day of the Beaujolais Nouveau is set by law: a decree of 29 October 1985 put it on the third Thursday of November. The wine is made fast from that autumn's harvest and meant to be drunk young, through the winter.

Why go. Pick a wine bar with regulars at the counter; that is where the night happens.

The galette des rois, JanuaryEpiphanyPuff pastry, frangipane and a hidden fève: every boulangerie sells it through January

The Epiphany falls on 6 January, a Wednesday in 2027, and in France the feast is usually kept on the first Sunday of January, which in 2027 is the 3rd. The Paris version of the galette des rois is puff pastry filled with frangipane, with a small fève hidden inside: whoever finds it wears the paper crown that comes with the cake. Boulangeries sell it from the turn of the year through most of January, and the youngest at the table traditionally sits under it and calls out who gets each slice.

Why go. Buy it from a real bakery, not a supermarket; the butter shows.

On the plate

Game, wild mushrooms and oysters in the bistros, and five days of chocolate at Porte de Versailles.

Game season in the bistrosla chasseWild game can only be sold in the hunting season, so from now to February it is on the blackboards

In France wild game may be sold only during the hunting season, which runs from autumn into February; farmed game is around all year. So this is when the bistros write grouse, mallard, hare and venison on the board. Chez L'Ami Jean in the 7th books up for its autumn and winter game, roast grouse and wild boar stew among them. Au Bascou in the 3rd, a South-West bistro, treats lièvre à la royale, hare slow-cooked in its own blood-thickened sauce, as its signature from October to the end of January. The dish is rare and kitchens make it in small batches.

Why go. Ask when you book whether the lièvre à la royale is on: it runs out.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eleven plates and habits and their honest, often contested, origins: the one detail that separates the real version from the tourist copy.

ORIGIN

Steak-frites

Onglet or bavette seared hard, a raft of thin fries, often maitre d'hotel butter or a peppercorn sauce. The unofficial plate of the zinc counter — and it is judged, always, by the fries. Origin. Contested between France and Belgium; a nineteenth-century bistro dish, not haute cuisine (Britannica; Wikipedia).

ORIGIN

Baguette de tradition française

By law only flour, water, salt and leaven — no freezing, no additives — which is exactly why it goes stale by nightfall. The 'tradition' is the good one; the plain 'baguette' can be industrial. Origin. Paris; the name and recipe fixed by the Decret Pain of 13 September 1993 (Legifrance).

ORIGIN

Croissant

Lamination — butter folded into dough — is the French contribution. Order it 'au beurre'; the cheaper 'ordinaire' is made with margarine. Origin. Austrian, not French: descended from the Viennese kipferl and brought to Paris by August Zang's 1838 Viennese bakery on rue de Richelieu. The Marie-Antoinette story is unsupported (The Local; ICE; Chowhound).

ORIGIN

Soupe à l'oignon

Onions cooked slowly to bronze, beef stock, a raft of bread and gratineed Gruyere. Peasant economy turned ceremony — long the 4 a.m. cure of market porters. Origin. French; by lore the restorative of the all-night workers of Les Halles (the market link is tradition, not documented).

ORIGIN

Blanquette de veau

Veal, cream, a whisper of lemon, button mushrooms and pearl onions. The Sunday dish, not the restaurant dish. Origin. French home cooking; 'blanquette' from blanc — the veal is poached, never browned, so the sauce stays pale.

ORIGIN

Confit de canard

A pre-refrigeration preservation technique now a bistro staple. Honest to say: it is a guest in Paris, native to the land of cassoulet. Origin. South-West France (Gascony), not Paris — duck legs cured and cooked slowly in their own fat.

ORIGIN

Jambon-beurre

Baguette, good butter, jambon de Paris. France's best-selling sandwich — reportedly over three million a day — and a fair test of a boulangerie. Origin. Paris; born late nineteenth century among Les Halles workers, on the baguette from the 1920s (Wikipedia; Taste of France).

ORIGIN

Croque-monsieur

Ham and melted cheese bound in bechamel, grilled. Crown it with a fried egg and it becomes a croque-madame. Origin. Paris; commonly attributed to a cafe on the Boulevard des Capucines around 1910, and in Proust by 1918 (Devour Tours; Grokipedia).

ORIGIN

Escargots de Bourgogne

The garlic-parsley butter is the whole point; the snail is the delivery vehicle. A brasserie classic in the capital. Origin. Burgundy, not Paris — its terroir is east, in the vineyards.

ORIGIN

Macaron

Two almond-meringue shells, a ganache or jam between. The Catherine de' Medici origin tale is popular and doubtful. Origin. Italian almond biscuit by descent; the double-shelled 'macaron parisien' is a twentieth-century Paris refinement credited to Laduree's Pierre Desfontaines (Wikipedia).

ORIGIN

Le café (express au comptoir)

A small black coffee taken standing at the zinc, faster and cheaper than the terrasse. For decades the weak link in a great food city — until the third wave arrived. Origin. The cafe as public institution is Parisian; the espresso itself is Italian by machine.
