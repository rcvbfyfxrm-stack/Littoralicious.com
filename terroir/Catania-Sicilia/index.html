The names A Greek colony, a mountain named twice, and an elephant everyone calls by a sorcerer's name.
Catania was Katane, founded from Naxos — the first Greek colony in Sicily, on the beach below Taormina — a few years after Siracusa. Etna is Greek Aitne, often linked to the verb for burning; Sicilians on its slopes call it 'a Muntagna, the Mountain, and in older speech Mongibello — Latin mons and Arabic jabal, two words for mountain stacked on each other. The lava elephant in Piazza Duomo is 'u Liotru, the city's mascot since the Middle Ages.

THE ESSAY

It is a quarter to eight in a bar in Catania and nobody is having a cappuccino. At the counter a man in a suit is eating ice with a spoon — a glass of almond granita, pale and coarse, with a brioche beside it still warm enough to steam when he tears off the little topknot and dips it in. Outside, Via Etnea runs straight uphill towards a mountain that is smoking. This is breakfast, and the ice used to come down from the volcano.

The mountain is in everything. In 1669 a lava flow crossed seventeen kilometres of farmland and climbed Catania's walls; after the earthquake of 1693 the city was rebuilt in its own black basalt, trimmed with white limestone, which is why its baroque looks like a photographic negative of Noto's. The same ash grows the Bronte pistachio, the Etna lemon, the blood orange that colours only where the nights turn cold, and the vines of Milo, whose Carricante is the only Etna white allowed to call itself Superiore.

The second thing is the market. The Pescheria shouts every morning except Sunday under an arch that says W S. Agata: swordfish, red prawns, in October the lampuga. Around it are the horse butchers and the fry shops, and Catania eats from all of them standing up — the pointed arancino, the cipollina, the scacciata, horse grilled on Via Plebiscito, a soda with lemon and salt at a kiosk after midnight.

The third is the saint. For three days every February Catania fills with hundreds of thousands of people for Sant'Agata, many in white, and every pasticceria makes her minne. Bellini was born here in 1801; Verga set his fishermen at Aci Trezza, under the rocks the Cyclops threw. Come in October, when the Ottobrata fills Zafferana every Sunday and the harvest is on the mountain.

The Duke of Bronte never saw Bronte. In 1799 King Ferdinand gave Horatio Nelson the Duchy of Bronte, on Etna's western flank, for helping restore him to the throne of Naples. Nelson signed his letters 'Nelson & Bronte' for the rest of his life and never set foot on the estate; his heirs kept the Maniace lands until 1981. The town he never visited grows the Pistacchio Verde di Bronte DOP on bare lava, and harvests it only in odd-numbered years — 2026 rests, the next crop is 2027.

The markets

La Pescheria, CataniaFish marketSwordfish heads on marble, prices sung across the slabs, and the whole show packed up by early afternoon.

Come down the steps from Piazza Duomo behind the Amenano fountain and the noise arrives before the fish. Vendors sing their prices in dialect, the abbanniata, over wooden counters topped with marble, hosing scales into the lava paving as they go. The stalls spread across three linked spaces: under the Palazzo del Seminario dei Chierici facing the Archi della Marina, then Piazza Pardo, then Piazza Alonzo di Benedetto. In October the slabs carry lampuga, the autumn dorado, beside the year-round anchovies and red prawns. By half past one the hoses are out and it is over.

Buy here what you cannot buy anywhere else in Catania: fish that was in the water this morning.

Fiera di Catania ('a Fera 'o Luni')Open-air market740 licensed stalls on paper, over a thousand on the ground, and one vendor who has shouted here for 70 years.

Via Plebiscito: arrusti e manciaStreet grillAfter 8 p.m. butcher shops put grills on the pavement and the street fills with smoke.

Arrusti e mancia: roast and eat. On Via Plebiscito the horse butchers turn into grill rooms after dark. You choose at the counter, take a plastic chair on the pavement, and minutes later the plate arrives: horse meatballs bound with garlic, parmesan and parsley, thin slices of horse steak, sausage, a wedge of lemon. What the volcano grows

Three protected names from Etna's soil.

Pistacchio Verde di Bronte DOPDOPA nut grafted onto wild terebinth in lava, picked by hand every other year.

Pistacchio Verde di Bronte is the Napoletana cultivar, grafted onto wild terebinth that grows where nothing else will, on the lava fields the locals call sciare. The DOP covers Bronte, Adrano and Biancavilla. Harvest is by hand, every second year, roughly 20 August to 10 October, and the husk must come off within 24 hours. Crack one and the seed is emerald under a ruby skin. 2025 was a harvest year; 2026 is a rest year, so the next fresh crop is 2027.

Without the DOP mark, 'pistacchio di Bronte' on a label proves nothing.

Limone dell'Etna IGPIGP 2020Lemons made to bloom a second time by being denied water.

The trick has a name: forzatura, or secca. In early summer the growers of the Ionian-Etna strip stop watering. The trees, on fast-draining volcanic soil, stress, wait, and when the water returns they bloom again. That second flowering gives the verdelli, green-skinned summer lemons that ripen when other groves have none. The IGP, registered in 2020, covers Femminello and Monachello in 16 comuni, in types called Primo Fiore, Bianchetto or Maiolino, and Verdello. In 1894 Federico De Roberto was already describing men digging through Etna lava to plant citrus.

This is the lemon squeezed into every seltz at a Catania kiosk.

Arancia Rossa di Sicilia IGPIGPBlood oranges from 32 comuni: out of season in October, but the reason the plain is green.

Three cultivars, Tarocco, Moro and Sanguinello, grow across 32 comuni of the Catania, Enna and Siracusa provinces, on the plain south of the volcano. They are cut by hand with curved clippers and no de-greening is allowed. The red comes with cold nights after warm days, which is why the season starts in December. The specification cites the Jesuit Giovanni Battista Ferrari, who wrote in 1646 that a Genoese missionary returning from the Philippines brought pigmented oranges. An amendment to the rules was published in the EU Official Journal on 2 July 2026.

In October, buy the marmalade or candied peel and come back in winter for the fruit.

Granita at seven — the morning ritual
Almond ice and a warm brioche for breakfast, and the snow that used to come down from the volcano
the signature

In Catania breakfast is a glass of granita and a brioche col tuppo, at a counter, all year. Order 'una granita di mandorla con la brioche' and never ask for it to go. If you have one morning, go to Savia on Via Etnea at opening.

Why it is like this

Granita is Arab sherbet plus Etna snowFood originBefore freezers, men called nivaroli carried Etna's snow down the mountain to make it.

Gambero Rosso traces granita to two things meeting: the Arab sherbet, iced drinks scented with flowers and fruit, and the Sicilian habit of packing Etna snow into neviere, stone snow-pits on the mountain, to last the summer. The nivaroli cut the snow and carried it down. Acireale keeps the word alive: its granita festival is the Nivarata, and the Condorelli family's gelateria is called Nevaroli.

The texture, coarse in Messina and creamy in Catania, still varies town by town.

Brioche col tuppoBreakfastThe bun on top has a name, and you are meant to eat it first.

The breakfast brioche of eastern Sicily wears a small ball of dough on top, the tuppo, the dialect word for a hair bun. You tear the tuppo off first and drag it through the granita. Gambero Rosso is specific: the brioche lightly warmed, and the moment the tuppo meets a creamy granita is the point of the whole thing. Order it at the counter, not at a table.

In Catania this is breakfast in summer and autumn, not dessert.

Going up, honestly

The summit rules, as of October 2026Read firstAbove about 2,500 m Etna is a regulated zone, and since 24 September it has been erupting again.

In August 2025 the Sicilian civil protection department adopted INGV-drawn perimeters: a Summit Zone, roughly everything above 2,500 m plus the upper Valle del Bove, and inside it a Permanent Hazard Zone around the active craters. On 13-14 August 2026 the summit towns suspended excursions in that inner zone until further notice; licensed guides, CAI staff and scientists are exempt. On 9 September La Sicilia reported the civil protection alert still at yellow, pre-alert since late June. At 5:00 on 24 September a new phase began: ash to about 4 km, aviation code red, landings at Catania capped at five an hour.

Any 'summit crater' trip sold this autumn is conditional on the morning's bulletin.

Valle del BoveVolcano walkA collapsed amphitheatre 5 by 7 km, walls a kilometre high, where most modern lava goes.

Stand on the rim and the ground falls away into a black horseshoe 5 by 7 km wide, its walls up to a kilometre high, left by the collapse of older volcanic centres. Because it opens east toward the Ionian, it catches many flows from the south-east crater, which is why Zafferana and Milo live under it. In 1992 lava ran out of the valley toward Zafferana; the army and US Marines dropped concrete blocks to divert it, and it stopped just short of the town. Several of this year's flows ran into it again.

The one place you can see the volcano's scale without climbing to the craters.

The city and its volcano

1669: when the lava reached CataniaHistoryThe worst eruption in Etna's recorded history crossed 17 km of farmland and climbed the city walls.

On 11 March 1669 a fissure opened near Nicolosi and built the twin cones now called Monti Rossi. For four months lava rolled south, burying villages, until it piled against Catania's walls, poured into the western quarter and filled the moat of Castello Ursino, Frederick II's seaside castle. The flow pushed the coastline out so far that the castle now stands inland. You can still put your hand on the 1669 lava at its foot, and walk up the Monti Rossi among the pines at Nicolosi.

The cones and the castle are the two ends of one flow: see both.

The city built of its volcanoCity walkBlack basalt underfoot, white limestone trim overhead: Catania is built of Etna.

After the 1693 earthquake Catania rebuilt with what was to hand: grey-black lava basalt for paving, walls and steps, and pale limestone from the Siracusa side for cornices and carvings. That is the city's two-tone look, sharpest in Piazza Duomo, Via Crociferi and the Benedictine monastery. The basole, the big paving slabs, are hand-dressed and still re-cut by stonemasons; after rain they shine like slate. Walk north up Via Etnea and the volcano itself sits at the end of the street on a clear day.

Look down as much as up: the paving tells you where you are.

Pappalardo's cut, 1669Volcano gemFifty men in wet cowhides tried to steer a lava flow with iron bars.

As the 1669 lava advanced on Catania, Diego Pappalardo led about fifty men wrapped in wet cowhides onto the flow. With iron bars they broke its cooled flank so the molten core would spill sideways. It worked, briefly, and turned the lava toward Paterno, whose citizens came out armed and drove them off. It is one of the first recorded attempts to divert lava, and the argument it started, whose town gets the lava, has never gone away.

True stories

Aci TrezzaSea storyOne boat, one cargo of lupins on credit, one storm: Verga's Malavoglia lost everything off these rocks.

Off Aci Trezza stand the Faraglioni dei Ciclopi, black basalt stacks that tradition makes the rocks the blinded Polyphemus hurled at Odysseus. In 1881 Giovanni Verga set I Malavoglia here. The Toscano family, nicknamed the unwilling though they work hardest in the village, own one boat, the Provvidenza. In 1863 Padron 'Ntoni buys a cargo of lupins on credit to sell along the coast; a storm sinks the voyage, drowns his son Bastianazzo and loses the cargo, and the family spends the rest of the book paying for it. In 1947-48 Luchino Visconti filmed La terra trema here with real fishermen and their families, in dialect, without a single professional actor.

★ The old hand's point: The boat is the family's capital and the sea is its creditor: one uninsured cargo and a working family is ruined.

11 January 1693Sea storyAt Augusta the sea drew back and left the harbour dry, then came back 165 m inland.

The earthquake of 11 January 1693 is the strongest in Italy's historical record. It flattened Catania, Noto, Ragusa and dozens of towns of the south-east; the baroque Val di Noto was built from its rubble. It also raised a tsunami along some 230 km of coast. At Augusta the sea first withdrew and left the harbour completely dry, then returned, flooding about 165 m inland. At Catania boats were thrown ashore; at Messina the sea pulled back about 100 m before flooding the basin; at Mascali it ran about 1.5 km inland. Modern simulations agree with the eyewitnesses: along almost the whole east coast the first motion was a withdrawal.

★ The old hand's point: When the sea goes out with no tide to explain it, it is coming back: high ground, or deep water if you are already off soundings.

Piazza Duomo, Catania and 'u LiotruSquareA lava-stone elephant carrying an Egyptian-style obelisk: the city's mascot since the Middle Ages.

In the middle of the square stands the Fontana dell'Elefante, assembled in 1736 by Giovanni Battista Vaccarini: a Roman-era elephant carved from basalt, a granite obelisk covered in pseudo-Egyptian hieroglyphs, and Saint Agatha's emblem on top. Catanesi call it 'u Liotru, from Eliodoro, an eighth-century sorcerer who, in legend, rode the elephant by magic. Around it: the cathedral where Bellini lies, the town hall in Palazzo degli Elefanti, and the Porta Uzeda leading down to the fish market.

Every Catania walk starts or ends here; the elephant is the meeting point.

Monastero dei BenedettiniMonasteryThe monks stopped the 1669 lava at their garden wall; now students eat lunch on top of it.

San Nicolo l'Arena was founded in the sixteenth century, hit by the 1669 lava, which stopped against its walls, and wrecked in 1693, then rebuilt on a scale few monasteries in Europe match: two cloisters, a library, a church with an unfinished facade. How to read it

Etna DOC: the volcano as a wine regionEtna wineSixty producers in 2013, 441 by 2024: the fastest-growing mountain wine in Europe.

The Etna DOC dates from 1968. Its reds are Nerello Mascalese, softened with Nerello Cappuccio; its whites Carricante, with Catarratto. Bush vines, alberello, climb lava terraces to the DOC's 1,000 m limit and beyond. The Dish — where the list comes from

The stories behind the canon, and where they are argued rather than settled.

#1
DISH

Granita and the brioche col tuppo

Granita is the sherbet of the Arab kitchen made with snow: until ice machines arrived, snow was packed into stone pits on Etna's flanks, the neviere, and carried down to the city. The Catanese granita is stirred as it freezes so the crystals stay loose, and it is eaten for breakfast with a brioche whose topknot, the tuppo, is the first thing torn off and dipped.
WhereSavia, Via Etnea · Bar Alecci, Gravina · Maps →
#2
DISH

The arancino

Catania says arancino, pointed like the volcano; Palermo says arancina, round. The Accademia della Crusca, Italy's language academy, has ruled that both forms are correct. The 'cone like Etna' explanation is folklore, but the shape is real: in Catania the ragù one is pointed and the pistachio one is often round.
WhereSavia; Canusciuti for the horse-meat one · Maps →
#3
DISH

Minne di Sant'Agata

Small domed cassatas of sponge, ricotta and candied fruit, iced white with a cherry on top, made for Catania's patron saint. Their shape recalls her martyrdom in 251. Every pasticceria makes them for 3–5 February; Savia sells them all year.
WherePasticceria Savia, Catania · Maps →
