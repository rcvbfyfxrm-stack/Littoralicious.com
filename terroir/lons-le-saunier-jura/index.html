Lons-le-Saunier

Jura · Bourgogne-Franche-Comté · France
"A salt town at the foot of the Revermont: arcades of golden stone, brine rising in the park, and up the slope behind it the yellow wine that waits six years in the barrel."
The name Lons-le-Saunier: Lons of the salt-maker. The town was named for the brine under it.
The saunier was the man who boiled brine into salt, and for most of its history this was a salt town before it was anything else: the springs still rise in the Parc des Bains. The other word you will need is the Revermont, the first wrinkle of the Jura mountains, whose west-facing slope of marl carries the vines from here north to Arbois.

Lons-le-Saunier is the prefecture of the Jura, a salt town of about 17,000 at the foot of the wine slope: Comté cut at three ages under the arcades, Morteau and cancoillotte at the Thursday market, the poularde with Vin Jaune and morels, and the cellars of Château-Chalon, Pupillin and Arbois within the hour. Make or break: taste Vin Jaune where it is made, and Comté side by side at three ages. The warning: from November many sites and some kitchens shut for the winter, and the best tables are on the slope, not in town: check the days on every card and plan a driver.

The soul of the place — the essay
A town built on brine, a slope of marl, a wine that waits under a veil, and a kitchen of kept things
THE ESSAY

Lons sits on salt. Under the town lie beds of rock salt laid down when shallow seas dried out, and brine still rises through the faults into the Parc des Bains. The salt made the town, the town gave the slope a market, and the slope made the strangest wine in France.

The salt came first: worked here since prehistory, it paid for the abbeys and the castles, and it travelled with the cheese. The geology and the springs have a card of their own below, and the story of how salt and the village dairies made Comté is the history fold. What it means at table is a kitchen of cured and kept things: smoked sausage, aged cheese, a wine that survives six years in a barrel no one tops up.

Then the slope. East of town the Revermont rises in steps of marl and limestone, and the vines run north along it for some fifty kilometres, through Château-Chalon on its cliff, Poligny, Pupillin and Arbois. It is a small vineyard with five grapes and a way of working none of its neighbours share: Savagnin left under a veil of yeast until it turns yellow, nutty and salty. The wine, its rules and the molecule behind its smell are on the Vin Jaune card; its family tree is a curiosity.

The quintessence. The kitchen is the mountain’s, finished with the wine. Comté from the cooperative dairies of the plateau, cut young and milky or old and crystalline; the Morteau sausage smoked in the farmhouse chimney; cancoillotte poured warm over potatoes; Mont d’Or in its spruce box once the cows come down for winter; and the Bresse hen from the plain to the west, poached with Vin Jaune and morels, which is the dish the whole region is built to explain.

Where it comes from

The town is small and walkable: the Place de la Liberté and the arcades of rue du Commerce, the market squares around the theatre, the park of the salt springs. The tables that matter are partly here and partly up the slope, at Arbois and Pupillin, about forty minutes north by road, with a star further on at Dole.

Arbois is the wine town, and Pasteur’s: he grew up there and worked on wine in a laboratory you can still see. Château-Chalon makes only Vin Jaune. Pupillin calls itself the world capital of the pale red Ploussard. Each has its card.

Where the money sits

The money in the Jura is in the barrels and the cheese cellars. A bottle of Vin Jaune has been six years in the making, and the most famous natural-wine growers sell everything they make before it is bottled. Most visitors see Arbois, taste a Savagnin on a terrace and buy a clavelin.

That is the line you can taste. The value is at the Thursday market before noon, a fromager who will cut three ages of Comté side by side, a tavern lunch of Morteau and Comté, and a cellar where the person pouring made the wine.

And the clock is the cow and the vine. The cows go up to summer pasture in spring, and the summer-milk Comté is the richest; the harvest is in September; and when the herds come down in autumn the Mont d’Or comes back, in its spruce box, until spring. The year turns on the first weekend of February, when a wine village opens the new Vin Jaune at the Percée. Whoever comes in June gets a green slope and long evenings; whoever comes in November gets the Mont d’Or, the game and the first fires, and finds some doors shut for the winter.

1264 — Salt, shared milk and a cheese built to keep
How the Jura's salt trade and its village dairies, the fruitières, made Comté a cheese large enough to store, carry and sell.
THE STORY

Comté is a cheese of scarcity turned into surplus. A mountain farm with a few cows could not make a wheel big enough to last the winter, so from the 13th century villages pooled their milk in shared dairies, the fruitières. Salt from Salins-les-Bains, a few hours away, was the region's wealth; the cheese became its other export.

The springs of Salins-les-Bains may have been worked from about 4300 BC, and from the 13th century the town ran two saltworks, the Grande Saunerie and the Puits-à-Muire. By the late Middle Ages they produced about 7,400 tonnes a year, salt that supplied Franche-Comté, Switzerland and part of the duchy of Burgundy. In 1674 the two works merged under the French crown. When the forests that fuelled the pans ran short, the brine was piped to Ledoux's Saline royale at Arc-et-Senans, from 1779 to 1895.

The first fruitière's date is disputed. The European Commission's Comté page gives one founded at Déservillers in the Doubs in 1253; Alain Mélo's study of the Franche-Comté fruitières starts its chronology in 1264; a regional agriculture office names Déservillers in 1267 and Poligny in 1284 as the oldest known. The logic is not disputed: a large pressed, cooked wheel takes far more milk than one farm gives in a day, so neighbours pooled theirs. Early on, members took turns making cheese at home.

A court recognised the appellation in 1952, under the name Gruyère de Comté, and a decree created the AOC Comté in 1958. The trade's interprofession, the CIGC, was set up by decree in 1963 at Poligny, and European protection as a PDO followed in 1996, though one source gives 1992. Sources count between about 140 and 150 fruitières today, and 2024 rules cap merged dairies at 7.5 million litres a year. In 2025 the trade sold a record 62,480 tonnes.

Taste what the story is about: Comté at three ages from the fromager under the arcades or the Thursday market, and the salt itself at the spring in the Parc des Bains.

The Parc des Bains and the Source Saléesalt and spaSeven hectares of old trees around a spa that still pipes in the town's salt water

The Parc des Bains is where Lons tried to become a spa town. Seven hectares of trees gathered from five continents, a bandstand from about 1900, and a modern Korean garden on the extension. At its heart are the Thermes Lédonia, still using the salt water: a panoramic pool kept at 32°C, a hammam and a sauna, with cures for joints and skin. The name goes back to Ledonia, the spring the Romans worked, later called the Puits-Salé. Lons never got official spa-resort status after the First World War, for reasons of hygiene, which explains the modest scale. On an autumn morning mist lifts off the brine basin.

Salt, grapes and a gazette

The brine under the town, a grape with a famous family, and the first list of appellations.

The salt under the towncuriosityLons is named for its salt: the Romans worked the spring, and saltworks ran into the 1890s

The town is built over Triassic rock salt, and brine still finds its way up through the faults. People lived by the salt spring from the Neolithic; at the Étang du Saloir, on the Montmorot side, salt-making is attested from the Bronze Age. The Gaulish Sequani settled by the spring, the Romans worked the main source, Ledonia, later the Puits-Salé, and the town became Lons-le-Saunier, saunier being the salt-maker. In 1743 a royal decree founded the Montmorot saltworks, built 1744 to 1752; another plant at Montaigu started in 1893 and was making 2,500 tonnes a year by 1895.

Why go. Every street name with 'salé' or 'saline' in it is a trace of what paid for this town.

Touch it today
The 18th-century saltworks buildings at Montmorot on the western edge of town (restored), and the brine itself in the Parc des Bains basin and the Thermes Lédonia pool
Where the sources argue
Whether salt was already being worked in the Neolithic or only from the Bronze Age; the old guide's closure date of 1966 and its 300 g/l brine figure could not be confirmed
Savagnin's family treecuriosityThe Jura's white grape, Traminer by another name, sits behind Sauvignon Blanc and Grüner Veltliner

Savagnin is the Jura's name for Traminer, and DNA has put it near the root of half the white-wine list. Austrian work in the late 1990s found Traminer as one parent of Grüner Veltliner; the other turned out to be a single neglected vine at St. Georgen am Leithagebirge, near Eisenstadt, found in 2000 and confirmed in 2007, officially named Mater Veltlinis in 2023. Sauvignon Blanc and Chenin Blanc each show a parent-offspring link with Savagnin, which makes Savagnin a grandparent of Cabernet Sauvignon, a Sauvignon Blanc and Cabernet Franc cross. Gewürztraminer is its aromatic, pink-skinned form.

Why go. Pour an ouillé Savagnin next to a Sauvignon Blanc and look for the family resemblance.

Touch it today
Order a Savagnin ouillé (topped-up, fresh) beside one aged sous voile (walnut and curry): the same grape, two styles; about EUR 18-45 a bottle (seen May 2026)
Where the sources argue
DNA shows a parent-offspring link but not its direction: Savagnin is taken as the parent of Sauvignon and Chenin from history, not genetics, and one glossary says an earlier suspected Savagnin x Chenin pedigree was disproved. The old guide's 'UC Davis/INRA 1999' credit could not be confirmed
Arbois in the first gazettecuriosityOn 15 May 1936 France named its first six AOCs, and Arbois heads the list

The 1935 law created the appellation d'origine contrôlée; the first decrees came on 15 May 1936, a day after the agriculture minister's report on Châteauneuf-du-Pape cleared the way. Six names went through: Arbois, Cassis, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Cognac, Monbazillac and Tavel. Arbois sits first on the alphabetical list, and from there grew the local line that the Jura holds France's oldest appellation. The campaign behind the law was led by the Rhône grower Baron Pierre Le Roy de Boiseaumarié and the Bordeaux senator Joseph Capus. All six turned 90 in 2026, and INAO marked the anniversary in February.

Why go. Read 'Appellation Arbois Contrôlée' on a label as a date: May 1936, among the first six in France.

Touch it today
Any bottle labelled Arbois or Arbois-Pupillin: taste one at Domaine Stéphane Tissot's caveau, 35 Place de la Liberté in Arbois, or order one with lunch at La Finette, whose list is all Jura
Where the sources argue
Arbois's 'first AOC in France' is a local and guidebook claim; INAO dates all six to the same day, and Châteauneuf-du-Pape points to the minister's report on its case, dated 14 May 1936. The old guide's inclusion of Vosne-Romanée was wrong: the sixth was Cognac

Born here

The officer who wrote the Marseillaise.

Rouget de Lisleborn hereThe man who wrote the Marseillaise was born above a shop under the Lons arcades

Claude-Joseph Rouget de Lisle was born at 24 rue du Commerce on 10 May 1760. A young engineering officer, he wrote the Chant de guerre pour l'armée du Rhin in Strasbourg in April 1792; troops from Marseille carried it north and it took their city's name. The family apartment is now a small museum, the Maison Rouget de Lisle, built on the donation of André Lançon and a Maison des Illustres since 2011, with an old lime tree in the courtyard. In 2026 the town marked 190 years since his death in 1836 with free summer visits.

Why go. Stand in the narrow rooms where he was born, then walk to his statue: the anthem feels smaller and closer.

Saint-Désiré and its cryptromanesque cryptBelow the choir, an 11th-century crypt holds the empty stone coffin of the town's patron saint

Saint-Désiré is the oldest standing layer of Lons. Why go. Go down into the crypt: ten minutes underground and you are a thousand years back.

The Saline Royaleunesco saltworksLedoux's half-moon royal saltworks, fed by brine piped in from Salins through the forest

Learn Savagnin, Ploussard and Trousseau, and the difference between a topped-up wine and one left under its veil, and every list in the Jura makes sense. Then taste in a cellar.

Ask for it by name

The yellow wine, the two red grapes, and the appellations on the label.

Vin Jaunethe yellow wineSavagnin left under a skin of yeast for six years, then sold in a bottle that holds only 62 cl

Vin Jaune is Savagnin put into old barrels and never topped up. As the wine evaporates, a film of yeast, the voile, spreads over its surface and keeps it from turning to vinegar while letting a little air through. It stays there for more than six years; producers count six years and three months from harvest before it may leave the cellar. What survives from a litre is roughly 62 cl, the size of the squat clavelin it must be sold in. The smell is the molecule sotolon, isolated from fenugreek in 1975: walnut, curry, maple syrup. Open it an hour ahead and serve it cool, not cold.

Why go. Order one glass with a wedge of old Comté before the poularde arrives; the walnut in both makes the case for the whole region.

In the glass
Deep gold; walnut, curry, dried apple and spice from sotolon (about 120 to 268 µg per litre in published analyses); dry, sharp, very long. A thinner voile than Sherry's flor, at lower alcohol, and never fortified
On the label
Château-Chalon (Vin Jaune only) or Vin Jaune under Arbois, Côtes du Jura or L'Étoile; always the 62 cl clavelin, and a vintage at least six years back
Ask for
A glass to taste before buying, then a clavelin from a named house such as Macle at Château-Chalon or Tissot at Arbois; about EUR 35 to 90 a clavelin (seen May 2026). Savagnin's ancestry is its own card
Poulsard and Trousseauthe jura redsTwo native reds: one pale enough to read through, the other dark, firm and tasting of iron

The Jura's own red grapes cover about a fifth of its vines between them, Poulsard (called Ploussard in Pupillin) a little more than Trousseau. Poulsard has thin skins and makes a wine so pale it can pass for a dark rosé: orange-ruby, cranberry and cherry, almost no tannin, bright acid. Pupillin, above Arbois, signs itself the world capital of Ploussard. Trousseau is its opposite, deeper in colour, firmer, black cherry with an iron, peppery edge, and at its best on the gravels around Arbois. Pour them side by side and the Jura's red range is laid out in two glasses.

Why go. Ask La Finette in Arbois, whose list is all Jura, for one of each, and taste the pale one first.

In the glass
Poulsard: translucent orange-ruby, red berries, light and acidic, serve at 12 to 14 °C. Trousseau: darker ruby, black cherry, iron and pepper, more grip, serve at 14 to 16 °C
On the label
Arbois or Arbois-Pupillin, Côtes du Jura, with Poulsard, Ploussard or Trousseau on the label; Tissot's Singulier is a Trousseau
Ask for
Poulsard with Morteau or Morbier; Trousseau with an 18-month Comté or game. Pupillin's Fruitière Vinicole for a village Ploussard; Overnoy-Houillon bottles if a list has one. About EUR 18 to 40 for Poulsard, 22 to 55 for Trousseau (seen May 2026)
The Jura appellationsreading the labelFour place names and three products on about 2,100 ha: the smallest serious vineyard map in France

The Jura vineyard runs as a thin strip of slope, the Revermont, from south of Lons to north of Arbois, about 2,100 ha in all by the CIVJ's count from 2023 declarations, with some 350 growers and co-operative members. Four appellations name places: Arbois (767 ha, every style), Côtes du Jura (720 ha, the broad regional), L'Étoile (86 ha, whites only) and Château-Chalon (55 ha, Vin Jaune and nothing else). Three name products: Crémant, Macvin and Marc du Jura. Small also means fragile: frost and mildew cut the 2024 harvest by about 71 percent; 2025 came back to normal volumes.

Why go. Learn the four place names before your first cellar; Arbois's 1936 first-gazette story has its own card.

In the glass
Five main grapes: Chardonnay (about 43% of the vines), Savagnin (25%), Pinot Noir, Poulsard and Trousseau. Whites come either topped-up (ouillé, fresh) or under voile (nutty, oxidative)
On the label
Arbois (Arbois-Pupillin for that village), Château-Chalon, Côtes du Jura, L'Étoile; plus Crémant du Jura, Macvin du Jura, Marc du Jura. L'Étoile's name is argued over: the star-shaped crinoid fossils in its soil, or the five hills that ring the village like a star
Ask for
Ouillé or sous voile when you order a white; it changes everything. 9.7 million bottles sold in 2022-23 (CIVJ)

Before, after, and the sweet one

The apéritif, the bubbles and the brandy, and the wine of dried grapes.

Macvin, Crémant and Marcbefore and afterThe apéritif made of must and brandy, the everyday sparkling, and the oak-aged digestif

Three Jura appellations name a product rather than a place. Macvin du Jura, an AOC since 14 November 1991, is a mistelle: unfermented grape must stopped with Marc du Jura, roughly two parts juice to one of brandy, then aged in oak. It comes out sweet, grapey, around 16 percent, and is poured cold before dinner. Crémant du Jura, recognised in 1995, is bottle-fermented sparkling, mostly Chardonnay, nine months at least on its lees: the bubble cellars open at any hour. Marc du Jura, the pomace brandy, became an AOC of its own by decree on 2 February 2015 and closes the meal.

Why go. Start a cellar visit with a Crémant and end it with a thimble of Macvin; it is the Jura's own way into and out of a meal.

In the glass
Macvin: amber or ruby, raisin and walnut, sweet, about 16%. Crémant: fine bubble, apple and brioche, dry. Marc: amber after oak, dried fruit and fire
On the label
Macvin du Jura (blanc, rosé or rouge); Crémant du Jura (brut, often blanc de blancs); Marc du Jura
Ask for
A Macvin as apéritif, a Crémant brut by the glass, a Marc after the cheese; EUR 14 to 35 a bottle (seen May 2026). Domaine Macle pours both Macvin and Crémant by appointment; the Tissot caveau in Arbois opens with its Crémant Indigène
Vin de Paillethe sweet oneGrapes dried for weeks on straw or in airy lofts, pressed into a few spoonfuls of honeyed wine each

After harvest, the best bunches of Chardonnay, Savagnin and Poulsard are laid on racks or straw, or hung from the beams of a ventilated loft, and left to shrivel. The rules ask for at least six weeks; many growers wait three months or more, until the must weighs 320 to 420 g of sugar a litre. Pressing gives very little juice. It ferments slowly to at least 14 percent and then ages three years, at least eighteen months of it in wood, before release. It smells of candied orange, quince and honey, with a cut of acidity that keeps it from cloying.

Why go. Ask for it with an old Comté instead of dessert; the salt in the cheese meets the candied fruit.

In the glass
Amber gold; candied orange, dried apricot, quince, honey; sweet but fresh
On the label
Vin de Paille under Arbois, Côtes du Jura or L'Étoile only, usually in a half bottle of 37.5 cl
Ask for
A small glass with Comté, or alone after the meal; about EUR 35 to 65 a half bottle (seen May 2026)

Under the arcades the café boards chalk up 'vin jaune' by the glass, and nobody brings the bottle to the table. That is the problem. The 2026 vintage, picked from 10 Augustnew vintageThe Jura's first secateurs went in on 10 August: 'never started that early', growers told France 3

The 2026 Jura harvest began on 10 August, a date the Société de viticulture du Jura called fairly historic, about three weeks ahead of normal. The first rows cut were mostly grapes for Crémant; Chardonnay followed in late August, Savagnin and the red varieties during September. Early does not mean plentiful: on the society's network of 21 monitored plots, 29 per cent showed losses above half the crop, and growers spoke of pressure on the whole trade. The press still calls the vintage promising. The Savagnin picked this year goes under its veil now and will not be Vin Jaune until 2033 at the earliest.

Why go. Ask a grower about yields plot by plot: in 2026 they swung hard between neighbours.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates and their honest passports: which are the Jura’s own, which it shares with the Doubs and the Bresse, and which come down from the mountain.

ORIGIN

Poularde au Vin Jaune et morilles

A Bresse hen poached, then finished in a sauce of Vin Jaune, cream and dried morels, the dish served at every grand table of the region and in the better taverns. The bird comes from the Bresse plain just west of Lons, the only French poultry with a protected appellation; the wine and the mushrooms come down from the slope and the woods. The wine’s nutty, salty edge is what makes the sauce taste of the Jura and nowhere else. Origin. The Jura’s own; the bird is from the Bresse, next door

Eat it — La Table du Grapiot, Pupillin, or La Finette, Arbois
ORIGIN

Comté

A great wheel of cooked, pressed cheese made in the village dairies of the massif from the raw milk of Montbéliarde and French Simmental cows, then aged for months or years. Young, it tastes of milk and butter; older, of hazelnut and broth, with white crystals that crunch. Ask for three ages cut side by side: it is the lesson the whole region gives. Origin. The Jura massif, French side: the Jura, the Doubs and the Ain

Saucisse de Morteau

A thick pork sausage from the Haut-Doubs, smoked slowly in the great pyramid chimney of a mountain farm, the tuyé, and closed at one end with a little wooden peg, the cheville, which is how you know it is the real one. Simmer it gently and never prick it; eat it with potatoes, lentils or cancoillotte. Origin. The Haut-Doubs, the mountains east of Lons

Eat it — the Thursday market, or La Finette, Arbois

Cancoillotte

A runny, nearly fat-free cheese made by melting dried skimmed-milk curd, the metton, with butter, water and garlic or white wine. Sold cold in jars in every market of Franche-Comté and almost nowhere else; warmed and poured over boiled potatoes or Morteau, it is the region’s everyday comfort. Origin. Franche-Comté’s own

Mont d’Or

A soft winter cheese ringed with a strap of spruce bark and sold in a spruce box, made only in the cold half of the year when the cows are down from the summer pasture and give too little milk for a wheel of Comté. Baked whole in its box, la boîte chaude, with white wine and garlic, and spooned over potatoes. Origin. The Haut-Doubs, on the French side of the Swiss border

Eat it — the Thursday market, in season from 10 September to 10 May

Morbier

A supple cow’s-milk cheese with a fine black line through the middle. The usual story is that farmers dusted the evening curd with ash to keep it until the morning’s milk filled the mould; today the line is vegetable charcoal and the story is a story. Mild, a little bitter at the rind, the cheese of the tavern board. Origin. The Jura massif; named for the village of Morbier

Bleu de Gex

A mild blue made in the high valleys of the Haut-Jura and the Pays de Gex from the raw milk of Montbéliarde cows, its blue grown naturally through the paste. Creamy rather than sharp, with a floury rind; the high-pasture cousin of the region’s cooked cheeses. Origin. The Haut-Jura and the Pays de Gex

Eat it — the Thursday market

Pochouse

A stew of river fish, pike, perch, tench, eel, cooked in white wine with garlic, onions and lardons and finished with cream, served over toast rubbed with garlic. A dish of the rivers rather than the mountain, shared with the Saône valley downstream; no table kept in this guide lists it in 2026, so take it where you find it. Origin.
