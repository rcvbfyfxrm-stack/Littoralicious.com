The names A threshold, a burned forest, or a sun-baked rock: Prague has never agreed on what its own name means.
Cosmas's chronicle of 1119–1125 tells the legend: the prophetess Libuše sent men into the forest to build where they found a man making a threshold, práh, and so the city was named Praha. Linguists are less poetic and do not agree. Josef Dobrovský read práh as a ford or rapid, the river crossing where the town began; since the 19th century most have preferred the old verb pražiti, to burn or roast, for a forest burned to clear a settlement, or, in Antonín Profous's version, a hot dry place on the shale where the castle stands. The river is older than all of it: Vltava and German Moldau are both traced to Germanic *wilt ahwa, 'wild water', written Fuldaha in 872 and Wlitaua in Cosmas. Hradčany is simply the castle town, from hrad; Vyšehrad is the high castle; Malá Strana, the Lesser Side, is the smaller town across the bridge. Žižkov, named in 1877, honours Jan Žižka, whose Hussites beat Emperor Sigismund's army on Vítkov Hill on 14 July 1420; it has more than 300 pubs. Karlín, laid out from 1817, is named for Caroline Augusta of Bavaria, fourth wife of Emperor Francis I. Vinohrady was Královské Vinohrady, the Royal Vineyards, from 1867 to 1960, after vines going back to Charles IV's 14th century.

The soul of Prague — the essay
The tank pub at a quarter past six, the kavárna morning, and the river nobody looks at: the three rooms the whole city follows from
THE ESSAY

Every stone of the old city came in on the Vltava, the last commercial timber raft tied up in Prague on 12 September 1961, and a country with no coast registered ocean-going ships in Prague until the 1990s. Then the embankments were raised, the trams took over, and the visitors started looking up — at the castle, the clock, the spires — instead of down at the water and into the glass. Look down and in. Prague is the place where a lager is poured with three different heads, where a Cubist café that closed within a decade was rebuilt from archive photographs in 2004 and opens at nine, and where the best svíčková in the centre costs two hundred crowns less than the one on the square. Everything in this guide is within twenty minutes of the Charles Bridge, and almost none of it is on it.

There is an hour, most weekday evenings, when the whole logic of this city shows itself in one room. It is a quarter past six in a pub with a Pilsner tank in the cellar. The waiter walks the floor with a tray of full glasses and does not ask anybody anything: he reads the tables, sets down a half-litre wherever a glass has gone empty without a beer mat on top, and makes a pencil mark on the paper slip by your elbow. Nobody orders. Nobody is in a hurry. The foam is wet and dense and sits two fingers deep, and the man at your table, whom you have never met, raises his glass and looks you in the eye before he drinks.

That ritual is the character of Prague and it is worth understanding rather than photographing. Czechs have drunk more beer per head than anyone on earth for more than three decades — about 121 litres each in 2025 — and they have kept the grammar that goes with it: the hladinka with its clean collar of foam, the šnyt that is mostly head and a small glass of beer under it, the mlíko that is almost all foam and drunk in one go. The world's first golden lager was brewed an hour down the railway in Plzeň in 1842, and a coachman carried it to Jakub Pinkas's pub in Prague the following spring. The pub is still pouring.

The second room is the kavárna. Prague learned the coffee house from Vienna and then made it a parliament: newspapers on wooden poles, marble tables, a dessert cabinet of věneček and větrník, and permission to stay all morning over one cup. Writers and dissidents argued in these rooms under three regimes; one of them, above a street of souvenir shops, is a Cubist café by Josef Gočár, lost in the 1920s and rebuilt from photographs in 2004. Go once in the morning, sit by the window, and do not look at your phone.

The third thing is the river, and it is the one visitors miss. The Vltava has four locks inside the city, public ferries that cost a tram ticket, and two paddle steamers older than the Second World War. Timber rafters brought the forests of southern Bohemia down it to Podskalí until the last commercial raft on 12 September 1961, and their craft was inscribed by UNESCO in 2022. Floods have come back through the old town every few generations — in August 2002 Karlín went under and eighteen metro stations flooded — and the city marks where. A sailor reading this will recognise the type: a place that lives by its water and has learned to pretend it doesn't.

Everything else follows from those three rooms. The food is heavy because it was cooked for people who worked outside in winter; the sauces are the point, the dumplings are for the sauce, and the beer is for the dumplings. The good tables are small and mostly new: MICHELIN only published its first Czech guide in December 2025, and the five Prague rooms it starred cook Bohemian produce with a seriousness the old town never needed. And the city is at its best in exactly the season the tourists leave — October, when the burčák is cloudy, the gardens under the castle turn, and the tank pubs fill with people who live here.

From Rettigová to the red guide
A cookbook written in Czech to save a language, a ham the Habsburgs ate, the stars that came in December 2025 — and what came between
FOOD HISTORY

Every plate on this page is downstream of three things: a nineteenth-century cookbook written as an act of national revival, an empire that taught Prague to bake, and forty years when the recipe was a state standard.

In 1826 the Hradec Králové printer Jan Hostivít Pospíšil published Domácí kuchařka by Magdalena Dobromila Rettigová, a treatise on meat and fasting dishes 'for Czech and Moravian daughters'. It was a political book dressed as a practical one. Written in Czech when an educated Bohemian household ran in German, it carried the language into middle-class kitchens during the National Revival. Four Czech editions appeared in her lifetime, 1826, 1831, 1837 and 1844, and, the telling detail, four German ones too.

The Habsburg table set the grammar. Karolína Vávrová's nineteenth-century Pražská kuchařka follows the French order of multi-course meals common across the monarchy; schnitzel, dumpling and strudel belong to a shared imperial larder more than to any one nation. Prague's own contribution was a ham. In 1857 František Zvěřina produced the first Pražská šunka, a brined, lightly smoked, then boiled leg of pork; from 1879 Antonín Chmel made it industrially and by 1929 employed 250 people. Nobody registered the name, so the empire copied it, then France and America. Interwar Czechoslovakia prosecuted fakes at home; recent attempts to protect the name internationally met objections from several countries. It still has no EU protection.

The First Republic ate in rooms like Café Imperial, whose ceramic hall on Na Poříčí opened in 1914 and still serves breakfast at seven. After 1948 the state took the restaurants; for forty years a pub, a canteen and a hotel dining room belonged to the same owner and cooked from the same repertoire.

The rebuild began when private kitchens became legal again. V Zátiší counts 35 years this year. La Degustation opened in 2006 to cook Czech food from old sources, buying nearly everything within 250 km; its sister counter Marie B displays an 1894 cookbook by the cook it is named after. On 11 December 2025, in Mariánské Lázně, MICHELIN published its first guide to Czechia: one two-star, eight one-stars, five of them in Prague. Two hundred years after Rettigová, the Czech kitchen is again writing itself down.

10 QUARTERS

Prague was four separate towns until 1784: Old Town, New Town, Malá Strana and Hradčany, each with its own council and its own grudges. Then, in 1922, Greater Prague absorbed the industrial and residential towns around it in one stroke: Vinohrady, Žižkov, Karlín, Vršovice and Smíchov were all independent until that year. Read the city as a set of stitched-together towns and the street plan starts to make sense.

Inside the old walls

The right bank: the medieval market town, the ghetto that was cleared, and Charles IV's planned city three times its size.

Staré Město (Old Town)the market town46,000 people lived here in 1869. 6,272 do now — and the square is priced for the view.

Old Town began as a marketplace on the Vltava bank, and it still behaves like one: every lane funnels you towards the square. In 1338 the councillors bought a merchant's house from the Volflin family and made it their town hall; the tower came in 1364, and the clock on it has drawn a crowd ever since. What changed is who lives here. In 1869 the census found some 46,000 people in these streets; in 2021 it found 6,272. The lanes between the square and the river are hotels and shops now.

Why go. Take the square at first light, then walk east to Náměstí Republiky or south to Národní, where Prague still does ordinary business.

Josefovwhat survived0.09 square kilometres, 977 residents, and what a clearance of 1893–1913 left standing.

Jews lived in Prague from the 10th century, and at its medieval peak more than 18,000 people crowded into this quarter. Emperor Joseph II's Edict of Tolerance of 1782 began their emancipation, and in 1850 the quarter took his name. Then, between 1893 and 1913, the city cleared it as a slum and drove Pařížská, a boulevard of luxury shops, straight through it. What stands is what the demolition spared: six synagogues, the Jewish Town Hall and the Old Jewish Cemetery.

Why go. Walk Pařížská, then turn into the side streets to see what the demolition spared.

Nové Město (New Town)the planned cityCharles IV's planned city of 1348: three times the old town, with a horse market that became Wenceslas Square.

In 1348 Charles IV founded a second, independent royal city beside the old one: 7.5 square kilometres, about three times the size of Old Town, with streets far wider than anything medieval Prague had seen and markets assigned by trade. Wenceslas Square was the horse market; Charles Square was the cattle market, at about 550 by 150 metres long the largest square in Europe. Its streets were laid out for traffic six centuries before there was any. Today it is where the city works and shops — and where it hides its covered passages.

Why go. Walk Wenceslas Square from the bottom, then leave it sideways through the arcades.

The two rocks and the town below the castle

Left bank and high ground: the baroque town rebuilt after a fire, the castle hill, and the fortress legend says came first.

Malá Stranarebuilt after 1541German merchants built it in 1257; one fire in 1541 turned it baroque.

King Ottokar II founded a new town under the castle in 1257 and filled it with German craftsmen and merchants. When Charles IV built his own New Town it was demoted to the Lesser Town, and in the 17th century the name Malá Strana — the Little Side — stuck. A fire in 1541 burned much of it down, and the rebuilding, first Renaissance and then baroque, is the town you see: palaces that are now embassies and ministries, and the Senate in Wallenstein Palace. 22,140 people lived here in 1869; 4,584 in 2021.

Why go. Come early to the Čertovka millstream on Kampa, before the bridge crowds walk down.

Hradčany and Nový Světthe castle townBehind the castle palaces, a lane of tiny houses where Tycho Brahe watched the sky.

Hradčany grew round the castle from about 1320 and was its own borough until 1784: noble palaces, the Loreta, Strahov. At its back edge is the surprise. Nový Svět, the New World, was built over a filled-in valley of the Brusnice stream by the Deer Moat, burned in 1420 and again in 1541, and rebuilt small — a lane of low houses that still feels like a village. The astronomer Tycho Brahe lived here; so, Czech Wikipedia says, does the surrealist filmmaker Jan Švankmajer. 1,774 people live in the whole district.

Why go. Walk Nový Svět and Černínská at dusk, after the castle coaches have gone.

Vyšehradthe legendary first seatLegend says Prague began on this rock above the river; archaeology has not agreed.

Three kilometres upriver from the castle, a fort stood on this rock from the 10th century. Legend makes it the seat of the prophetess Libuše and the cradle of Prague; that remains a legend. Its real peak came in the 11th century, when Vratislav II made it a royal residence, and from that century the Romanesque Rotunda of St Martin still stands. The Habsburgs turned it into a baroque fortress; it joined Prague only in 1883. Now it is a park with long views up the Vltava and the national cemetery.

Why go. Slavín cemetery first, then the walls at sunset — and Chochol's cubist houses at the foot of the rock.

The towns Prague swallowed in 1922

Independent industrial and residential towns until Greater Prague took them in: where people actually live, eat and drink.

Vinohradythe royal vineyardsThe royal vineyards are apartment blocks now — but the vines still make about 4,000 litres a year.

The name means vineyards, and from the 14th century these slopes were planted with vines; from 1788 to 1867 the district was called the Vineyard Hills. It joined Prague in 1922 as a bourgeois suburb of tall apartment houses, and in 1960 the Communists cut it into three districts — on purpose, locals still say. The vines survived in one park, Grébovka, which presses about 4,000 litres a year. Around it: Plečnik's church on Jiřího z Poděbrad, Mocker's neo-Gothic St Ludmila on Náměstí Míru, and Riegrovy sady for the view.

Why go. A glass at the Grébovka vineyard pavilion is wine from inside the city limits.

Žižkovthe free republicNamed for a one-eyed Hussite general, it calls itself a republic and keeps more than 300 pubs.

Jan Žižka's Hussites beat an imperial army on Vítkov Hill in 1420, and the district below the hill took his name. Franz Joseph made it a city in 1881, with about 21,000 people; it joined Prague in 1922 and never quite stopped acting independent. Working-class and bohemian, it was home to Jaroslav Hašek and to the Nobel poet Jaroslav Seifert, and it styles itself the Free Republic of Žižkov. It is often said to have Europe's highest density of pubs per head. Treat the ranking as a boast; the 300-odd pubs are real.

Why go. Climb Vítkov to the Žižka statue at sunset, then walk downhill into the pubs under the TV tower.

Karlínthe flood-built revivalA factory grid named for an empress, drowned in 2002 and rebuilt as the city's best lunch street.

Karlín was laid out from 1817 on cheap riverside land and named for Caroline Augusta of Bavaria, the fourth wife of Emperor Francis I. It was a factory town on a grid, and in 1850 the Negrelli Viaduct — 1,111 metres, still counted the longest bridge on Czech territory — carried the railway over it. In August 2002 the hundred-year flood put the whole district under water. The rebuilding turned warehouses into offices, flats and kitchens. At its eastern end stands the Invalidovna, a veterans' home modelled on Les Invalides of which only a fraction was ever built.

Why go. Walk Křižíkova and Thámova at lunchtime, when the district eats.

The street and the hotels

Late afternoon on Pařížská, then up to a hotel bar with a view you would otherwise pay a tasting menu for.

Pařížská streetthe expensive avenueCut through the Jewish ghetto around 1900, named for Paris in 1926, now Czechia's priciest shopfront.

Pařížská did not grow; it was drawn. During the clearance of Josefov around 1900 the planners cut a new boulevard straight through the old Jewish quarter and lined it with eclectic and Art Nouveau facades. It was Mikulášská třída first, after St Nicholas church; in 1926 it took the name of Paris for its boulevard airs. By 2022–2023 it had become the most expensive retail address in the country, overtaking Na Příkopě. At its river end stands the brutalist InterContinental of 1968–1974, now the Fairmont Golden Prague.

Why go. Walk it from Old Town Square to the river and read the facades, not the shop windows.

Pražská šunka · Prague hambacteria of the genus chmelBrined, smoked and boiled on Celetná in 1857 — and good enough to send to Roosevelt.

In 1857 the butcher František Zvěřina, in a small shop on Celetná, brined a whole bone-in leg of pork, smoked it and then cooked it. Antonín Chmel took the method to a big Vinohrady factory and sold it round the world; the journalist Egon Erwin Kisch wrote that nobody without 'bacteria of the genus Chmel' could make a good one. By the 1920s and 30s it was famous enough to be a gift for Franklin Roosevelt. Today it is an EU Traditional Speciality Guaranteed: a method, not a place.

Why go. Buy it sliced by weight at a butcher. The leg turning on a spit in Old Town Square is sold per 100 g, and the till surprises people.

Olomoucké tvarůžkyone maker leftA golden curd cheese that smells like a stable, and one company left that makes it.

Tvarůžky are small rolls of ripened quark with a golden skin, a sharp taste and about half a per cent of fat in the dry matter. They are mentioned in the 15th century; in the Moravian town of Loštice they have been made since 1876, and the A.W. company founded by the Wessels family is now the last producer. It opened a museum there in 2014, and the name has had EU protection since 2010. In a pub they come pickled, fried in breadcrumbs, or on bread with raw onion beside a beer.

Why go. Buy one sealed pack for the crew's education, and keep it away from everything else in the fridge.

Hermelínthe ermine cheeseIt couldn't be called camembert, so in 1946 it was named after a white fur.

One of the first Czech soft cheeses with a white bloom was the Přibyslav camembert, made at Hesov. Camembert was a protected name, so Josef Hojdar, later head of the Madeta dairy, looked at the white rind and called it hermelín — ermine. The trademark was registered on 9 February 1946. Its pub form is the one to know: nakládaný hermelín, halved, stuffed with garlic and paprika and pickled in oil with onion and chilli. Every pub makes its own.

Why go. Order the pickled one with your second beer; buy the plain one for the galley board.

Oplatky, perník, trubičkythree protected sweetsA spa wafer, a gingerbread and a rolled wafer, each tied by EU law to one town.

Karlovarské oplatky are thin round wafers first recorded in a Karlovy Vary guide of 1788, made with the spa's water; since 2011 only a wafer made with Karlovy Vary thermal water may carry the name. Pardubický perník is gingerbread documented from the 16th century, with production rights upheld by Maria Theresa in 1759, and made and packed only in Pardubice. Hořické trubičky are rolled wafer tubes from Hořice in north-east Bohemia. All three are EU-protected, which means each one tastes of one particular town.

Why go. A tin of oplatky travels flat and survives the humidity if it stays sealed.

6 PASSAGES

Behind the facades of Wenceslas Square, Na Příkopě and Národní runs a second city of roofed arcades, courtyards and cinemas, built mostly between 1900 and the 1930s and once called bazaars. Nobody agrees how many there are: Aktuálně.cz picked the 30 most interesting, Czech Wikipedia's list, which calls itself incomplete, passes 70, more than 50 of them in Praha 1. Prague has a habit of throwing people out of windows, inventing words the world keeps, and building monuments it then blows up. These are the facts that hold up.

Things Prague did first, or strangely

The defenestrationsout of the windowPrague threw its governors out of windows three times — and in 1618 all three men survived.

In 1419 Hussites led by the priest Jan Želivský threw the judge, the burgomaster and councillors from the New Town Hall window; the Hussite Wars followed. In 1483 it happened again. In 1618 Protestant nobles threw the imperial governors Martinic and Slavata and their secretary about 21 metres from a castle window, and all three lived. Catholics credited the Virgin; Protestants later said they landed on a dung heap, a version no contemporary mentions. The Thirty Years' War followed. In 1948 Jan Masaryk was found below a ministry window, and that case is still argued.

Why go. The 1618 window is in Prague Castle; the 1419 one is on Charles Square.

Robot is a Czech wordjosef named itKarel Čapek wanted to call them labori. His brother, the painter, had a better idea.

The word robot reached the world in Karel Čapek's play R.U.R., Rossum's Universal Robots, in 1920. On Christmas afternoon 1961, at his kitchen table with his wife Linda helping, Wichterle built a machine from his son's Merkur construction kit, a bicycle dynamo and a doorbell transformer, and spun the first four soft contact lenses. He put them in his own eyes: the prescription was wrong, but they were comfortable. In four months of 1962 the couple made 5,500 lenses; American rights were sold in 1965.

Why go. The next time you see a soft lens, think of a Prague kitchen.

The Golem, a 19th-century inventionnothing in the atticThe clay giant in the synagogue attic is a story written two centuries after the rabbi died.

The legend: Rabbi Judah Loew shaped a man from Vltava clay to protect the ghetto, woke him with a holy name, switched him off before every Sabbath, and laid the body in the attic of the Old-New Synagogue. The history: no contemporary source mentions any of it. Historians treat the Prague version as a German literary creation of the early 1800s, and Yudel Rosenberg's 1909 'eyewitness' account as a hoax. Searches of the attic in 1883 and 2014 found nothing. Rabbi Loew himself was real, and died in 1609.

Why go. His statue stands on the corner of the New Town Hall.

The rafters' river

Before the locks and the dams, the Vltava was a timber road, and the men on the logs were the city's sailors.

Voroplavba: the timber raftersunesco, 2022140 metres of lashed logs, no engine, no keel — and the last one tied up in Prague in 1961.

Rafting on the Vltava is recorded from the 11th century. A raft was a chain of tied log sections: the front one, the předák, carried the steersman; the middle carried the kitchen; the back carried the brakes and steering gear. The largest Vltava raft on record had eight sections and 142 logs and ran 140 metres. From Vyšší Brod to Prague took about 45 hours on good water. The last commercial raft arrived on 12 September 1961, and on 1 December 2022 UNESCO inscribed timber rafting as world heritage, a joint nomination of six countries.

★ The old hand's point: a 140-metre raft is steered from the bow by reading the current a bend ahead and using the river's own speed against the brakes astern. Pilotage in its purest form — which is why the rafters were also the city's ice-cutters and flood men.

Before refrigerators, the Vltava was cut into blocks every winter. Once the ice was three or four centimetres thick, men flooded it to build it up to ten, then cut. When the river came into town

The Vltava keeps its own logbook, carved in stone and written in bridge arches, and every entry was paid for.

Bradáč, the bearded gaugethe stone logbookWhen the river reached his mouth, Prague moved upstairs.

Under the Old Town end of Charles Bridge a bearded stone head, Bradáč, sticks out of the embankment wall. It was carved on the last arch of the older Judith Bridge; nobody knows whom it shows or how old it really is. Prague read it as a flood gauge: water touching the beard meant the river was leaving its banks; at the mouth, the embankment streets were flooding; with only his bald crown showing, you could row across Old Town Square. The chronicles say the flood of 1432 reached his nose and 1481 nearly covered his head.

★ The old hand's point: a gauge everyone can read beats an accurate one nobody looks at. Each threshold on that face meant an action — the way a good safety sign on a boat works.

The bridge and the martyr

Every boatman on the Vltava has a patron, and he went into the water from the bridge in the night.

St John of Nepomuk, patron of bridges and boatmentwo versions of a drowningThe legend says he kept a queen's confession; the documents say he crossed a king over an abbey.

On the night of 20 March 1393 King Wenceslas IV had John of Pomuk, the archbishop's vicar general, thrown from the bridge into the Vltava. The documented cause was a power struggle over the rich Kladruby abbey: John confirmed an abbot the king did not want. The popular version — that he died refusing to reveal Queen Žofie's confession — came later, and the Charles Bridge Museum judges it unhistorical. The chronicler Václav Hájek then gave the wrong name and the wrong year, and the errors fed into his canonisation in 1729.

★ The old hand's point: he became the patron of bridges, millers and boatmen because he drowned. Crews choose their protectors after the danger they fear most, and on the Vltava that was the water itself.

The landlocked navy

A country with no coast registered ocean ships in Prague, leased a harbour in Hamburg, and lost its fleet in a few years.

Československá námořní plavba: Praha on the sternhome port prahaOcean ships that sailed the world with a landlocked capital painted on the stern.

Czechoslovak Ocean Shipping was founded on 1 April 1959 and took over the six sea-going ships already under the flag — Republika, Julius Fučík, Lidice, Mír, Dukla and Ostrava — all registered in the home port of Praha. Between 1952 and 1998, 44 merchant ships flew the Czechoslovak and then Czech flag. After voucher privatisation, a company linked to Viktor Kožený took control in 1995 and sold the fleet cheaply to Dutch and Norwegian owners. The last ship, Jan Želivský, went in December 1998. The company survives as a property holder with no ships.

★ The old hand's point: a flag is not a coastline; it is a crew, a register and a training system. Czechoslovakia trained seafarers from nothing, and that competence left with the ships.

Pionýr, the only one lostreef, bahamas, 1969Fourteen hours out of Cuba, with cigars and rum aboard, she found a reef in the dark.

On 6 August 1969 the Czechoslovak cargo ship Pionýr struck a coral reef off Mayaguana in the Bahamas, fourteen hours after leaving Cuba with ore, cigars and rum for Szczecin. ★ The old hand's point: groundings on charted reefs come from a chain of small things — a heavy ship, a night passage, a set nobody allowed for. The discipline is to break the chain before the bang.

The crews, from both sides of the Cold War, formed the Great Bitter Lake Association in October 1967 and printed hand-made stamps that the Egyptian post recognised. The canal reopened in 1975; only two of the fifteen ships, both German, left under their own power.

★ The old hand's point: when a ship cannot move, the crew becomes the ship. Seamanship includes knowing how to wait.

Engineered water

Ponds dug by a Renaissance steward, dams that stopped the ice, and a port that outlived its ships.

Jakub Krčín and the Christmas carppond engineeringThe carp in every Czech bathtub at Christmas swims out of a pond dug in 1590.

Jakub Krčín, steward of the Rožmberk estates from 1569 to 1589, built much of the pond system around Třeboň. The largest, Rožmberk, was dug in 1584–1590: a dam 2,355 metres long and 11 high holding about 647 hectares of water, with a new channel cut to throw the Lužnice's floods into the Nežárka. Folklore put him in league with the devil. Carp became the ordinary Christmas Eve fish only in the late 19th century; today around 3,000 street stalls sell them live before Christmas, and the bathtub custom is argued over on welfare grounds.

★ The old hand's point: a fish pond is a hull dug into the land, with a sluice for a scuttle. Krčín's system has run for 430 years because water management, not fish, was the design problem.

The four pourshladinka to mlíkoThe same beer, poured four ways, is four different beers.

4 WARNINGS

Prague is safe, cheap by western standards and generous to the curious. It also runs a well-oiled machine for separating visitors from their money within four hundred metres of the Orloj. These are the ones worth knowing.

The warnings

Each one sourced and priced; none of them is a reason not to come.

Eating on Old Town Squarethe warningThe same svíčková, 224 crowns dearer, ten minutes from the cheaper one.

Staroměstská restaurace on the square lists svíčková at 549 CZK and goulash soup in a bread bowl at 399 CZK (own site, Sept 2026). Ten minutes away, Lokál Dlouhááá serves the svíčková at 325. A Czech survey of May 2025 put tourist-strip goulash near 500 CZK and beer at 150, while an ordinary Prague lunch averaged about 199.

Instead. Look at the square, then walk two streets to eat.

Trdelníkthe warningSold as 'traditional Czech' — it reached Prague in the early 2000s, from Hungary by way of Slovakia.

Trdelník is a spit cake: dough wound round a rod, baked over heat, rolled in sugar and walnuts, a cousin of the Hungarian kürtőskalács. The Slovak town of Skalica credits a Transylvanian-born general with bringing the recipe in the 1780s, and Skalický trdelník has had EU protection since 2007. In Prague it appeared in the early 2000s, and the Old Town stalls turned it into an ice-cream cone sold as a traditional Czech cake.

Instead. Eat a větrník or a koláč at a cukrárna instead.

Ham off the spit, by the 100 gthe warningThe 89 on the board was per hundred grams; the carver cut a kilo.

The ham stand behind the Old Town Hall showed 89 CZK, which visitors read as a portion; it was per 100 grams, and the carver cut half a kilo or a kilo. Seznam Zprávy and the Honest Guide filmed a ham-and-sausage lunch billed at 1,040 CZK, and Prague 1 voted in October 2023 to end the stand's lease. At U Zlatého tygra 100 grams of Prague ham is 90 CZK.

Instead. Wherever meat is sold by weight, say the weight first.

ATMs that offer to convertthe warningAlways choose crowns, 'without conversion'.

Street ATMs run by independent operators, and many card terminals, offer to charge you in your home currency at their own rate — dynamic currency conversion, a margin hidden in the exchange. Decline it every time: choose CZK, or 'without conversion'.

Instead. Use bank ATMs where you can.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The same canon, told properly: what each thing is, the history that put it on a Bohemian table, and the detail that separates the real version from the one made for visitors.

#1
DISH

Svíčková na smetaně SVEECH-ko-vah na SMEH-tah-nyeh

Named for the tenderloin, svíčková, from svíčka, candle, for the cut's shape. Rettigová's 1826 Domácí kuchařka is the first well-known printed recipe, and Radio Prague calls it a national treasure two centuries on. The beef is larded and braised on carrot, parsley root, celeriac and onion with allspice, bay and pepper; the vegetables are puréed with cream into the sauce. Plate it the Czech way: bread dumplings to mop, cranberries for acid, whipped cream for no reason anyone can defend and everyone expects. It is the Sunday and wedding dish, and the sauce, not the meat, is the thing families argue about.
WhereLokál Dlouhááá, Dlouhá 33 (325 CZK, 27 Sept 2026) · Maps →
#2
DISH

Vepřo knedlo zelo VEP-rzho KNED-lo ZEH-lo

Pork, dumpling, cabbage, with the endings clipped, which is how it is ordered. Roast pork with caraway and its juices, sauerkraut, bread dumplings: the three parts sit side by side on the plate. Knedlík comes from German Knödel, and the dish shares its bones with Bavaria and Austria, which makes a clean origin story impossible; what is Czech is the insistence on it as the Sunday lunch. Look for the caraway on the crackling and the cabbage cooked sweet-sour rather than sharp.
WhereU Pinkasů, Jungmannovo náměstí 16 (classic pivnice kitchen; check the day's menu) · Maps →
#3
DISH

Smažený sýr SMAH-zheh-nee seer

A slab of cheese, usually eidam, breaded and fried, with tartar sauce and potatoes. Expats.cz traces its Prague rise to the Grand Hotel Šroubek (now the Evropa on Wenceslas Square), where Masaryk and Vlasta Burian ate it as a treat; the communist-era canteens of the 1960s turned it into the default meatless plate of a nation. It is still the vegetarian option on most pub menus, which says a lot about Czech pubs. Lokál makes it with farm eidam from Struhy.
WhereLokál Dlouhááá, Dlouhá 33 (315 CZK, 27 Sept 2026) · Maps →
#4
DISH

Tatarák s topinkami TAH-tah-rahk s TOH-pin-kah-mee

Raw minced beef, not Czech in origin, eaten here with an enthusiasm nobody fully explains. The Czech signature is the topinka: rye-wheat bread fried in fat, then rubbed hard with a raw garlic clove until the clove disappears. At U Zlatého tygra you get 150 g of tartare with six topinky, and the menu notes the mixing is done only if you ask. At Lokál it arrives freshly mixed. It is a beer snack, not a starter.
WhereU Zlatého tygra, Husova 17 (150 g with 6 topinky, 240 CZK, menu seen Sept 2026) · Maps →
#5
DISH

Obložený chlebíček OB-lo-zheh-nee KHLEH-bee-chek

The open sandwich of two or three bites, credited to Jan Paukert's delicatessen on Národní, opened September 1916, and to a painter friend who found canapés too small. A slice of veka, a bed of potato salad, then ham, egg, salami, cheese, a gherkin, sometimes a curl of mayonnaise. It is what Czechs put out at every office party, name day and funeral. Paukert's shop closed in 2015; Lahůdky Zlatý kříž, open since the First Republic, reopened in 2025 with chlebíčky as its flagship.
WhereLahůdky Zlatý kříž, Jungmannova 34 · Maps →
#6
DISH

Pražská šunka PRAHZH-skah SHOON-kah

Brined, cooked, mildly beech-smoked pork leg: an EU Traditional Speciality Guaranteed since 2018. The inventor is disputed (František Zvěřina, 1857 or 1870, depending on the source); the man who made it famous, Antonín Chmel, is not. Kisch joked that without Chmel's bacteria no one could make it. The honest way to eat it is by weight, at a pub, with butter and a gherkin, not carved off a spit on Old Town Square.
WhereU Zlatého tygra, Husova 17 (100 g, 90 CZK, menu seen Sept 2026) · Maps →
#7
DISH

Utopenec OO-toh-peh-nets

The drowned man: a fat špekáček sausage sliced, stuffed with onion and pickled in sweet-sour vinegar with onion and chilli, eaten cold with bread and beer. Radio Prague repeats the pub legend that the inventor was a miller and innkeeper called Šamánek from the Beroun district, who wanted sausages that kept without a fridge, and who himself drowned mending his mill wheel. The duller explanation is that the sausages drown in the jar. Either way it has been on Czech pub counters for more than a century.
WhereLokál Dlouhááá, Dlouhá 33 (their own špekáček, 129 CZK, Sept 2026) · Maps →
#8
DISH

Nakládaný hermelín NAH-klah-dah-nee HER-meh-leen

Hermelín is the Czech camembert, named in 1946 for the ermine of a royal cloak because the name camembert was protected. Nakládaný means pickled: the cheese is split, stuffed with paprika, garlic and pepper, packed in oil with onion and chilli and left to ripen for days until the centre runs. It is the definitive beer cheese of the Bohemian pub, eaten with dark bread and the oil mopped up.
WhereLokál Dlouhááá, Dlouhá 33 · Maps →
#9
DISH

Pivní sýr PIV-nee seer

Beer cheese: a pungent soft ripened cheese served with butter, mustard and raw onion, which you mash together on your plate, loosen with a splash of your beer, and spread on bread. U Zlatého tygra claims on its own site that it was the first pub the beer-cheese maker supplied regularly, and that 'the era of beer cheese began here'. It is also the pub where Bohumil Hrabal drank for decades and where he took Bill Clinton and Václav Havel. The same house rule since then: no spirits, only the 12-degree Pilsner.
WhereU Zlatého tygra, Husova 17 (90 g with butter, mustard, onion, 110 CZK, menu seen Sept 2026) · Maps →
#10
DISH

Hladinka, šnyt, mlíko HLAH-din-kah, shnit, MLEE-ko

Three pours from the same tap. Hladinka is the standard: roughly three quarters beer, a thick cap of wet foam that protects it. Šnyt is a short pour, more foam than beer, for when you are leaving. Mlíko, milk, is a glass of foam, drunk in one go. All depend on the side-pull LUKR tap; the codified 'three pours' are to a large extent a Pilsner Urquell and Ambiente promotion of the last two decades, but the foam is real and so is the flavour it carries. Tank beer (tankové pivo), unpasteurised and delivered to tanks in the cellar, is what makes it worth it.
WhereLokál Dlouhááá, Dlouhá 33 (12 tanks) · Maps →
#11
DISH

Burčák BOOR-chahk

Partly fermented grape must, cloudy, sweet and fizzing, somewhere between juice and wine and stronger than it tastes. Czech law lets it be sold only from 1 August to 30 November, only from grapes grown and processed in Czechia that same year, and the seller must post origin and producer. It keeps fermenting in the bottle, so caps are never tight: carry it upright. The peak is late September to mid-October, the heart of the Moravian harvest.
WhereVinograf, Senovážné náměstí 23 (Czech wine bar; ask for burčák) · Maps →
#12
DISH

Macharův dort MAH-khah-roov dort

A chocolate cake named in 1919 for the poet Josef Svatopluk Machar, a regular at František Myšák's pastry shop on Vodičkova. Myšák opened on Vodičkova in 1904 and moved to no. 31 in 1911; the building at no. 31 got its rondo-cubist facade of red brick in the 1920s. Czech Radio's Prague station calls the cake a legend of more than a hundred years, and it is still made there.
WhereMyšák, Vodičkova 710/31 (daily 8:00/9:00 to 19:00) · Maps →
