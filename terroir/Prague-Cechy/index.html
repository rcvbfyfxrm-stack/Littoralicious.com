The names A threshold, a burned forest, or a sun-baked rock: Prague has never agreed on what its own name means.
Cosmas's chronicle of 1119–1125 tells the legend: the prophetess Libuše sent men into the forest to build where they found a man making a threshold, práh, and so the city was named Praha. Linguists are less poetic and do not agree. Josef Dobrovský read práh as a ford or rapid, the river crossing where the town began; since the 19th century most have preferred the old verb pražiti, to burn or roast, for a forest burned to clear a settlement, or, in Antonín Profous's version, a hot dry place on the shale where the castle stands. The river is older than all of it: Vltava and German Moldau are both traced to Germanic *wilt ahwa, 'wild water', written Fuldaha in 872 and Wlitaua in Cosmas. Hradčany is simply the castle town, from hrad; Vyšehrad is the high castle; Malá Strana, the Lesser Side, is the smaller town across the bridge. Žižkov, named in 1877, honours Jan Žižka, whose Hussites beat Emperor Sigismund's army on Vítkov Hill on 14 July 1420; it has more than 300 pubs. Karlín, laid out from 1817, is named for Caroline Augusta of Bavaria, fourth wife of Emperor Francis I. Vinohrady was Královské Vinohrady, the Royal Vineyards, from 1867 to 1960, after vines going back to Charles IV's 14th century.

The soul of Prague — the essay
The tank pub at a quarter past six, the kavárna morning, and the river nobody looks at: the three rooms the whole city follows from
THE ESSAY

Every stone of the old city came in on the Vltava, the last commercial timber raft tied up in Prague on 12 September 1961, and a country with no coast registered ocean-going ships in Prague until the 1990s. Then the embankments were raised, the trams took over, and the visitors started looking up — at the castle, the clock, the spires — instead of down at the water and into the glass. Look down and in. Prague is the place where a lager is poured with three different heads, where a Cubist café that closed within a decade was rebuilt from archive photographs in 2004 and opens at nine, and where the best svíčková in the centre costs two hundred crowns less than the one on the square. Everything in this guide is within twenty minutes of the Charles Bridge, and almost none of it is on it.

There is an hour, most weekday evenings, when the whole logic of this city shows itself in one room. It is a quarter past six in a pub with a Pilsner tank in the cellar. The waiter walks the floor with a tray of full glasses and does not ask anybody anything: he reads the tables, sets down a half-litre wherever a glass has gone empty without a beer mat on top, and makes a pencil mark on the paper slip by your elbow. Nobody orders. Nobody is in a hurry. The foam is wet and dense and sits two fingers deep, and the man at your table, whom you have never met, raises his glass and looks you in the eye before he drinks.

That ritual is the character of Prague and it is worth understanding rather than photographing. Czechs have drunk more beer per head than anyone on earth for more than three decades — about 121 litres each in 2025 — and they have kept the grammar that goes with it: the hladinka with its clean collar of foam, the šnyt that is mostly head and a small glass of beer under it, the mlíko that is almost all foam and drunk in one go. The world's first golden lager was brewed an hour down the railway in Plzeň in 1842, and a coachman carried it to Jakub Pinkas's pub in Prague the following spring. The pub is still pouring.

The second room is the kavárna. Prague learned the coffee house from Vienna and then made it a parliament: newspapers on wooden poles, marble tables, a dessert cabinet of věneček and větrník, and permission to stay all morning over one cup. Writers and dissidents argued in these rooms under three regimes; one of them, above a street of souvenir shops, is a Cubist café by Josef Gočár, lost in the 1920s and rebuilt from photographs in 2004. Go once in the morning, sit by the window, and do not look at your phone.

The third thing is the river, and it is the one visitors miss. The Vltava has four locks inside the city, public ferries that cost a tram ticket, and two paddle steamers older than the Second World War. Timber rafters brought the forests of southern Bohemia down it to Podskalí until the last commercial raft on 12 September 1961, and their craft was inscribed by UNESCO in 2022. Floods have come back through the old town every few generations — in August 2002 Karlín went under and eighteen metro stations flooded — and the city marks where. A sailor reading this will recognise the type: a place that lives by its water and has learned to pretend it doesn't.

Everything else follows from those three rooms. The food is heavy because it was cooked for people who worked outside in winter; the sauces are the point, the dumplings are for the sauce, and the beer is for the dumplings. The good tables are small and mostly new: MICHELIN only published its first Czech guide in December 2025, and the five Prague rooms it starred cook Bohemian produce with a seriousness the old town never needed. And the city is at its best in exactly the season the tourists leave — October, when the burčák is cloudy, the gardens under the castle turn, and the tank pubs fill with people who live here.

From Rettigová to the red guide
A cookbook written in Czech to save a language, a ham the Habsburgs ate, the stars that came in December 2025 — and what came between
FOOD HISTORY

Every plate on this page is downstream of three things: a nineteenth-century cookbook written as an act of national revival, an empire that taught Prague to bake, and forty years when the recipe was a state standard.

In 1826 the Hradec Králové printer Jan Hostivít Pospíšil published Domácí kuchařka by Magdalena Dobromila Rettigová, a treatise on meat and fasting dishes 'for Czech and Moravian daughters'. It was a political book dressed as a practical one. Written in Czech when an educated Bohemian household ran in German, it carried the language into middle-class kitchens during the National Revival. Four Czech editions appeared in her lifetime, 1826, 1831, 1837 and 1844, and, the telling detail, four German ones too.

The Habsburg table set the grammar. Karolína Vávrová's nineteenth-century Pražská kuchařka follows the French order of multi-course meals common across the monarchy; schnitzel, dumpling and strudel belong to a shared imperial larder more than to any one nation. Prague's own contribution was a ham. In 1857 František Zvěřina produced the first Pražská šunka, a brined, lightly smoked, then boiled leg of pork; from 1879 Antonín Chmel made it industrially and by 1929 employed 250 people. Nobody registered the name, so the empire copied it, then France and America. Interwar Czechoslovakia prosecuted fakes at home; recent attempts to protect the name internationally met objections from several countries. It still has no EU protection.

The First Republic ate in rooms like Café Imperial, whose ceramic hall on Na Poříčí opened in 1914 and still serves breakfast at seven. After 1948 the state took the restaurants; for forty years a pub, a canteen and a hotel dining room belonged to the same owner and cooked from the same repertoire.

The rebuild began when private kitchens became legal again. V Zátiší counts 35 years this year. La Degustation opened in 2006 to cook Czech food from old sources, buying nearly everything within 250 km; its sister counter Marie B displays an 1894 cookbook by the cook it is named after. On 11 December 2025, in Mariánské Lázně, MICHELIN published its first guide to Czechia: one two-star, eight one-stars, five of them in Prague. Two hundred years after Rettigová, the Czech kitchen is again writing itself down.

13 QUARTERS

Prague was four separate towns until 1784: Old Town, New Town, Malá Strana and Hradčany, each with its own council and its own grudges. Then, in 1922, Greater Prague absorbed the industrial and residential towns around it in one stroke: Vinohrady, Žižkov, Karlín, Vršovice and Smíchov were all independent until that year. Read the city as a set of stitched-together towns and the street plan starts to make sense.

Inside the old walls

The right bank: the medieval market town, the ghetto that was cleared, and Charles IV's planned city three times its size.

Staré Město (Old Town)the market townA town that grew out of one big riverside market, and still funnels everyone to it.

Old Town laid itself out around a spacious marketplace on the Vltava bank from roughly the 9th century and took town privileges under King Wenceslaus I. The councillors bought a patrician house from the Volflin family in 1338 and made it the Old Town Hall; the tower followed in 1364. In 1869 some 46,000 people lived here. The 2021 census counted 6,272.

That emptying is the thing to understand: the lanes between Old Town Square and the river are now mostly hotels and shops, and the square is priced for the view. Go early for the square, then walk east toward the Municipal House and Náměstí Republiky, or south to Národní, where Prague still does ordinary business.

Josefovwhat survivedPrague's smallest cadastral district: 0.09 square kilometres and 977 residents.

Jews lived in Prague from the 10th century. The quarter was renamed Josefstadt in 1850 for Emperor Joseph II, whose 1782 Edict of Tolerance began emancipation. Between 1893 and 1913 the city demolished most of it in a slum-clearance programme and laid out Pařížská, the Art Nouveau boulevard of luxury shops that runs through it now. What survived: six synagogues, the Old Jewish Cemetery and the Jewish Town Hall. At its medieval peak more than 18,000 people lived here; the 2021 census found 977.

The one thing: the Old-New Synagogue, built in 1270 and still in use. Note that the Jewish Museum sites close on Saturdays and Jewish holidays.

Nové Město (New Town)the planned cityFounded in 1348 as a separate royal city, three times the size of the old one.

Charles IV founded New Town in 1348 as an independent royal city with its own legal framework, 7.5 square kilometres, roughly three times Old Town, with unusually wide streets and markets assigned by trade. Its squares were markets: Wenceslas Square was the horse market, Charles Square the cattle market, at about 550 by 150 metres for a long time the largest square in Europe. The first defenestration, in 1419, happened at its town hall.

Today it is where Prague works and shops, and it hides the city's covered passages (see Pasáže). The one thing: walk Wenceslas Square from the bottom, then leave it sideways through the arcades.

The two rocks and the town below the castle

Left bank and high ground: the baroque town rebuilt after a fire, the castle hill, and the fortress legend says came first.

Malá Stranarebuilt after 1541A medieval German merchants' town that a single fire turned baroque.

King Ottokar II founded it in 1257 as the New Town beneath Prague Castle and populated it with German craftsmen and merchants. When Charles IV founded his own New Town in 1348 it became the Lesser Town; the name Malá Strana, the Little Side, stuck in the 17th century. A fire in 1541 destroyed much of it, and the rebuilding in Renaissance and then baroque is why it looks the way it does: palaces, now largely embassies and ministries, and the Senate in Wallenstein Palace.

22,140 people lived here in 1869; 4,584 in 2021. The one thing: the Čertovka millstream on Kampa, early, before the bridge crowds come down.

Hradčany and Nový Světthe castle townBehind the palaces, a lane of tiny houses where Tycho Brahe once lived.

Hradčany grew up around the castle from about 1320 and stayed a separate borough until 1784. It is mostly noble palaces, the Loreta and Strahov. But at its back edge, Nový Svět, the New World, was built over a filled-in valley of the Brusnice stream along the Deer Moat, folded into Hradčany under Charles IV in 1360, burned in 1420 and again in 1541, and rebuilt small. The astronomer Tycho Brahe lived here; so, according to Czech Wikipedia, does the surrealist filmmaker Jan Švankmajer.

2021 population: 1,774. The one thing: walk Nový Svět and Černínská at dusk, after the castle buses have gone.

Vyšehradthe legendary first seatLegend says Prague started on this rock; archaeology has not agreed.

A fort on the right bank about 3 km upriver from the castle, founded in the 10th century. Legend makes it Prague's original seat, the home of the prophetess Libuše; that remains unproven. It peaked as a royal residence under Vratislav II in the 11th century, and its oldest surviving building, the Romanesque Rotunda of St Martin, is from that century. The Habsburgs rebuilt it as a baroque fortress; it joined Prague in 1883.

Now it is a park with views up the river and the national cemetery. The one thing: the cemetery at Slavín, then the walls at sunset. Below the rock, on Rašínovo nábřeží and around it, stand Josef Chochol's cubist houses.

The towns Prague swallowed in 1922

Independent industrial and residential towns until Greater Prague took them in: where people actually live, eat and drink.

Vinohradythe royal vineyardsCharles IV's vineyards, still producing about 4,000 litres a year.

The name means vineyards: the slopes were planted from the 14th century, and from 1788 to 1867 the area was called Viničné Hory, the Vineyard Hills. It joined Prague in 1922 and was cut into three districts, Prague 2, 3 and 10, in 1960; locals say the Communists split the bourgeois suburb on purpose. The vines survive in Havlíčkovy sady (Grébovka), about 4,000 litres a year.

Tall apartment blocks, Náměstí Míru with Josef Mocker's neo-Gothic St Ludmila (1892), Plečnik's Church of the Most Sacred Heart (1932), Riegrovy sady for the view, and the city's gay scene. The one thing: a glass at the Grébovka vineyard pavilion.

Žižkovthe free republicNamed for a one-eyed Hussite general, it calls itself a republic and has the pubs to prove it.

Žižkov is named after Jan Žižka, whose Hussites won the Battle of Vítkov Hill in 1420. Franz Joseph made it a city in 1881, when it had about 21,000 people; it joined Prague in 1922. Working-class and bohemian, it was home to Jaroslav Hašek and the Nobel poet Jaroslav Seifert, and styles itself the Free Republic of Žižkov. It is often claimed to have Europe's highest density of pubs per head, more than 300; treat the ranking as local boast, the pubs as real.

The one thing: climb Vítkov to the Žižka statue, then come down into the pubs under the TV tower.

Karlínthe flood-built revivalA grid town named for an empress, drowned in 2002 and rebuilt as the city's office quarter.

Laid out from 1817 and named for Caroline Augusta of Bavaria, fourth wife of Emperor Francis I, Karlín was a cheap-land factory town on a grid. The Negrelli Viaduct, 1,111 metres, opened on 1 June 1850 and is counted the longest bridge on Czech territory. The 2002 hundred-year flood put the district under water; the regeneration that followed turned it into offices, restaurants and flats.

At its east end stands the Invalidovna, K. I. Dientzenhofer's 1731 to 1737 veterans' home modelled on Les Invalides, of which only a fraction was ever built; it awaits restoration and appeared in Amadeus and Nosferatu. The one thing: walk Křižíkova and Thámova at lunch.

Vršovicetwo football clubsFirst written down in 1088; the New York Times called it an 'it' neighbourhood in 2016.

Vršovice appears in a founding charter of 1088 and joined Prague in 1922. It is dense, residential and football-divided: Slavia Prague play at Eden, the country's largest football stadium, and Bohemians 1905 at the small Ďolíček ground. The Koh-i-Noor Waldes factory here made buttons and press-studs. Its population peaked near 59,000 in 1961 and sits around 36,800 now.

Krymská and the streets off it carry the café and bar life that made the New York Times notice in 2016. The one thing: a match at Ďolíček if Bohemians are at home.

Smíchovbrewery and railcarsThe Soviet name of its main square was replaced by an angel.

An industrial town on the left bank, Smíchov became a municipality in 1903 and joined Greater Prague in 1922. Baron Franz Ringhoffer's railway-carriage works, founded in 1852, became one of the empire's biggest factories; Staropramen still brews here. After 1989 the Soviet tank monument went from Štefánik Square, the junction lost its Soviet-era name and became Anděl, angel, and Jean Nouvel's glass building went up with an angel from Wings of Desire etched into it.

The one thing: David Černý's own gallery, Musoleum, opened here in April 2023.

Holešovicethe river bendA factory district in a Vltava meander, now where the National Gallery keeps its modern art.

Holešovice sits on a meander of the Vltava in Prague 7. Industry drove its population from about 3,100 in 1869 to 61,000 in 1950; it is around 37,700 now. The Guardian called it one of Europe's coolest neighbourhoods in 2020, which tells you what happened to the warehouses. The National Gallery's Trade Fair Palace (Veletržní palác) holds the modern collection, and the National Technical Museum is on the Letná edge.

The oddity: the neo-Gothic St Anthony of Padua on Strossmayerovo náměstí, modelled on Týn, holds a 1918 replica of the Liberty Bell. The one thing: the Trade Fair Palace, then Stromovka.

Letnáthe plain above the bendWhere 750,000 people stood in 1989, and where the Stalin monument stood before that.

The plateau above the river bend, between Holešovice and the Old Town view. Letná park holds the cast-iron Hanavský Pavilion (1891), one of Europe's oldest carousels (1892, reopened in July 2022 after 16 years of restoration), and the Metronome on the pedestal of the Stalin monument. On the Letná Plain behind it about 750,000 people protested during the Velvet Revolution in 1989, and more than 250,000 in June 2019.

Residential streets north of the park, AC Sparta's stadium, and every August the Letní Letná circus festival. The one thing: the beer garden at the edge of the park with the bridges below you.

Tvarůžky are small rolls of ripened quark, golden-skinned, pungent, with a sharp taste and about 0.5 per cent fat in dry matter. How it works

The ritual, the cabinet, one café you can no longer sit in, and what to do if you only have a single morning.

How to sit in a kavárnathe one-coffee tableWalk in, choose a table, wait. Nobody queues at a counter in a grand café.

Table service is the rule: a waiter brings the menu and a glass of water often follows the coffee. The order that marks you as a regular is vídeňská káva, Viennese coffee: the houses disagree on what it is (Slavia serves filter coffee under whipped cream, Imperial an espresso lungo in a glass with cream), which is itself the lesson. The convention of the grand café is that one coffee buys the table for the morning; newspapers are there to be read.

Pay at the table and ask for the bill; it will not come unasked. A tip is usually given by rounding up when you pay, not left on the table.

Pasáže — the covered passages
Prague's answer to the Paris passages: a roofed city inside the blocks of New Town.
12 PASSAGES

Behind the facades of Wenceslas Square, Na Příkopě and Národní runs a second city of roofed arcades, courtyards and cinemas, built mostly between 1900 and the 1930s and once called bazaars. Nobody agrees how many there are: Aktuálně.cz picked the 30 most interesting, Czech Wikipedia's list, which calls itself incomplete, passes 70, more than 50 of them in Praha 1. Some lock at night, some have been reopened (Hrzánská, 2024), some have simply disappeared into shop space. Almost all the good ones are in New Town, the old trading district, and you can cross it in the rain without getting wet.

The Wenceslas Square labyrinth

Enter from the square, come out on Vodičkova or in a Franciscan rose garden, and never see the sky in between.

Palác Lucernathe first, it saysBuilt by the president's grandfather, who called his arcade the Bazar.

Vácslav Havel, builder and grandfather of President Václav Havel, bought the old Bělský palace on Vodičkova in 1905, got his permit on 16 September 1907, and built Lucerna in phases to 1921 to a reinforced-concrete design by Stanislav Bechyně. His passage runs about 105 metres from Vodičkova to Štěpánská; he called it the Bazar, after the Paris arcades. Lucerna describes itself as the first passage building in Prague. Czech Wikipedia says the U Nováků arcade across the street, built 1901 to 1904, was only the second; the two claims do not fit together, and that is the argument locals have.

The family lost it in 1952, got it back in 1991, and it became a national cultural monument in 2017. The cinema has run since the building opened; the Great Hall was for decades the city's largest.

Prague has a habit of throwing people out of windows, inventing words the world keeps, and building monuments it then blows up. These are the facts that hold up.

Things Prague did first, or strangely

Stories to tell over a beer, each checked.

The defenestrationsout of the windowPrague threw its councillors from windows in 1419, 1483 and 1618.

In 1419 Hussites led by the priest Jan Želivský threw the judge, burgomaster and councillors from New Town Hall on Charles Square; the Hussite Wars followed. In 1483 it happened again to Old and New Town councillors. In 1618 Protestant nobles threw the imperial governors Martinic and Slavata and their secretary Fabricius about 21 metres from a castle window. All three survived. Catholics credited the Virgin; Protestants later said they landed on a dung heap, a version no contemporary mentions. The Thirty Years' War followed. In 1948 Jan Masaryk was found below a ministry window; the case is still disputed.

Robot is a Czech wordjosef named itKarel Čapek wanted to call them labori. His brother had a better idea.

The word robot entered the world in Karel Čapek's 1920 play R.U.R., Rossum's Universal Robots. By Karel's own account he first wanted to call his artificial workers labori, from Latin labor, disliked it, and asked his brother, the painter Josef Čapek, who said: call them roboti. It comes from robota, the forced labour a serf owed his lord, and by extension drudgery. Both brothers are Prague men; Karel is buried at Vyšehrad.

Otto Wichterle and Drahoslav Lím created the hydrogel pHEMA in 1953. On Christmas afternoon 1961, in his kitchen and with his wife Linda helping, Wichterle made the first four soft contact lenses on a machine built from a Merkur children's construction kit, his son's bicycle dynamo and a bell transformer. He tried them in his own eyes: wrong prescription, but comfortable. In four months of 1962 the couple made 5,500 lenses. American rights were sold in 1965 and sublicensed to Bausch and Lomb.

The legend: Rabbi Judah Loew, the Maharal, shaped a man from Vltava clay to protect the ghetto, woke him with a holy name and switched him off before each Sabbath; the body lies in the attic of the Old-New Synagogue. The history: no contemporary source mentions it. Historians treat the Prague version as a German literary creation of the early 1800s, and Yudel Rosenberg's 1909 eyewitness account as a hoax. Searches of the attic in 1883 and 2014 found nothing. Rabbi Loew himself is real and buried in the Old Jewish Cemetery (died 1609); on the New Town Hall corner stands his statue.

Prague has no sea. It has a working river with locks, ferries, steamers and a lot of old scores to settle.
11 TO DO

Every stone of the old town arrived by water, and every few generations the water came back for some of it. The Vltava still runs Prague's timetable: four locks inside the city, public ferries that cost a tram ticket, and two paddle steamers older than the Second World War. In October the summer fleet thins out, which is exactly when the working river shows through. If you have one afternoon, make it a Saturday and do the first card.

If you have one afternoon

A Saturday in October, a tram ticket in the pocket, and the river done the way the people of Podskalí did it.

The steamers and the boats you can take

Two paddle wheels older than the war, a lock that lifts you two and a half metres, and a rowing boat when the sun allows.

Parník Vltava (1940)the last one workingChristened to the sound of Smetana, launched in an occupied city.

The Vltava was meant to be built at the Ústí nad Labem yard. When that town was lost to Germany in 1938–39, the hull moved to the Praga works in Libeň, and she was launched there on 27 August 1940 while the Minister of Commerce blessed her to Smetana's Vltava. Apart from a wartime spell as Moldau, she has kept her name ever since, and was restored to her original look in 2007–2009.

53 metres long, a 150-horsepower steam engine you can watch through the engine-room opening, and a paddle wheel that handles the shallow reaches better than modern motor ships. She is a cultural monument since 2013. Her long run upriver to the Slapy dam (9.5 hours, 790 CZK one way / 990 CZK return in 2026) is a summer weekend service and is finished for the year by October; the in-town hourly runs continue at weekends.

Parník Vyšehrad (1938) and the steam navigation museumfive names, one hullA steamer that carried the politics of every regime on her bow.

Built at Ústí nad Labem in 1938 and christened on 1 May 1938 as Antonín Švehla, then Karlstein (1942), T. G. Masaryk (after 1945), Děvín (1952) and finally Vyšehrad (1992): the name plate reads like a short history of the country. 62 metres long, once licensed for 885 passengers, now 350. She no longer sails; she is permanently moored at Rašínovo nábřeží as a floating facility.

In 2025, for the 160th anniversary of the Prague Steam Navigation Company (Pražská paroplavební společnost, founded in 1865 by mayor František Dittrich), a museum opened on board with a restored steam engine and the captain's salon panelling. Between 1911 and 1925 the company carried over a million passengers a year as part of the city's commuting network.

The Smíchov lockthe busiest chamberTwo medieval weirs, one lift, two and a half metres.

Prague's river is a staircase. The Smíchov lock (river km 53.8) took its present form in 1911–1922 to a design by F. Sander, built by František Schön a synové. A boat going through it clears two old weirs at once, the Šítkovský and the Staroměstský, rising or falling nearly two and a half metres. It is the most-used lock on the Vltava: 17,844 vessels in 1998 alone, and it works year-round from 07:00 to 23:00.

The raft chutes in the middle of both weirs are still there and stay shut, except once a year for the Napříč Prahou – přes tři jezy canoe race. Upstream is Modřany (km 62.2), downstream Štvanice (km 50.7) and Troja–Podbaba (km 43.5).

Slovanský ostrov built itself out of 17th-century sediment; it was called Žofín after Archduchess Sophie and renamed for the Slavic Congress of 1848. Žluté lázně opened in Podolí on 16 June 1910 on a former raft landing, built for 9,000 bathers. Its best decades were the 1930s to 1960s; when the Slapy dam was completed upstream in the mid-1950s, the water came out of the reservoir colder and river bathing declined. Podskalská celnice na Výtonithe rafters' customs houseThe city took its toll by cutting a piece off each raft.

Around 1500 this late-Gothic house became the toll point for timber floating into Prague, and the toll was taken in kind: a section of the wood was cut out (vytínání), which is where the place-name Výtoň comes from. Around it stood Podskalí, the rafters' and ice-cutters' quarter, demolished in the early 1900s. The city bought the house in 1908 and kept it.

The 2002 flood destroyed the exhibition on the first floor; the museum reopened in 2003 and got a new permanent display in 2015: raft models, the Podskalí trades, the first steamers. The ground floor is the rafters' pub again.

The old river hands — true stories of this water
A landlocked city with a merchant navy, a sea coast in Shakespeare, and a river that keeps its own records.
17 TRUE STORIES

Every story here is sourced, with the arguments left in where the sources argue. Each one ends with what an old hand would want you to understand, and with a place where you can still put your hand on it. Prague has no sea, which is why its people have always taken the water seriously.

The rafters' river

Before the locks and the dams, the Vltava was a timber road, and the men on the logs were the city's sailors.

Voroplavba: the timber raftersunesco, 2022Nine centuries of logs, and the last one arrived in 1961.

Rafting on the Vltava is recorded from the 11th century. Rafts were chains of tied log sections: the front one, the předák, carried the steersman; the middle carried the kitchen; the back carried the braking and steering gear. The largest recorded Vltava raft had 8 sections, 142 logs and ran 140 metres. From Vyšší Brod to Prague took about 45 hours on good water. The last commercial raft ran on 12 September 1961. On 1 December 2022 UNESCO inscribed timber rafting as intangible heritage, a joint nomination of Czechia, Latvia, Germany, Poland, Austria and Spain.

★ The old hand's point: a 140-metre raft has no engine and no keel; it is steered from the bow by reading the current a bend ahead and using the river's own speed against the brakes astern. That is pilotage in its purest form, and it is why the rafters were also the city's ice-cutters and flood men: they knew the river's moods by the log.

Touch it today: the raft models in the Podskalská celnice at Výtoň, and the chutes in the middle of the Prague weirs, opened once a year for the three-weirs race. · Podskalská celnice, Sat–Sun 10:00–18:00, 80 CZK

The ice harvesters of Podskalíice as cargoEvery pub in Prague was cooled by the river it faced.

Before refrigerators, the Vltava was cut into blocks every winter. The season lasted two or three weeks: once the ice was 3–4 cm thick, men flooded it to build it to about 10 cm and then cut. In 1906 over 100,000 cartloads left Prague's banks in one winter, worked by about 550 men in crews of 10–12; many were rafters in summer. The cleanest ice came from between the Smíchov and Šítkov mills. Prague's publicans financed the Branické ledárny, built 1909–1911 to a design by Josef Kovařovič, which held 20,000 tonnes behind cork insulation.

★ The old hand's point: the trade was killed by an engineering decision upstream, not by the fridge alone. After the Slapy dam was built in the 1950s, water released from the deep reservoir kept the city stretch from freezing. Change the river above you and you change every job on it below.

Sova's mills, now Museum Kampamill-race to museumGrain, steam, fire, flood, and then art.

The mills at the tip of Kampa are first mentioned in 1393 and take their name from Václav Sova z Liboslavě, a city councillor granted the site in 1478. František Odkolek turned them into a steam mill by 1858. After a fire in 1896 the city refused to let the industrial mill restart in the centre. Meda Mládková turned the ruin into Museum Kampa; in August 2002, as the museum was getting going, the flood went through the building, and staff and volunteers reopened it soon after.

★ The old hand's point: a mill is where a river is made to work for rent, and every mill on the Vltava sat at a weir. The weirs outlived the mills and now set the water levels that the Prague locks manage. The engineering is the heritage; the buildings are just what stood on it.

When the river came into town

The Vltava keeps its own logbook, carved in stone and written in bridge arches, and every entry was paid for.

Bradáč, the bearded gaugethe stone logbookWhen the water reached his mouth, you moved upstairs.

Under the Old Town end of Charles Bridge a stone bearded head, Bradáč, sticks out of the embankment wall between the bridge tower and the Knights of the Cross monastery. It was carved on the last arch of the older Judith Bridge; nobody knows whom it represents or how old it really is. Prague used it as a flood gauge: water touching the beard meant the river was leaving its banks; at the mouth, the embankment streets were flooding; with only the bald crown showing, you could row across Old Town Square. The chronicles say 1432 reached his nose and 1481 nearly covered his head.

★ The old hand's point: a flood gauge that everyone can read is worth more than an accurate one nobody looks at. Bradáč worked for centuries because the warning was a face, visible from the bridge, and each threshold meant a specific action. Good safety signs on a boat work the same way.

1784: the ice took the guardhouse73 frozen daysAn angel fell off the bridge and was found 220 years later.

After the Laki eruption in Iceland in 1783, Prague's Klementinum observatory counted 73 consecutive ice days that winter. On 26–27 February 1784 the temperature jumped to 9 °C with heavy rain, the Vltava ice broke, and on 28 February ice floes undermined the sixth pier of Charles Bridge and damaged five more piers and three arches. A military guardhouse on the bridge went into the river with five soldiers. The flood reached 114 streets and 785 houses. The next day an angel from the St Wenceslas group fell in; divers found its torso under the bridge in 2004.

★ The old hand's point: ice is not water. A thaw flood carries floes that hit like rams, and a bridge built to pass water can dam ice and fail. Anyone who has seen a harbour after an ice break-up knows the danger is the load, not the level.

Touch it today: the recovered angel is in the National Gallery's lapidarium collection; on the bridge, find the sixth pier from the Old Town side and count. · Charles Bridge, free · lapidarium, check ngprague.cz

4 September 1890: the rafts broke the bridgelogs as battering ramsCharles Bridge was brought down by timber from the river's own trade.

Just before half past five in the morning of 4 September 1890, logs from broken-up rafts and other debris jammed against Charles Bridge and two arches collapsed; a third went at half past nine, with three piers undermined. The peak discharge in Prague was 3,975 cubic metres a second; the water covered nearly a third of the city, about 4,000 houses, up to Old Town Square. Two statues fell in, St Ignatius of Loyola and St Francis Xavier, and waited nine years to be lifted out. In Karlín 20 people were swept away. The repaired piers were afterwards tied firmly to the rock.

★ The old hand's point: in a flood, the debris is the weapon. A river carrying its own cargo, here the rafters' timber, turns every obstruction into a dam and every dam into a failure. Clear the drift, protect the piers, and know what is upstream of you.

Touch it today: walk the bridge from the Old Town and look at the arches near the middle; the Charles Bridge Museum at Křižovnické náměstí covers the collapse (entry included with the Prague Venice cruise). · Charles Bridge, free · museum at Křižovnické náměstí

August 2002: the metro under water5,160 cubic metres a secondAbout 20 percent more water than the great flood of 1845.

On 14 August 2002 the Vltava peaked at Velká Chuchle at 5,160 cubic metres a second and 782 cm, roughly a fifth more than the 1845 flood, the largest before it. Karlín went under; 18 metro stations were flooded, with damage around 7 billion CZK and months of repairs. Across the country 17 people died and 225,000 were evacuated. The Vltava dam cascade upstream caught some of it: Orlík received 3,900 cubic metres a second and the cascade trimmed the peak by about 800–900.

★ The old hand's point: storage buys time, not safety. Reservoirs cut the peak, but a full reservoir has nothing left to give. Plan for the case where every buffer is already used.

Touch it today: look for the 2002 high-water marks on buildings in Karlín and on Kampa; the Podskalská celnice and Museum Kampa both lost exhibitions to this water. · Karlín and Kampa, free · no single site
Gaston, the sea lion who swam to Germany300 km downstreamThe only sailor in this section who went nearly all the way to the sea.

Gaston, a 12-year-old South African fur seal (lachtan in Czech), was carried out of the flooded Prague zoo on 13 August 2002. He swam down the Vltava and the Elbe for more than 300 km and was caught by German rescuers at Lutherstadt Wittenberg on 19 August. He died of stress and exhaustion. The sources disagree on the moment: the Czech encyclopaedia says the same day, 19 August, in Wittenberg; Deník reported that he died on 20 August during transport back towards Prague. The zoo lost 134 animals in that flood.

★ The old hand's point: the river is a road to the sea whether you mean to take it or not. The Vltava joins the Elbe at Mělník and the Elbe goes to Hamburg: anything that goes in at Troja is, by water, headed for the North Sea.

The bridge and the martyr

Every boatman on the Vltava has a patron, and he went into the water from the bridge in the night.

St John of Nepomuk, patron of bridges and boatmentwo versions of a drowningThe legend says a confession; the documents say a monastery.

On the night of 20 March 1393 King Wenceslas IV had John of Pomuk, vicar general of the Prague archbishop, thrown from the bridge into the Vltava. The documented cause was a power struggle over the rich Kladruby abbey: John confirmed a new abbot the king did not want. The popular version, that he died refusing to reveal Queen Žofie's confession, came later and is judged unhistorical by the Charles Bridge Museum. Chronicler Václav Hájek then gave the wrong name and the wrong year, 1383, errors that fed into the canonisation papers of 1729.

★ The old hand's point: he became patron of bridges, fishermen, millers and boatmen because he drowned. Every river trade took him, because crews choose their protectors after the danger they fear most, and on the Vltava that danger was the water itself.

Touch it today: the bronze reliefs under his statue on Charles Bridge (cast in Nuremberg, 1683) are polished bright by hands. The spot of the fall is marked by a forged grille with his relief and a bronze double cross on the parapet, with five stars; touching it is said to bring luck. · Charles Bridge, free, any hour · the quietest time is before 07:00

The landlocked navy

A country with no coast registered ocean ships in Prague, leased a harbour in Hamburg, and lost its fleet in a few years.

Československá námořní plavba: Praha on the sternhome port prahaDeep-sea ships with an inland capital as home port.

Czechoslovak Ocean Shipping (Československá námořní plavba) was founded on 1 April 1959 and took over the six sea-going ships already under the Czechoslovak flag: Republika, Julius Fučík, Lidice, Mír, Dukla and Ostrava. The Julius Fučík sailed under the flag from 1953 to 1965. Their registered home port was Praha. Between 1952 and 1998, 44 merchant ships flew the Czechoslovak and then Czech flag. After voucher privatisation in 1992, Stratton Investments, linked to Viktor Kožený, took control in 1995 and sold the fleet cheaply to Dutch and Norwegian owners. The last new ship, Jan Želivský, was sold in December 1998. The company survives as a property holder with no ships.

★ The old hand's point: a flag is not a coastline, it is a crew, a register and a training system. Czechoslovakia trained officers and ratings who learned the sea from nothing; that competence went with the ships. The sources read here put the end of the fleet in 1998, not 2008.

Touch it today: nothing floats. The closest thing is the river-trade display in the Podskalská celnice; in Hamburg, the Czech port lots (below).
Pionýr, the only one lostreef, bahamas, 1969Fourteen hours out of Cuba, eight minutes before nine at night.

On 6 August 1969 the Czechoslovak cargo ship Pionýr struck a coral reef off Mayaguana in the Bahamas, 14 hours after leaving Cuba with ore, cigars and rum for Szczecin. All the crew survived; the last left the ship on 13 September after salvage failed. She is the only Czechoslovak merchant ship lost at sea. Poor manoeuvrability, bad weather and navigation error are all cited; the exact cause is still unexplained. Czech technical divers found the wreck in late 2015.

★ The old hand's point: groundings on charted reefs usually come from a chain of small things, a heavy ship, a night passage, a set nobody allowed for, rather than one mistake. The discipline is to break the chain before the bang.

Touch it today: nothing in Prague; the wreck lies off Mayaguana.
Lednice, eight years in the Bitter Lakethe yellow fleetA Czechoslovak ship trapped in the Suez Canal from 1967 to 1975.

When the Six-Day War closed the Suez Canal in June 1967, 15 ships were trapped in the Great Bitter Lake, among them the Czechoslovak Lednice, 1,462 gross tons, carrying raw cowhides from Ethiopia. The crews formed the Great Bitter Lake Association in October 1967 and printed hand-made stamps that the Egyptian post recognised. The canal reopened in 1975; only two ships, both German, left under their own power.

★ The old hand's point: when a ship cannot move, the crew becomes the ship. The Bitter Lake crews kept their vessels and themselves in order across the Cold War line through routine and shared rituals. Seamanship includes knowing how to wait.

Touch it today: nothing in Prague; the Great Bitter Lake stamps turn up with collectors.
The Czech harbour in Hamburga 99-year leaseVersailles gave a landlocked country a piece of a North Sea port.

The Treaty of Versailles (1919) required Germany to lease Czechoslovakia areas in the ports of Hamburg and Stettin for 99 years. The Hamburg contract was signed in Prague on 16 February 1929: Moldauhafen and Saalehafen, about 28,500 square metres together, plus Peutehafen, bought outright. Czechia inherited the rights in 1993. The Czech operator went bankrupt in 2001 and the site decayed; Hamburg offered a swap in 2015 tied to its Olympic bid, which Hamburg voters then rejected. The lease runs out in 2028.

★ The old hand's point: access to the sea is a legal fact as much as a geographic one. A river is only a route if you hold the right to land at the far end. The Vltava–Elbe route from Prague to Hamburg still exists; what a landlocked state needs is a quay at the end of it.

Touch it today: in Prague, stand at Holešovice port (last group). In Hamburg, Moldauhafen lies in the Grasbrook redevelopment, where a U4 metro station is planned.
The coast of Bohemiashakespeare's joke?A ship in The Winter's Tale lands on the shore of a country without one.

In The Winter's Tale a ship lands on the coast of Bohemia, which has none, and Ben Jonson mocked it. Shakespeare took the plot from Robert Greene's Pandosto (1588), which also gave Bohemia a coast, and swapped the kingdoms round. The explanations since: Ottokar II's 13th-century lands briefly reached the Adriatic; Edmund von Lippmann (1891) argued "Bohemia" was a rare name for Apulia; Thomas Hanmer (1744) thought it a misprint for Bithynia; Andrew Gurr suggests Shakespeare did it on purpose, and Jonson took the bait.

★ The old hand's point: every sailor has met a chart with a coast in the wrong place. The fix is the same as with Shakespeare: trust the soundings, not the paper, and remember that borders move while coasts do not.

Touch it today: nothing to touch; the joke is the landscape itself.

Engineered water

Ponds dug by a Renaissance steward, dams that stopped the ice, and a port that outlived its ships.

Jakub Krčín and the Christmas carppond engineeringThe carp in the Czech bathtub was made possible in 1590.

Jakub Krčín (1535–1604), steward of the Rožmberk estates from 1569 to 1589, built much of the pond system around Třeboň. Rožmberk, the largest, was dug in 1584–1590: a dam 2,355 m long and 11 m high, about 647 hectares, with the Nová řeka channel cut to send the Lužnice's floods into the Nežárka. Folklore had him in league with the devil; his prepared tomb at Obděnice stayed empty. Carp only became the ordinary Christmas Eve fish in the second half of the 19th century. The Czech Republic produced 17,945 tonnes of market carp in 2019, more than half exported; around 3,000 street stalls sell live carp before Christmas, and the bathtub custom is now argued over on welfare grounds.

★ The old hand's point: a fish pond is a hull dug into the land, with a sluice for a scuttle. Krčín's system has run for 430 years because the water management, not the fish, was the design problem.

The Vltava cascadenine damsThe reservoirs that ended the ice trade and saved part of Prague in 2002.

Between 1930 and 1992 nine dams were built on the Vltava, among them Vrané (1936), Štěchovice (1945), Slapy (1954), Lipno (1959), Kamýk (1962) and Orlík (1966). Orlík holds 720 million cubic metres; the cascade generates up to 750 MW. Built for power, it later became a flood and water store. Orlík's ship-lift was never finished, which is why barges cannot run the whole river.

★ The old hand's point: a dam changes everything below it: water temperature, ice, silt and the river's timetable. Prague lost its frozen winters, its ice trade and its warm bathing water to Slapy, and gained a buffer against floods. There is no free engineering on a river.

Pivo — how Prague drinks beer
The city's real sacrament, with its own grammar of foam, degrees and pencil marks.
HOW-TO + 8 PUBS

Czechs have drunk more beer per head than anyone on earth for 32 years running, and they still drink it with rules. The rules are not snobbery; they are how a fresh, unpasteurised lager is kept at its best for the twenty minutes it lives in the glass. Learn five words, where the tanks are, and the difference between a 74-crown half-litre and a 160-crown one, and the city opens.

Order like a regular

Five things to know before the first glass lands on the beer mat — foam, degrees, tanks, the paper slip, the price.

The four pourshladinka to mlíkoThe same beer, poured four ways, tastes like four different beers.

Hladinka is the default: a full half-litre with a wet, dense head to the rim, poured in one go; drink it and the foam should leave rings down the glass. Šnyt is a small beer under a tall head in the big glass — the regular's 'one more, but not really', and the one Karel Čapek praised for wetting the whistle without committing. Mlíko ('milk') is a glass of almost pure foam, sweet and soft, taken in a few swallows between beers. Čochtan is the opposite: no head at all, all the carbonation left in, sharp and bitter; drink it fast.

Not every pub will pour all four; mlíko and šnyt are the tapster's pride, so ask at a Pilsner tank pub rather than a tourist terrace. Say the word, not the description.

Any hospoda · ask by name: 'jedno mlíko, prosím'
Degrees are not alcohol°Plato, decodedA dvanáctka is not 12 percent. It is about 5.

The number on a Czech tap — desítka (10°), jedenáctka (11°), dvanáctka (12°) — is the degree Plato: the share of dissolved extract, mostly malt sugar, in the wort before fermentation. Twelve degrees means roughly 12 percent extract by weight; after fermentation a dvanáctka lands around 4.5–5 percent alcohol (Dva kohouti's house 12° is 4.9 percent). Czech labelling sorts them: tens and below are výčepní (draught) beers, elevens and twelves are ležák (lager), thirteen and up are speciální.

The trend is up the scale: in 2025, 11°–12° lagers were 59.2 percent of Czech consumption, the lighter draught beers 35.5 percent (Czech Beer and Malt Association). A desítka is the long-session beer; the twelve is the one with body.

Read the tap handle or the chalkboard · ask 'jaké máte pivo?'
Tankové pivothe tank pubsUnpasteurised Pilsner, trucked in and drunk within days.

Tank beer is lager delivered unpasteurised and usually unfiltered in large refrigerated tanks rather than kegs, so it keeps its live yeast and a softer, rounder mouthfeel. It has to be drunk fast — which is why only high-volume pubs get it. Lokál Dlouhááá pours from 12 tanks and serves about 1,300 guests a day; U Zlatého tygra and U Pinkasů are tank houses too.

Test it yourself: a tank Pilsner Urquell next to a bottle of the same beer is the cheapest lesson in Czech brewing you will ever get.

Look for 'tankové pivo' or 'tankovna' on the door
The slip, the mat, 'ještě jedno'the pencil marksNobody asks if you want another. The glass simply arrives.

Your tab lives on a paper slip left on the table: the waiter adds a pencil stroke for each beer and adds them up when you pay, often per person. In a traditional pub a fresh glass lands the moment yours is nearly empty; 'ještě jedno, prosím' (one more, please) is the polite version. To stop, put your beer mat on top of the empty glass — the one signal every waiter reads.

Tables are shared. Ask 'je tu volno?' (is this free?) before sitting at an occupied one; a card reading 'rezervace' or 'stůl štamgastů' means regulars. Toast with 'Na zdraví', look each person in the eye as glasses touch, and do not cross arms with the person next to you.

Pay at the table · 'zaplatím' (I'll pay) · split: 'zvlášť'

Where the lager came from

The three facts behind every glass — a Bavarian brewer in Plzeň, a record thirst, and a hundred-year lawsuit.

Plzeň, 1842the first golden lagerMost of the beer drunk on earth descends from one mash in a Bohemian town.

The burghers of Plzeň, tired of bad ale, built a new Burghers' Brewery (Měšťanský pivovar) and hired a Bavarian, Josef Groll. On 5 October 1842 he mashed a bottom-fermented beer from pale, indirectly kilned malt, soft Plzeň water and Saaz (Žatec) hops; it was first served on 11 November 1842 at the St Martin's market. That beer became Pilsner Urquell, and 'pils' became the template for pale lager worldwide.

It reached Prague fast: on 8 April 1843 a coachman brought two buckets to the tailor-turned-publican Jakub Pinkas, and U Pinkasů became the first Prague pub to tap it.

In 2025 Czechs drank 121 litres of beer per person — a historic low, down 3.2 percent in a year, eight fewer half-litres each — and still the highest per-capita consumption in the world, for the 32nd year in a row (Czech Beer and Malt Association, reported April 2026). Budvar v. Budweisersince 1907Two breweries, one town's name, more than a century in court.

Budějovický Budvar, the state-owned brewery in České Budějovice (Budweis in German), and the American Anheuser-Busch, now AB InBev, have fought over 'Budweiser' since a first agreement in 1907, across well over a hundred cases. The result is a map: Budvar owns the name in most of Europe, AB InBev in North America, where the Czech beer is sold as Czechvar; in much of Europe the American beer is just 'Bud'.

It is not over. In January 2025 an EU court ruling went against Budvar over the word 'Bud' and Budvar said it would appeal. In Prague, order Budvar as 'Budvar' — nobody will know what you mean by Czechvar.

BecherovkaKarlovy Vary, 1807A spa-town bitter whose recipe is known to two people.

Josef Vitus Becher, a Carlsbad (Karlovy Vary) merchant, worked out the recipe with the physician Christian Frobrig and launched it in 1807 as a stomach tonic: more than twenty herbs and spices, 38 percent, gingery and cinnamon-sweet. Clubs, live rooms & the history

Clubs, jazz cellars and the band whose trial helped write Charter 77.

Prague's night has two histories: the dissident underground of the 1970s, when a psychedelic rock band's trial became a human-rights movement, and the post-1989 club city that filled factories and cellars. Both are still playing. Programmes below were read from the venues' own sites on 27 September 2026.

The history

Why a rock band matters to Czech politics, and where it still echoes.

The Plastic People of the Universe1968 to Charter 77A band jailed for playing, and the document their trial provoked.

Founded in 1968, weeks after the Soviet invasion, by bassist Milan Hlavsa, with the art historian Ivan Martin Jirous as manager, the Plastic People played Zappa- and Velvet-inflected rock the regime refused to license. In 1976 members were tried for 'organised disturbance of the peace' and jailed for 8 to 18 months. Václav Havel and others answered with Charter 77, published in January 1977 — the founding text of the dissent that led to 1989.

The band has played on in two lineups since a 2016 split. Palác Akropolis keeps an Underground section in its programme; the samizdat trail runs through pubs such as U Černého vola.

Listening bars & record shops — thin, and said so

The hi-fi bar layer is thin in Prague; the record-shop and club-collective layer is not.

Tokyo-style listening bars have not really arrived in Prague, and we could not verify a single dedicated hi-fi bar in September 2026. What the city has instead is a thick, older layer: record shops that feed the DJs, and member-run cultural clubs that grew out of the jazz and underground scenes the regime tried to shut down.

The beer, wine and spa-water country

For the day you want to taste a place at its source: the first pale lager, the hop fields, a king's vines, a bitter from a spa town.

Plzeň · Pilsner Urquell brewerythe first pale lagerIn 1838 the town poured its bad beer away. Four years later it invented the style most of the world now drinks.

Plzeň's burghers were tired of their own top-fermented beer, preferred imported lagers, and built a modern brewery of their own. They hired a Bavarian, Josef Groll, who mashed the first batch on 5 October 1842 (see the beer section). Pilsner Urquell (German for 'original source', a trademark since 1898) is the ancestor of the pale lagers that make up most of the world's beer.

The tour is the brewery's own and it is corporate, but it ends somewhere worth the fare: 20 metres down, in 9 km of sandstone cellars, with a glass of unfiltered, unpasteurised beer drawn from an oak barrel. That beer does not travel; drink it here.

Žatec · the Saaz hop landscapeunesco since 2023The hop in half the world's lagers grows in one river valley, and the town was built around drying it.

Žatec (Saaz in German) was added to the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2023 as 'Žatec and the Landscape of Saaz Hops': fertile hop fields along the Ohře farmed continuously for centuries, and a town whose streets of tall drying and packing houses were designed around one crop. Žatecký chmel carries an EU Protected Designation of Origin, the strongest of the EU food labels.

The Temple of Hops and Beer is the town's showpiece: a hop lighthouse tower, a hop astronomical clock, a maze. Kitsch at the edges, but the guided tour explains why the Saaz hop thrives only here. Mělník · where the Vltava meets the Elbeludmila's vinesThe river you walked along in Prague ends here, under a chateau with a wine cellar.

Mělník sits on a bluff above the confluence of the Vltava and the Labe (Elbe). Tradition says Saint Ludmila, grandmother of Saint Wenceslas, planted the first vines here; the slope below the castle still carries her name. Charles IV brought in varieties from Burgundy and the Rhine and in 1358 issued a decree founding vineyards, with an official 'vineyard master' to protect them.

The Lobkowicz family's chateau runs tours of its cellars with tastings from its own vineyards. The wine is northern, light and dry; do not expect Moravian weight. The grape-harvest festival ran 18-20 September 2026. Stand on the terrace behind the chateau and watch two rivers become one.

Karlovy Vary · Becherovka and the spa waferthe thirteenth springA spa town that sold a stomach bitter as medicine, and a wafer made with the hot-spring water.

Josef Becher began experimenting with liqueurs in 1794 and made his first sale in 1807; it was sold as 'English bitter', a remedy. Today only two people know the full process and may enter the Drogikamr, the room where, once a week, the herbs and spices are mixed. Production left the old Becher building in 2010; the museum stays there, with the family recipe book, old bottles and a tasting at the end.

The other taste of the town is the oplatka, a thin round spa wafer. The first written mention is a 1788 guide to Karlovy Vary; the early ones were made with spring water and measured 13 cm. EU protection came in 2011, and only a wafer made with Karlovy Vary thermal water may carry the name. Buy them warm from a colonnade stand, not boxed at the station.

Castles, silver and sandstone

For the day you want rock and stone: a silver town, the emperor's treasury, a royal hunting forest, and the gorge country the 2022 fire burned.

Kutná Hora · silver, bones, St Barbarathe money townMedieval Bohemia's coins came out of this hill. The bones came out of its cemetery.

Kutná Hora began as a silver-mining town in the 13th century; Wenceslas II moved the royal mint here into the Italian Court, and the Prague groschen minted there paid for the kingdom. St Barbara, patron of miners, got the church: begun in 1388, interrupted by wars and money, finished only in 1905. The historic centre with St Barbara and the Sedlec cathedral has been on the UNESCO list since 1995.

The Sedlec ossuary, near the main station, is the bone chapel the world photographs. Tickets are timed and must be bought in advance online or at the Sedlec info centre. Honest note: the reason to come is silver and bone, not food; eat back in Prague.

Terezín has two parts: the Small Fortress, used as a Gestapo prison, and the town itself, which held the ghetto. The Terezín Memorial covers both. One ticket is valid for four days, each site once.

Lidicea field of rosesThe village was erased on 10 June 1942. Where it stood there is grass, and 24,000 rose bushes.

Lidice was destroyed by Nazi forces on 10 June 1942 in reprisal after the assassination of Reinhard Heydrich. The site is now open grass where the village was, with a museum and a gallery.

The rose garden came from Britain: the 'Lidice Shall Live' movement, chaired by the MP Sir Barnett Stross, pushed for it, and it opened in June 1955 as the Garden of Peace and Friendship. It now holds 240 varieties and more than 24,000 bushes. In late September and October the last roses are still out. The grounds and rose garden are free.

Konopištěthe archduke's antlersThe country house Franz Ferdinand left for Sarajevo in 1914, still lined with what he shot.

Konopiště was the last residence of Archduke Franz Ferdinand d'Este, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, assassinated in Sarajevo in June 1914. The water, the ponds and the parks

For the slow day: the carp country, the white horses, a count's arboretum, and two wild ravines you reach by tram.

Třeboň · the carp pondswhy carp at christmasA 16th-century water system built for fasting days still decides the Czech Christmas Eve dinner.

The Rosenberg family turned south Bohemian marsh into fish farms. Štěpánek Netolický's Golden Canal (Zlatá stoka), dug from 1508, about 45 km long, fed the ponds; Jakub Krčín built Rožmberk, the largest, between 1584 and 1590, behind a 2,355 m dam. Its area is given as about 489 ha in some sources and 647 ha in others. Radio Prague credits Krčín with the Golden Canal too; the Czech record says Netolický. Carp was fasting food, and carp on Christmas Eve became the national custom in the 19th century.

The paradox: the Czech lands are a major freshwater-fish producer, yet more than 90 per cent is exported. News for this October: the ceremonial Rožmberk harvest planned for 16-18 October 2026 is cancelled because of low water. The fishery runs 'Days with the Třeboň fishermen' at the Sádky holding tanks on 16-17 October instead.

The walk home

The corner shop, the night tram, and what a sailor can still buy between one and five in the morning.

The večerkathe evening shopEvery Prague street has one, and it is almost always run by a Vietnamese family.

Večerka means 'evening shop': the late-opening corner grocery. After the 1989 contracts ended, Vietnamese workers who stayed moved into trade, and the corner shop became the community's signature business. For the late plate it means rohlíky, a jar of pickled sausage, cheese, a cold beer. Opening hours vary shop by shop; many close near midnight, some later.

Trdelníkthe warningSold as 'traditional Czech'; it arrived in Prague in the early 2000s.

Trdelník is a spit cake: dough wound round a rod, baked over heat, rolled in sugar and walnuts. It belongs to the family of the Hungarian kürtőskalács, from the old Kingdom of Hungary and Transylvania. By the mid-19th century it was known as a Slovak dish; the Slovak town of Skalica credits the Transylvanian-born general József Gvadányi, who lived there from 1783 to 1801, with bringing the recipe, and Skalický trdelník received EU protected geographical indication in 2007.

In Prague it appeared in the early 2000s, and the Old Town stalls turned it into an ice-cream cone with strawberries and chocolate, sold as a 'traditional Czech cake'. The journalist Janek Rubeš has called the stalls tourist traps for exactly that. Eat a větrník or a koláč at a cukrárna instead.

On the plate

Young wine in plastic bottles, mushroom soup with dill, and a goose on the horizon: the Czech kitchen turns to autumn.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The same canon, told properly: what each thing is, the history that put it on a Bohemian table, and the detail that separates the real version from the one made for visitors.

#1
DISH

Svíčková na smetaně SVEECH-ko-vah na SMEH-tah-nyeh

Named for the tenderloin, svíčková, from svíčka, candle, for the cut's shape. Rettigová's 1826 Domácí kuchařka is the first well-known printed recipe, and Radio Prague calls it a national treasure two centuries on. The beef is larded and braised on carrot, parsley root, celeriac and onion with allspice, bay and pepper; the vegetables are puréed with cream into the sauce. It is the Sunday and wedding dish, and the sauce, not the meat, is the thing families argue about.
