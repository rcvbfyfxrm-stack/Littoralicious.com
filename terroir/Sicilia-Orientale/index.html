East Sicily

Siracusa · Val di Noto · Catania · Etna · Taormina — Sicilia
"To have seen Italy without having seen Sicily is not to have seen Italy at all, for Sicily is the clue to everything." — Goethe, Italian Journey, 1787
The names A marsh, a quail, a mountain named twice, and a bull that may never have been there: the east coast's names are older than its languages.
Siracusa is the Greek Syrakousai, usually traced to Syrako, a marsh beside the first settlement; the Corinthians who founded the city in 734–733 BC built it on the island first, for the harbour and the spring. That island is Ortigia, Greek Ortygia, conventionally read as 'quail island'. Catania was Katane, founded a few years later from Naxos, the first Greek colony in Sicily, whose site is the beach at Giardini Naxos below Taormina. Taormina is Tauromenion, the town on Monte Tauro; whether the mountain was named for a bull is a guess, not a fact. Etna is Greek Aitne, often linked to the verb for burning; Sicilians on its slopes do not call it Etna at all but 'a Muntagna, the Mountain, and in older speech Mongibello — Latin mons and Arabic jabal, two words for mountain stacked on each other, which is as good a summary of Sicilian history as any. Noto is the Roman Netum, moved ten kilometres after the earthquake of 1693; the ridge where it stood is still called Noto Antica.

The east coast of Sicily is one long argument between a volcano and the sea, and the kitchen takes both sides. From the Strait of Messina to Capo Passero it runs for some two hundred kilometres: Taormina on its cliff above the bay, Catania built of black lava under Etna, Siracusa on the island where the Greeks landed in 734 BC, and the honey-coloured baroque towns of the Val di Noto that rose all at once after the earthquake of 1693. Look at what is on the table. A pistachio that grows only on lava, a blood orange that needs the mountain's cold nights, a tomato from Pachino's salt-edged sand, Nerello Mascalese picked in late October a thousand metres up, swordfish from the strait and tuna from the old traps — and a breakfast that is a glass of almond ice with a warm brioche.

Make or break: sleep in two places, not one. Ortigia for the south — the market, the theatre, Noto and the coves of Vendicari — and Taormina or an Etna village for the north, with the wineries and the strait; the autostrada joins them in under two hours, and a single base costs you half the coast in driving. Eat the big meal at lunch in the villages and the late one in the cities, as Sicilians do.

The warning: Etna's summit is not a walk. Since 14 August 2026 hiking in the inner summit zone has been suspended except with licensed guides, and the volcano erupted again on 24 September; read the INGV bulletin before any plan above the cable car.

The Duke of Bronte never saw Bronte. In 1799 King Ferdinand gave Horatio Nelson the Duchy of Bronte, on Etna's western flank, for helping restore him to the throne of Naples. Nelson signed his letters 'Nelson & Bronte' for the rest of his life and never set foot on the estate; his heirs kept the Maniace lands until 1981. A year earlier he had watered his fleet at Siracusa on the way to the Nile. The town he never visited grows the Pistacchio Verde di Bronte DOP on bare lava, and harvests it only in odd-numbered years — 2026 is a rest year, so the next crop is 2027.

The soul of the east coast

It is a quarter to eight in a bar in Catania and nobody is having a cappuccino. At the counter a man in a suit is eating ice with a spoon — a glass of almond granita, pale and coarse, with a brioche beside it still warm enough to steam when he tears off the little topknot and dips it in. Behind him two students share a pistachio one with a cloud of cream on top. Outside, on Via Etnea, the street runs straight uphill towards a mountain that is smoking. This is breakfast. It has been breakfast here for as long as anyone can say, and it is the first thing to understand about this coast: the ice used to come down from the volcano.

The mountain is in everything. Etna is the highest active volcano in Europe and one of the most active anywhere, and the east coast is built of it, fed by it and periodically flattened by it. In 1669 a lava flow crossed seventeen kilometres of farmland and climbed the walls of Catania; after the earthquake of 1693 the city was rebuilt in its own black basalt, trimmed with white limestone, which is why its baroque looks like a photographic negative of Noto's. The same ash makes the soil that grows the Bronte pistachio on bare lava, the Etna lemon, the blood orange that colours only where the nights turn cold, and the Nerello Mascalese vines — some of them ungrafted and older than anyone's grandparents — that are picked a thousand metres up at the end of October, among the last grapes harvested in Italy.

The second thing is the sea, and it is not scenery. The Greeks came here by water: Corinthians founded Siracusa on the island of Ortigia in 734–733 BC because it had a great harbour and a freshwater spring a few steps from the sea, and the spring is still there, with papyrus growing wild in it. In 413 BC the Athenian fleet was trapped and destroyed inside that harbour. Nelson watered his ships at the same spring in July 1798 on his way to the Nile. The tuna came past this coast for millennia and the tonnare caught them in a labyrinth of nets — Marzamemi was built around one — while in the strait to the north the swordfish boats still hunt from a mast and a long gangway. Catania's fish market, the Pescheria, shouts every morning except Sunday under an arch that says W S. Agata.

The third is the stage. Siracusa's Greek theatre is cut into the living rock of the Temenite hill, and every spring since 1914 the INDA foundation has played Greek tragedy in it, to an audience of thousands, at sunset; the 2027 season opens on 7 May. Taormina's theatre was laid out so that the bay and the volcano are the backdrop — Goethe climbed up to it on 7 May 1787 and wrote that no audience had ever had such a scene before it. The puppet theatre, the Opera dei Pupi, still fights the paladins of Charlemagne in armour beaten by hand in Ortigia and Catania. On this coast even the saints are theatre: Catania fills with hundreds of thousands of people for Sant'Agata in February, and on 13 December many Sicilians eat no bread or pasta at all, only cuccìa — boiled wheat with ricotta — in memory of a ship of grain that ended a famine in Siracusa on Lucy's feast day.

Everything on these pages follows from those three. The food is layered — Greek, Arab, Norman, Spanish, monastic — and Sicilians will tell you which layer each dish belongs to, often wrongly and always with conviction. The grand tables are serious and recent: fourteen MICHELIN-starred kitchens sit between Ragusa and Taormina in the 2026 guide, most of them cooking the island's own produce with a precision the old trattorie never needed. But the coast is at its best in the small rooms and at the counters — the arancino eaten standing, the granita at seven, the plate of raw red prawns two streets from the market — and in exactly this season, October, when the summer crowd has gone, the harvest is on the mountain, and Zafferana fills every Sunday with honey, apples and chestnuts.

From Mithaecus to the red guide
The first Greek cookbook author, the Arab ice, the convent pastry, the baroque rebuilt in one go — and the stars that came after
2,500 years

Every plate on this coast is a stratum. Sicilians name the layers fluently — Greek, Arab, Norman, Spanish, convent — and the honest version is that some dishes can be dated and most cannot. What follows keeps the line between the two.

The Greeks wrote it down first. Siracusa had a reputation for its tables before Rome had one. Plato, in the Gorgias, names Mithaecus as the author of a treatise on Sicilian cookery, the first cookbook writer in Greek whose name survives; fragments of his fish recipes are quoted by later writers. The city that fed him was, Cicero said, the largest and most beautiful of the Greek cities. What survives of that table on today's plates is the olive, the wheat, the wine and the fish — and the habit of treating all four as a subject for argument.

The Arab layer is the one everyone claims. Muslim rule from the ninth to the eleventh century reorganised Sicilian agriculture, and citrus, sugar cane, rice, almond confectionery and the sherbet that became granita are routinely traced to it. Some of that is documented; much of it is tradition repeated until it hardened. The granita at least has a clear mechanism: sweet syrups, the Arab sharbat, cooled with snow carried down from Etna and stored in pits on its flanks, the neviere, until ice machines made the trade obsolete in the twentieth century.

Spain and the convents made the sweets. Chocolate reached Modica under Spanish rule, and the town still works it cold, without conching, so the sugar stays crystalline in the bar; whether the method itself came from Mexico is a story Modica tells, not one historians have settled. The monasteries and convents of the Val di Noto turned almonds, ricotta and candied fruit into a devotional pastry of their own — the cassata, the cannolo, the pasta reale moulded into fruit — and sold it through the turning wheel in the convent wall.

1693 rebuilt the stage. The earthquake of 11 January 1693, the worst in Italian history, destroyed some forty-five towns in the south-east and killed tens of thousands. Noto was refounded ten kilometres from its ruins and built in one golden stone; Catania rose again in black lava; Ragusa, Modica and Scicli were rebuilt in the same late baroque, and UNESCO listed eight of these towns together in 2002. The aristocratic kitchens of that rebuilt world employed French-trained cooks the Sicilians called monsù, from monsieur, and their timballi and gelatine are the ancestors of the island's fine dining.

The red guide came late, and then all at once. Ciccio Sultano's Duomo in Ragusa Ibla holds two MICHELIN stars and Gambero Rosso's highest Sicilian score for 2026; St. George by Heinz Beck in Taormina holds two; twelve more rooms between Marina di Ragusa and Linguaglossa hold one, including Cortile Spirito Santo, Siracusa's first star, awarded in late 2023, and Zash, which serves its tasting menu inside a restored nineteenth-century palmento on Etna. Read the list for what it says about the produce: almost every one of those menus is written in pistachio, almond, citrus, Etna wine and fish from this coast.

Wilhelm von GloedenTaorminaThe German baron whose photographs put Taormina on the map of the European elite.

Wilhelm von Gloeden arrived in Taormina in 1878 for his health and stayed almost until his death in 1931. He photographed local young men posed as figures from Greek antiquity among the ruins and agaves; the prints circulated across Europe and drew visitors including Oscar Wilde, Kaiser Wilhelm II and the Krupp family. His work is controversial today for its homoerotic and exploitative dimension. Part of his archive was destroyed by the Fascist police in the 1930s. Prints can be seen in some Taormina hotels and bars.

No permanent museum; look for exhibition notices in Taormina. Treat with the context it deserves.

Ortigia and Siracusa

Twenty-seven centuries on one island and the hill behind it.

Duomo di Siracusa: a temple still standing inside a churchOrtigiaWalk down the left aisle and you are walking between Doric columns raised in the 5th century BC.

The cathedral of Siracusa is built into the Temple of Athena, erected after the Greek victory over Carthage at Himera in 480 BC. In the 7th century the cella walls were opened into arches and the gaps between the peristyle columns walled up, so the fluted Doric columns stand embedded in the outer north wall and line the inside of the nave. The baroque facade by Andrea Palma was added after the 1693 earthquake. Cicero described the temple's gold-and-ivory doors before Verres stripped them. The temple is the reason the building survived: it had been in continuous religious use for 2,500 years.

The towns that rose after 1693.

The earthquakes of 9 and 11 January 1693 destroyed much of southeast Sicily and killed some 60,000 people. Noto's old hilltop town was abandoned and a new city laid out on a gentler slope to the south, on a grid of terraces: nobility and clergy above, the main civic and religious buildings along Corso Vittorio Emanuele. The protected names

What the DOP and IGP labels mean here — and one famous almond that has neither.

Pistacchio Verde di Bronte DOPDOP · BronteA nut grafted onto wild terebinth in lava, picked by hand every other year.

The DOP covers the Napoletana cultivar (also called Bianca or Nostrale) grown in Bronte, Adrano and Biancavilla, mostly on lava soils — the 'sciare'. It is grafted on terebinth (Pistacia terebinthus), the wild rootstock that thrives there. Harvest is by hand, every two years, generally 20 August to 10 October, and the husk must come off within 24 hours. The seed is emerald green under a ruby-red skin. The Arabs brought the tree in the 8th–9th centuries. Harvest falls in odd years: 2025 was a harvest, 2026 is a rest year, the next crop is 2027.

The 35th Sagra del Pistacchio ran 25–27 Sep and 2–4 Oct 2026. In shops, look for the DOP mark: 'pistacchio di Bronte' without it proves nothing.
Cioccolato di Modica IGPIGP 2018 · ModicaGritty on purpose: sugar crystals that never melt into the cocoa.

The IGP was registered in October 2018 and covers only the comune of Modica. Cocoa paste is melted at no more than 50 °C at the core, sugar is added, and the mass is not conched, so the sugar stays as crystals: the bar is grainy, crisp, with possible cocoa-butter bloom. Bars weigh no more than 100 g, tapered like a truncated pyramid; one 25 g segment in hot water makes a cup. Archive papers of the Grimaldi family document chocolatiers in 1746. Antica Dolceria Bonajuto, the oldest maker, stayed out of the IGP and cannot use the name.

Compare an IGP bar with a Bonajuto bar side by side — the dispute is on your tongue.
Pomodoro di Pachino IGPIGP · PachinoGreenhouse tomatoes on brackish well water at Sicily's southern tip.

Grown in Pachino, Portopalo di Capo Passero and parts of Noto and Ispica, always under cover (greenhouse or tunnel) and irrigated from local wells. Types: round smooth, ribbed costoluto, cherry, plum and miniplum, picked by hand every 3–4 days. Cultivation began on the coast in 1925; it spread from the 1950s with the first improvised greenhouses, and accelerated when the area's vineyards collapsed in the 1970s. The IGP specification notes an unusually long keeping time.

Transplanting runs August–February, so October is early season for most types; cherry and plum are year-round.
Limone dell'Etna IGPIGP 2020 · Ionian EtnaLemons that are made to bloom again by withholding water.

The IGP (registered 2020) covers Femminello and Monachello lemons in 16 comuni of the Ionian-Etna strip. Its specific technique is the 'forzatura' or 'secca': growers stop irrigating for a set period, then resume, forcing a second bloom whose fruit — verdelli, the summer lemon — ripens out of season. It works because the volcanic soil drains fast. Types include Primo Fiore, Bianchetto or Maiolino, and Verdello. Federico De Roberto's 1894 novel I Viceré already describes digging through Etna lava to plant citrus.

This is the lemon in a Catania kiosk seltz and in a lemon granita.
Arancia Rossa di Sicilia IGPIGP · Etna plainBlood oranges from 32 comuni — out of season in October, but the reason the plain is green.

The IGP covers Tarocco, Moro and Sanguinello in 32 comuni of the Catania, Enna and Siracusa provinces. Tarocco is picked December–May, Moro December–March, Sanguinello February–April, by hand with curved clippers; no de-greening is allowed. The specification cites the Jesuit Giovanni Battista Ferrari (1646), who credits a Genoese missionary back from the Philippines with bringing pigmented oranges. An ordinary amendment to the specification was approved in the EU Official Journal on 2 July 2026.

Not in season in October; buy marmalade or candied peel instead.
Ficodindia dell'Etna DOPDOP · Etna south-westRemove the first flowers and the plant answers with a late, larger fruit.

Three varieties: Sanguigna (red flesh), Sulfarina or Nostrale (yellow), Muscaredda or Sciannarina (white). The zone is Bronte, Adrano, Biancavilla, Santa Maria di Licodia, Ragalna, Camporotondo, Belpasso and Paternò. After flowering in late May–mid June growers strip most flowers or fruitlets ('scozzolatura'), and the plant reflowers: the second-flush fruit, the 'scozzolati' or 'bastardoni', are larger than the first-flush 'agostani'. How the plant reached Sicily is contested: Columbus's return from the Americas, or earlier Arab rule.

October is bastardoni season. Peel with gloves or a fork; the glochids are worse than the spines.
Ciliegia dell'Etna DOPDOP · June–JulyThe Mastrantonio cherry, grown on lava turned into orchard.

The DOP covers the Mastrantonio (Donnantonio) ecotype across almost all the foothill comuni of Etna in the province of Catania. Picking is by hand in June and July, with the long stem left on. Farmers made the 'sciare' — a dialect word of Arabic origin meaning burnt land — fertile with local irrigation over generations. Medium-large, bright red, crisp, low in acid.

Out of season in October; worth knowing for a June charter.
Ragusano DOPDOP · Hyblaean plateauA brick-shaped pasta filata cheese once aged astride a beam.

Made in the whole province of Ragusa plus Noto, Palazzolo Acreide and Rosolini. Curd is cooked with water at 80 °C, rested under the hot whey left from ricotta-making, then stretched and shaped into a rectangular block with a thin rind no more than 4 mm thick. Sweet and delicate young, sharp after long ageing; over 12 months it becomes a grating cheese. Trade beyond Sicily is documented in the 16th century; the old name caciocavallo likely comes from hanging pairs 'a cavallo' over a support to mature.

Keep it wrapped and out of the coldest part of the fridge; take it out an hour before serving.
Monti Iblei DOP extra-virgin olive oilDOP · Siracusa, Ragusa, CataniaTonda Iblea oil that smells of green tomato.

At least 80% from Tonda Iblea, Moresca, Nocellara Etnea, Verdese, Biancolilla and Zaituna, with sub-zones such as Val d'Anapo, Val Tellaro, Frigintini and Gulfi. The specification's tasting note: green or ripe tomato on the nose from Tonda Iblea, artichoke from Moresca, Verdese and Zaituna; herbaceous, medium to intense fruit, bitter and peppery. Olives are picked as their colour turns from dull to shiny green-yellow.

Store at 14–18 °C away from light, says the specification — not next to the galley stove.
Mandorla di Noto (Slow Food presidium, not a DOP)Presidio · Noto, AvolaThree old almond varieties beaten from the tree with long canes.

The presidium covers Noto, Avola, Rosolini and Canicattini Bagni and three varieties: Romana (squat, prized by pastry cooks), Pizzuta d'Avola (oval and flat — the one sold as 'mandorla di Avola') and Fascionello (a cross between them). Thick woody shells keep the oil and aroma but lower the yield. In summer crews ('ciurme') beat the branches with long canes onto sheets, hull the nuts and dry them in the sun. Early winter bloom makes frost a constant risk. Sicilian pasta reale, unlike central-European marzipan, is worked raw.

The 'Avola almond' has no DOP or IGP; trust named presidium producers or Caffè Sicilia's own sourcing.
Marzamemi tuna: bottarga, ventresca, mosciameSea · MarzamemiA tonnara village whose conserves outlived its tuna trap.

Marzamemi grew around its tonnara, described in secondary sources as built in 1642 under Spanish rule (Arab origins are also claimed). The trap no longer fishes, but conserve makers such as Campisi, who date their business to 1854, still sell tuna bottarga (salted, pressed roe), ventresca (belly in oil) and mosciame (salted, dried loin). Bottarga is grated raw over pasta, never cooked.

Buy in the village shops; keep bottarga vacuum-packed and cool.

The tomb that isn't Archimedes'CuriosityA Roman tomb in the Grotticelli necropolis has carried the name for centuries. Cicero found the real one.

Archimedes, born in Siracusa around 287 BC, was killed in 212 BC when the Romans took the city. In 75 BC Cicero, then quaestor in Sicily, found his tomb overgrown near one of the city gates, identified by a carving of a sphere inside a cylinder, as he recounts in the Tusculan Disputations. Its location is now lost. The 'Tomba di Archimede' signposted in the Grotticelli necropolis is a Roman-era tomb with a Doric pediment and has nothing to do with him.

1693: the earthquake that made the baroqueCuriosityThe worst earthquake in Italian history destroyed some 45 towns, and the rebuilt ones became a UNESCO site.

On 9 January and again on 11 January 1693 an earthquake estimated at magnitude 7.4 struck southeastern Sicily. About 60,000 people died; Catania lost perhaps two-thirds of its population. The reconstruction, led by the Duke of Camastra for the Spanish crown, rebuilt entire towns in the new late-baroque style, sometimes on new sites (Noto, Grammichele) and sometimes on the old ones (Catania, Ragusa Ibla). In 2002 UNESCO inscribed eight of these towns: Caltagirone, Militello Val di Catania, Catania, Modica, Noto, Palazzolo Acreide, Ragusa and Scicli.

Etna in numbersCuriosityThe highest active volcano in Europe, and one of the most active in the world.

Etna is about 3,400 m high (the height changes with each eruption), with a base about 40 km across. It has been in almost continuous activity for centuries and has the longest documented record of volcanism of any volcano, going back about 2,700 years. UNESCO inscribed it as a World Heritage Site in 2013. Its fertile soils feed vineyards, pistachios, citrus and orchards, and nearly a million people live on its slopes. Locals call it 'a Muntagna', the mountain.

Sicilians on its slopes call it the Mountain, and treat it as weather. The old sailors' coast — true stories of this sea
An Athenian fleet trapped in a harbour, the claw of Archimedes, Nelson at the spring, the swordfish hunters of the strait and the morning the harbour of Augusta ran dry
10 true stories

Every story here is sourced, with the arguments left in where the sources argue. Each ends with what an old hand would want you to understand, and with a place where you can still stand and see it. This is a coast where the sea decided history more than once — and where the fishing that fed it for three thousand years is, in places, still going on.

War in the harbour

Siracusa's two great sieges, and the admiral who watered at the spring.

The harbour that ate a fleettrue storyAthens in the Great Harbour, 413 BC

In 415 BC Athens sent the largest armada of its age against Syracuse. Two years later the fleet was penned inside the Great Harbour, the horseshoe bay south of Ortigia. Thucydides (VII.36) records the Syracusan answer to Athenian seamanship: they cut their prows down 'to a smaller compass to make them more solid', made the 'cheeks' (the catheads) stouter and braced them with stays six cubits into the hull, as the Corinthians had done at Naupactus. Athenian triremes, built light in the bow for sailing round and ramming the side, were now to be met head-on, 'solid and stout beaks against hollow and weak ones', in water too crowded to manoeuvre. On 27 August 413 BC an eclipse of the moon made Nicias wait 'thrice nine days' before retreating (VII.50). The Syracusans used the time to close the harbour mouth, 'nearly a mile wide', with boats, merchantmen and galleys moored broadside (VII.59). About 110 Athenian ships tried to break the boom and failed. The army fled overland and was destroyed; the survivors, 'not less than seven thousand', were put in the stone quarries, given half a pint of water and a pint of corn a day for eight months (VII.87).

★ The old hand's point: Sea-room is a weapon. A fast, handy fleet that lets itself be shut in a closed basin trades its whole advantage for the enemy's: no room to turn, no water to back into except a hostile shore. The Syracusans did not out-sail Athens; they changed the geometry of the fight. Any skipper who has been caught in a crowded harbour with the wind setting onto the quay knows the lesson in miniature: decide where you will fight before you are forced to.

Where the sources argue. Which quarry held the Athenians is not stated by Thucydides; the Latomia del Paradiso and the Latomia dei Cappuccini are both claimed by local tradition. Ship numbers are Thucydides' own and approximate ('about one hundred and ten').

Touch it today: Stand on the Lungomare Alfeo on the west side of Ortigia and look across the Porto Grande: the whole battlefield is in view, mouth included, between Ortigia and the Plemmirio headland. Then walk the Latomia del Paradiso in the Neapolis archaeological park, the quarry tradition places the prisoners in.

Archimedes' claw, and the mirrors nobody sawtrue storythe Roman siege, 214-212 BC

When Marcus Claudius Marcellus attacked Syracuse from the sea, he brought sixty quinqueremes, some lashed in pairs to carry sambucae, ladder-engines four feet broad raised by pulleys against the walls (Polybius VIII.4). Archimedes, then in his seventies, met them with engines graded by range: catapults for distance, small scorpions through loopholes for close work, and beams that swung out over the wall. From one hung an iron hand on a chain. It gripped a ship by the prow, the counterweighted beam was pressed down, the ship was lifted upright onto its stern and then let fall, to capsize or fill (VIII.6). Marcellus joked that Archimedes 'uses my ships to ladle sea-water into his wine cups'. The city fell anyway in 212 BC, by treachery during a festival, and Archimedes was killed by a Roman soldier. The burning mirrors that set the fleet alight are a different matter: no contemporary describes them.

★ The old hand's point: The claw worked because a galley's weakness is its bow: a long, light hull with all its buoyancy aft of a ram is easy to lever up by the stem. And it worked under walls with deep water close in, where a ship has to come to deliver its boarding party. Close-quarters attack on a fortified shore is always a gamble with your own hull.

Where the sources argue. The claw is in Polybius (2nd c. BC, the nearest source), Livy and Plutarch. The burning mirrors are not. Lucian (2nd c. AD) is the first to say Archimedes burned ships, without mirrors; Galen, later in the same century, is the first to mention mirrors; Anthemius of Tralles tried to work out the geometry in the 6th century. Descartes rejected it. In October 2005 an MIT class (course 2.009) ignited a stationary red-oak hull with 127 one-foot-square mirrors at about 100 feet, in under ten minutes once the sun cleared cloud; a first attempt under cirrostratus failed, and the instructor noted a target would need to stay nearly still for minutes. Feasible on a calm, clear day against a moored target; unattested by any witness.

Touch it today: The sea walls of Ortigia and the Castello Maniace at its southern tip stand on the line Marcellus attacked; the Euryalus fortress on the Epipolae ridge above the city is the best-preserved Greek fortification of the period.

Watering at Arethusatrue storyNelson at Syracuse, July 1798

In the summer of 1798 Rear-Admiral Horatio Nelson had chased Bonaparte's Egypt expedition to Alexandria, found the port empty, and beat back to Sicily with no frigates and no news. The Kingdom of Naples was officially neutral and had decided to give his squadron no help; through Lady Hamilton's influence at court, writes Southey, secret orders reached the Sicilian governors, and at Syracuse Nelson got 'everything which he wanted'. His water casks were filled at the spring of Arethusa, the freshwater source that rises a few metres from the sea on Ortigia's harbour side. To Sir William and Lady Hamilton he wrote: 'Thanks to your exertions, we have victualled and watered; and surely watering at the fountain of Arethusa, we must have victory. We shall sail with the first breeze; and be assured I will return either crowned with laurel or covered with cypress.' He sailed on 25 July, learned at Coron that the French had steered for Egypt, and on 1 August 1798 found them in Aboukir Bay. The Battle of the Nile followed that night.

★ The old hand's point: A fleet's range is its water. Nelson's ships had been at sea for weeks on a 600-league chase; without a friendly port to fill casks and take on fresh food, there was no second pursuit. The admiral who finds water and victuals keeps the initiative. The galley chef provisioning a yacht before a long passage is doing the same arithmetic.

Where the sources argue. The words are as printed by Southey (1813). Secondary sources date the letter 22 July 1798 from HMS Vanguard; the exact date was not confirmed on a primary edition in this research.

The strait and its hunters

The real oceanography under Scylla and Charybdis, and the boats that still chase swordfish there.

Scylla, Charybdis and the rematrue storywhat the Strait of Messina really does

Homer put a six-headed monster on the Calabrian side and an all-swallowing whirlpool, Charybdis, on the Sicilian side near Capo Peloro. Behind the myth is a strait 32 km long and 3 km wide at its narrowest (ESA), joining two seas with tides out of step: when the Tyrrhenian is high the Ionian is low, and the water rushes across a shallow sill. Strait pilots call the stream the rema: the scendente runs north to south, the montante south to north. Each runs roughly six hours, with an ora di stanca, slack water, between them. The streams reach up to about 5 knots (Imray), throw off eddies and whirlpools, and generate internal waves visible from satellites. The montante also lifts cold, dense Ionian water up into the strait. Fish that live hundreds of metres down, lanternfish, hatchetfish, viperfish, ride it up at night when they follow plankton to the surface, and are thrown onto the beaches between Ganzirri and Capo Peloro, most often in winter with Scirocco. Messina naturalists have recorded these strandings since at least Mazzarelli in 1909.

★ The old hand's point: Treat the strait as a tidal gate, not a stretch of water. Time the passage for slack or a fair stream, keep out of the traffic separation scheme and the ferry lanes, and expect sudden overfalls and swirls off the points even in calm weather: the danger the Greeks named is real, only smaller.

Where the sources argue. Classical Charybdis is placed off Capo Peloro by most authors; some locate it nearer Messina harbour's sickle (Punta San Raineri). No modern whirlpool approaches the myth's scale; the danger to an ancient oared ship was the combination of current, eddy and wind against tide.

Touch it today: Walk the beach at Capo Peloro / Torre Faro at dawn after a winter Scirocco with the montante running: you may find the stranded deep-sea fish yourself. The swirls are visible from the shore off the point.

The felucatrue storyhunting swordfish from a mast and a gangway

Each spring and summer the swordfish pass through the Strait of Messina, and a boat built for nothing else goes out to meet them: the feluca. It carries a tall lattice lookout mast and a passerella, a gangway about a metre wide that projects forward far beyond the bow, roughly one and a half times the boat's length. Lookouts at the masthead spot the fish basking or swimming near the surface; the boat is driven up from astern, and the harpooner, standing at the very end of the gangway, strikes before the hull is close enough to frighten the fish. Ganzirri, the fishing village between Messina and Torre Faro, is the Sicilian base; Scilla and Bagnara work the Calabrian shore. The fishery is one-fish-at-a-time and highly selective. On 26 March 2026 the Sicilian Region announced nearly 386,000 euros from the EU FEAMPA 2021-2027 programme to support the historic feluche, describing them as a selective system that 'drastically reduces unwanted catches'.

★ The old hand's point: Height of eye is everything. The feluca's mast and gangway are the old answer to two problems: seeing a fish under a glittering sea, and reaching it without the bow wave warning it. Every lookout who has climbed the ratlines to read coral or a reef channel understands the mast; every helmsman who has approached a mooring buoy upwind understands the gangway.

Where the sources argue. Origins are argued: sources call the boat 'of probable Phoenician origin' or 'Arab heritage'. Older rowing luntri, and the claim that the boat-songs used Greek-derived words, were not verified in this research and are left out.

Touch it today: Ganzirri and Torre Faro, north of Messina, where feluche lie along the shore in season (roughly late spring to late summer). Swordfish alla messinese and involtini (braciole) of swordfish are the dishes to eat there.

The tuna and the fishermen

A village built round a trap, and the family Verga put in a boat called Providence.

Marzamemitrue storya village built around a tuna trap

Marzamemi's name is usually derived from the Arabic marsa al-hamam, 'bay of the doves'. Its reason to exist was the tonnara, a fixed labyrinth of nets anchored each spring across the route of bluefin tuna moving along the coast. Sources date the tonnara to 1642 under Spanish rule and rank it the most important in Sicily after Favignana. In the 18th century the Nicolaci, princes of Villadorata, held the right to work it, and the village grew as a company town: the palazzo, the tonnara church of San Francesco di Paola (1752), the warehouses and the fishermen's houses round the courtyard that is now the piazza. The tuna were driven through chambers of net into the camera della morte and killed in the mattanza under a rais, the master of the trap. Overfishing is recorded even in the Bourbon era; the plant became chiefly a canning works in the 20th century and was abandoned after wartime bombing. The old church, damaged in the war, was restored with 600,000 euros of regional money as a Museo del Mare for underwater archaeology (announced 2019).

★ The old hand's point: A tonnara is a bet on a migration route that never moves. It works only where the coast funnels fish close inshore, as it does here between the Ionian and the Sicilian Channel. When industrial purse-seiners began catching the bluefin offshore, before they reached the coast, the fixed traps had nothing left to catch: the fish was not gone, it was intercepted.

Where the sources argue. Dates and owners conflict across sources: some say the Villadorata founded the settlement in 1630, others that the tonnara was built in 1642 under Spain, others that the church and manor were built in 1752 by Baron Bernardo Calascibetta and only later managed by the Nicolaci. No primary source was reached; the year of the last mattanza was not confirmed.

Touch it today: The Piazza Regina Margherita in Marzamemi, the tonnara courtyard, with the old church and the Villadorata palazzo. Bottarga di tonno, tuna salted and cured (mosciame, lattume), is still made and sold in the village.

Aci Trezzatrue storythe Cyclops' rocks and the boat called Provvidenza

Off Aci Trezza stand the Faraglioni dei Ciclopi, black basalt stacks and the island of Lachea, which tradition identifies with the rocks the blinded Polyphemus hurled at Odysseus' ship in Odyssey IX. They are volcanic, columnar lavas interleaved with clays, and since 1989 a protected area named for that encounter. In 1881 Giovanni Verga set I Malavoglia here. The Toscano family, nicknamed 'Malavoglia' (the unwilling) though they are the hardest workers in the village, own one boat, the Provvidenza. In 1863 Padron 'Ntoni buys a cargo of lupins on credit to sell along the coast; a storm sinks the Provvidenza's voyage, drowns his son Bastianazzo and loses the cargo, and the family spends the rest of the book paying for it. In 1947-48 Luchino Visconti filmed La terra trema in Aci Trezza with real fishermen and their families, speaking in dialect, no professional actors.

★ The old hand's point: Verga understood what every small-boat owner knows: the boat is the family's capital and the sea is its creditor. One uninsured cargo, one autumn storm, and a working family is ruined. The novel is the best economic history of Mediterranean small fishing ever written.

Where the sources argue. The identification of the Faraglioni with Polyphemus' rocks is literary tradition, not geography: the Odyssey gives no location that can be fixed with certainty.

Touch it today: The harbour of Aci Trezza, with its painted wooden boats, looks straight at the Faraglioni. Wrecks and the wave

Gliders in the sea, a Christmas wreck the state ignored, and the morning the harbour ran dry.

Ladbroketrue storythe gliders that fell short, 9-10 July 1943

Operation Husky, the Allied invasion of Sicily, began on the night of 9-10 July 1943 with British landings on the beaches between Cassibile, Avola and Pachino. Ahead of them went Operation Ladbroke: 136 Waco and 8 Horsa gliders carrying about 2,075 men of the 1st Airlanding Brigade, towed from Tunisia to seize the Ponte Grande, the bridge over the Anapo just south of Syracuse. A gale was blowing, the tug crews were inexperienced at night, and flak and searchlights pushed them off course. Sixty-five gliders were cast off too early and came down in the sea; about 252 men drowned. Only twelve landed where they were meant to. A platoon under Lieutenant Withers took the bridge; 87 men held it until 15:30 on 10 July, when, down to fifteen unwounded, they surrendered to the Italians. Forty-five minutes later the 2nd Royal Scots Fusiliers, come up from the beaches, retook it. On 3 September 1943 the armistice between Italy and the Allies was signed at Fairfield Camp, Cassibile, by General Giuseppe Castellano and General Walter Bedell Smith; it was announced on 8 September.

★ The old hand's point: Wind and darkness decide distance. Releasing a glider is like letting go of a tow in a seaway: misjudge the range to the lee shore and the craft cannot claw back. The men who drowned were lost to a navigation error over water, not to the enemy.

Where the sources argue. Casualty figures vary between sources (Wikipedia gives 313 British killed overall in the operation). Blame for the early releases, inexperienced USAAF tug crews versus weather and anti-aircraft fire, is still argued.

Touch it today: The Ponte Grande over the Anapo at the head of the Porto Grande of Siracusa; the Syracuse War Cemetery (Commonwealth) holds many of the Ladbroke dead. The landing beaches run south from Fontane Bianche past Cassibile to Avola.

The Christmas wreck off Portopalo, 1996true story

On the night of 25-26 December 1996, some 19 nautical miles off Portopalo di Capo Passero, about three hundred people from India, Sri Lanka and Pakistan were being transferred in a heavy sea from the freighter Yohan (Iohan) to a wooden fishing boat, 18 by 4 metres, known by its registration as F174. The two vessels collided and the F174 sank. At least 283 men, women and children died. For more than four years authorities treated reports of the wreck as rumour. Then a Portopalo fisherman, Salvatore Lupo, brought up in his nets the identity card of a seventeen-year-old Tamil, Anpalagan Ganeshu. On 15 June 2001 Giovanni Maria Bellu published the story in La Repubblica; a remotely operated camera, paid for by Repubblica and L'Espresso, filmed the hull and the remains at 108 metres. A Pakistani organiser, Tourab Ahmed Sheikh, was sentenced to 30 years in 2008. The wreck was never raised.

★ The old hand's point: Fishermen know what lies on their grounds before anyone else does, and they carry the weight of it. For years the wreck was treated as a ghost story. What broke the silence was one fisherman who kept what his net brought up and handed it over.

Where the sources argue. The exact death toll is unknown; 283 is the minimum usually cited. Names of the ships vary in transliteration (Yohan / Iohan El Hallal).

Touch it today: A memorial stands at Capo Passero. Bellu's book I fantasmi di Portopalo was later made into a RAI miniseries of the same name. The grave itself is open water south-east of the cape.

11 January 1693true storythe day the harbour of Augusta ran dry

The earthquake of 11 January 1693 is the strongest in Italy's historical record. It flattened Catania, Noto, Ragusa and dozens of towns of the south-east; the baroque Val di Noto that visitors now admire was built from its rubble. It also raised a tsunami along some 230 km of coast. At Augusta the sea first withdrew and left the harbour completely dry, then returned, flooding about 165 m inland; waves are reported up to 15 m. At Catania and Messina boats were thrown onto the shore; at Messina the sea pulled back about 100 m before flooding the port basin; at Mascali it ran about 1.5 km inland. Modern simulations agree with the eyewitnesses: along almost the whole east coast of Sicily the first motion was a withdrawal of the sea.

★ The old hand's point: When the sea goes out without a tide to explain it, it is coming back. A harbour emptying is the warning, and minutes is all there is: get to high ground, or, if the boat is already off soundings, stay in deep water where a tsunami is only a long swell. This coast has done it twice in recorded history, 1693 and 1908.

Where the sources argue. Wave heights are reconstructed from 17th-century accounts and vary between studies; the source of the tsunami (the quake itself or a submarine landslide) is still debated in the literature.

Touch it today: Augusta's harbour and its old island town; and the whole of Noto, Catania's Via Crociferi and Ragusa Ibla, which exist in their present form because of this day.

Etna wine — the contrade, the palmenti, the cellar door
Sicily's mountain wine: a DOC since 1968, Nerello on lava terraces, Carricante from Milo — and the eighteen cellars that open the door
the signature

The other thing this coast is uniquely for. Etna wine is volcanic, high and recent in its fame: some 440 producers in 2024 against sixty a decade earlier, bottling Nerello Mascalese by the contrada — the named lava parcel — the way Burgundy bottles by climat. Nothing is walk-in; every cellar below is booked ahead, and in October the harvest is on, so book early and expect the winemaker to be in the vines. If you have one afternoon, book a contrada tasting on the north slope between Castiglione and Randazzo and drive back by Linguaglossa for dinner.

How to read it

The DOC, the contrade, the palmento, the white that only one commune may call Superiore.

Etna DOC: the volcano as a wine regionEtna wineBorn 1968, grown from 60 producers to over 400: Europe's most talked-about mountain wine.

The Etna DOC was founded in 1968. Its reds are Nerello Mascalese, softened with Nerello Cappuccio; its whites are Carricante, with Catarratto. Vines climb to the DOC's 1,000-metre limit and beyond, many on lava terraces as bush vines (alberello). Jamie Goode counts producers rising from 60 in 2013 to 130 in 2017 to 441 by 2024, on about 1,290 hectares making 5.8 million bottles. In November 2023 the Consorzio voted unanimously to seek DOCG status; Vinous (Sep 2026) reports ministry approval as imminent, for the 2026 or 2027 vintage, with lower contrada yields and two years' ageing.

No winery takes walk-ins; book every visit ahead, and expect harvest (Sep-Oct) slots to be scarce. Prices 2026: EUR 40-55 for a standard tasting (Cornelissen, Graci, Palmento Costanzo), EUR 210+ for top-tier flights.
Reading a contrada on the labelContradeSame grape, same cellar, a different lava flow: the contrada is Etna's cru.

A contrada is a named district of the volcano, often defined by a single lava flow of a known date. Since the contrade were written into the rules, a producer may print one on the label of an Etna Rosso or Bianco: Calderara Sottana, Santo Spirito, Feudo di Mezzo, Guardiola, Rampante, Arcurìa. Decanter reports 133 contrade; Jamie Goode counts 142 units across 11 communes (2024). Vinous credits Marc de Grazia of Tenuta delle Terre Nere with the strategy that made the contrada Etna's language. From the 2026 vintage, Vinous adds, communal names may also appear on front labels.

Ask for a horizontal flight — several contrade, one vintage. Terre Nere and Graci both pour contrada wines side by side.

The palmento: a winery made of stone and gravityPalmentoEvery old Etna farmhouse had one. Since 1997 almost none may make wine.

A palmento is a stone wine-press building on a slope: grapes were trodden in an upper basin, the must ran by gravity into a lava-stone vat below, and a great beam press finished the job. Jamie Goode writes that EU hygiene rules banned palmenti for winemaking in 1997, forcing growers to sell grapes instead of wine; some still use them illegally, and Salvo Foti has sold palmento wine abroad labelled as experimental. Today you stand inside restored ones: Graci's 19th-century palmento, Palmento Costanzo (still the working cellar), and Zash at Riposto, where a MICHELIN-starred restaurant sits in the old fermenting vat.

Graci and Palmento Costanzo both include the palmento in every tasting (2026). Ask to see the press beam.

Moscato of Siracusa and Noto: the oldest wine story in ItalySweet wineSyracusans say their Moscato is the Pollio of the Greek tyrants. Nobody can prove it — nobody has disproved it.

Tradition identifies Moscato di Siracusa with 'Pollio' or 'Biblino', a wine said to have been brought by Pollis, a legendary ruler of Syracuse, in the 7th century BC; the link is a legend, not documentation. The wine nearly vanished in the 20th century. Moscato di Siracusa became a DOC in 1973 and was folded into the wider Siracusa DOC in 2011 because so little was made. Moscato di Noto became a DOC in 1974 and was renamed Noto DOC in 2008 to include Nero d'Avola. Antonino Pupillo founded his estate in the 1980s specifically to revive it.

Noto AnticaRuinThe town that was abandoned in 1693, left on its ridge among almond and carob trees.

About 10 km northwest of modern Noto, on the Monte Alveria ridge, the ruins of the original city were left as they fell in 1693. A Sicel and Greek settlement before the Romans, it was the capital of one of Sicily's three Arab-era valleys (the Val di Noto). Today you enter through the rebuilt Porta della Montagna and walk among overgrown walls, the shell of the castle and the Eremo della Madonna della Provvidenza. It is quiet, free and explains better than any museum why the new Noto exists.

Free, open ground, no facilities. Wear sturdy shoes and bring water. 20 minutes by car from Noto.

ModicaVal di NotoA town poured down two ravines, famous for cold-worked chocolate.

Modica rebuilt after 1693 on both sides of a gorge, with Modica Alta above and Modica Bassa below, joined by stairways. San Giorgio, attributed to Rosario Gagliardi, rises at the top of 250 steps; San Pietro has life-size apostles on its stair. The town's chocolate is made by a cold method descended from the Spanish period: cocoa ground with sugar at low temperature so the sugar does not melt, giving a gritty texture. Cioccolato di Modica gained EU IGP status in 2018. The poet Salvatore Quasimodo, Nobel 1959, was born here in 1901.

About 50 minutes by car from Noto. Steep streets; wear flat shoes. The Chocobarocco chocolate festival is usually held in the winter months; 2026-27 dates not verified.

Ragusa IblaVal di NotoThe old town on its own spur, reached by stairs from the newer upper city.

After 1693 Ragusa split: part of the population rebuilt on the old site, Ibla, and part founded a new grid city on the plateau above. The two were separate municipalities until 1926. In Ibla, Gagliardi's San Giorgio stands at the top of a sloping piazza, its three-tier facade and green dome visible from the stairway of Santa Maria delle Scale that joins the two towns. PantalicaUNESCOSome 5,000 tombs cut into the cliffs of two gorges, 13th to 7th century BC.

The necropolis of Pantalica, inland from Siracusa, has about 5,000 rock-cut chamber tombs honeycombing the limestone walls of the Anapo and Calcinara gorges, dating from the 13th to the 7th century BC. Paolo Orsi excavated them from 1895. On the plateau are the foundations of the Anaktoron, a megalithic 'prince's palace'; Byzantine-era cave chapels were cut later. The old railway line along the Anapo valley floor is now a walking path. Pantalica forms the UNESCO site with Siracusa (2005).

Access from Sortino (north) or Ferla (south): the two roads do not join, so pick one. The valley-floor walk is flat; reaching the tombs means steep paths. No services on site; bring water and food.

Gole dell'AlcantaraNatureA gorge of basalt columns cut by a cold river, 20 km inland from Taormina.

An ancient lava flow from Etna filled the bed of the Alcantara river; as it cooled it cracked into hexagonal basalt columns. The river then cut through it, leaving a narrow canyon whose walls look like organ pipes, up to 25 m high. The name comes from the Arabic al-qantara, 'the bridge', after a Roman bridge that stood here. The water stays very cold even in August. The area is a regional river park.

Free public access by the stairs on the SS185 at Motta Camastra; the private 'Gole' park charges for its lift and paths. Wader hire for walking up the river. In winter and after rain, the gorge floor is closed.

★ Opera dei Pupi in Ortigia: the Vaccaro-Mauceri familydo this oneA family of puppeteers that carves, paints, armours and performs its own knights.

Why this one. It is a living family trade in the town you are already sleeping in: the same family carves, paints, armours and performs its own knights, the show lasts about an hour and needs no Italian, and they show the workshop after the curtain.

The Opera dei Pupi was proclaimed a UNESCO Masterpiece of Intangible Heritage in 2001: marionettes on iron rods, up to a metre tall and heavily armoured, act out the deeds of Charlemagne's paladins, Orlando and Rinaldo. 4 traps

The warnings

None of them is a reason not to come.

Etna 'summit' tours sold from Taorminathe warningMost coach tours stop at the Silvestri craters and the souvenir shops at 1,900 m.

Many day tours sold on Corso Umberto or at cruise terminals drive to Rifugio Sapienza, give you an hour among the Crateri Silvestri (free and walkable alone) and the shops, and return. That can be pleasant, but it is not the summit. Since 14 August 2026 hiking in the summit's Permanent Hazard Zone is suspended except with licensed guides, so any promise of 'reaching the craters' should be checked against the current ordinance. Book licensed volcanological guides directly, ask the altitude reached and what happens if access is closed.

Ask: licensed guide? which altitude? refund if closed? Check ct.ingv.it.
Corso Umberto at midday, and Isola Bella sunbeds in Augustthe warningTaormina is two different towns, before 10:00 and after.

From late morning to late afternoon in season, Corso Umberto and the Teatro Antico take thousands of cruise and coach day visitors; restaurant terraces on the main street charge for the view rather than the food. Isola Bella's free beach is tiny and the lidos price sunbeds accordingly in July-August. The fix is timing: stay overnight, walk the Corso early, go to the theatre at 09:00, eat in the side streets or in Castelmola.

Driving into Ortigia and Taorminathe warningBoth centres are limited-traffic zones with camera fines.

Ortigia and central Taormina are ZTL (zona a traffico limitato) areas: cameras photograph number plates and fines arrive months later, often via the rental company with a fee. Satellite navigation will happily route you in. Park at the Talete car park (Ortigia) or Lumbi and Porta Catania car parks (Taormina) and walk.

If your hotel is inside the ZTL, ask it to register your plate in advance.
Cavagrande in August, and papyrus 'art' in Ortigiathe warningTwo classic disappointments, both fixable.

Cavagrande's 250 m climb out of the gorge in midsummer heat sends people to hospital each year; the path also closes in high fire-risk periods. Go in spring or autumn, early, with water. In Ortigia, many shops sell 'papyrus' paintings mass-made on banana-leaf paper or imported sheets; if you want the real thing, buy where you can watch a sheet being made from Siracusan plants.

Who knows

Read before you go: Verga, Vittorini, Quasimodo, BrancatiFollowFour writers born on this coast who explain it better than any guide.

Giovanni Verga (Catania, 1840) wrote I Malavoglia (1881), the story of a fishing family in Aci Trezza, and the stories of Cavalleria rusticana. Verga's house in Catania (Via Sant'Anna) is a museum; Aci Trezza has the Casa del Nespolo, a museum set up in the house identified with the Malavoglia.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The canon, told properly: what each thing is, the history that put it on an east-Sicilian table, and where the story is argued rather than settled.

#1
DISH

Granita and the brioche col tuppo

Granita is the sherbet of the Arab kitchen made with snow: until ice machines arrived, snow was packed into stone pits on Etna's flanks, the neviere, and carried down to the cities. The coarse Catanese granita is not a sorbet — it is stirred as it freezes so the crystals stay loose — and it is eaten for breakfast with a brioche whose topknot, the tuppo, is the first thing torn off and dipped.

#2
DISH

Pasta alla Norma

Pasta with fried aubergine, tomato and grated ricotta salata. Catania claims it absolutely; the name is disputed. The popular story ties it to Bellini's opera Norma (1831), the composer being Catanese. The other credits the playwright Nino Martoglio (1870–1921), who is said to have tasted it and called it 'a real Norma', a perfect thing. Neither story is documented at the time. The ricotta salata is not optional.

#3
DISH

The arancino

Catania says arancino, pointed like the volcano; Palermo says arancina, round. The Accademia della Crusca, Italy's language academy, has ruled that both forms are correct. The 'cone like Etna' explanation is folklore, but the shape is real and regional: in Catania the meat ragù one is pointed and the pistachio one is often round.
WherePasticceria Savia, Catania · Maps →
#4
DISH

Cioccolato di Modica

Modica works chocolate at low temperature without conching, so the sugar does not dissolve and the bar breaks with a grainy snap. The method is said to descend from the Aztec metate, brought by the Spanish; the town tells that story, historians have not settled it. The name is an IGP since 2018 — and Antica Dolceria Bonajuto, founded in 1880 and the most famous maker, left the consortium the same year, so its bars legally cannot carry the name.

#5
DISH

Bottarga and the tonnara

For centuries the tuna came past this coast and the tonnare caught them in a chamber of nets, hauled in the mattanza to the crews' work-song, the cialoma. Marzamemi grew around one. The trap is gone — industrial purse-seining killed the tonnare — but the village still cures the fish: bottarga from the roe, ventresca from the belly, mosciame from the fillet, dried in the sea air.

#6
DISH

Cuccìa

On 13 December, Santa Lucia's day, many Sicilians eat no bread or pasta at all, only cuccìa: whole wheat berries boiled and served with ricotta and sugar, or with vino cotto. The custom recalls a famine in Siracusa ended, the story goes, by a ship of grain that arrived on Lucy's feast; the people were too hungry to mill it, and boiled it whole.
