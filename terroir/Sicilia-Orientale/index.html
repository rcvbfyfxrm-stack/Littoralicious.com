East Sicily

Siracusa · Catania · Taormina — Sicilia
"To have seen Italy without having seen Sicily is not to have seen Italy at all, for Sicily is the clue to everything." — Goethe, Italian Journey, 1787

One coast, three bases, three guides. From the Strait of Messina to Capo Passero the east coast runs for some two hundred kilometres between a volcano and the sea. It is too much for one stay and too much for one guide — so each town below has its own, with the places around it, and never more than three of a kind.

The essay — the coast that holds them together

It is a quarter to eight in a bar in Catania and nobody is having a cappuccino. At the counter a man in a suit is eating ice with a spoon — a glass of almond granita, pale and coarse, with a brioche beside it still warm enough to steam when he tears off the little topknot and dips it in. Behind him two students share a pistachio one with a cloud of cream on top. Outside, on Via Etnea, the street runs straight uphill towards a mountain that is smoking. This is breakfast. It has been breakfast here for as long as anyone can say, and it is the first thing to understand about this coast: the ice used to come down from the volcano.

The mountain is in everything. Etna is the highest active volcano in Europe and one of the most active anywhere, and the east coast is built of it, fed by it and periodically flattened by it. In 1669 a lava flow crossed seventeen kilometres of farmland and climbed the walls of Catania; after the earthquake of 1693 the city was rebuilt in its own black basalt, trimmed with white limestone, which is why its baroque looks like a photographic negative of Noto's. The same ash makes the soil that grows the Bronte pistachio on bare lava, the Etna lemon, the blood orange that colours only where the nights turn cold, and the Nerello Mascalese vines — some of them ungrafted and older than anyone's grandparents — that are picked a thousand metres up at the end of October, among the last grapes harvested in Italy.

The second thing is the sea, and it is not scenery. The Greeks came here by water: Corinthians founded Siracusa on the island of Ortigia in 734–733 BC because it had a great harbour and a freshwater spring a few steps from the sea, and the spring is still there, with papyrus growing wild in it. In 413 BC the Athenian fleet was trapped and destroyed inside that harbour. Nelson watered his ships at the same spring in July 1798 on his way to the Nile. The tuna came past this coast for millennia and the tonnare caught them in a labyrinth of nets — Marzamemi was built around one — while in the strait to the north the swordfish boats still hunt from a mast and a long gangway. Catania's fish market, the Pescheria, shouts every morning except Sunday under an arch that says W S. Agata.

The third is the stage. Siracusa's Greek theatre is cut into the living rock of the Temenite hill, and every spring since 1914 the INDA foundation has played Greek tragedy in it, to an audience of thousands, at sunset; the 2027 season opens on 7 May. Taormina's theatre was laid out so that the bay and the volcano are the backdrop. The puppet theatre, the Opera dei Pupi, still fights the paladins of Charlemagne in armour beaten by hand in Ortigia and Catania. On this coast even the saints are theatre: Catania fills with hundreds of thousands of people for Sant'Agata in February, and on 13 December Siracusa still keeps the memory of a famine at its table.

Everything on these pages follows from those three. The food is layered — Greek, Arab, Norman, Spanish, monastic — and Sicilians will tell you which layer each dish belongs to, often wrongly and always with conviction. The grand tables are serious and recent: fourteen MICHELIN-starred kitchens sit between Ragusa and Taormina in the 2026 guide, most of them cooking the island's own produce with a precision the old trattorie never needed. But the coast is at its best in the small rooms and at the counters — the arancino eaten standing, the granita at seven, the plate of raw red prawns two streets from the market — and in exactly this season, October, when the summer crowd has gone, the harvest is on the mountain, and Zafferana fills every Sunday with honey, apples and chestnuts.
