Ostrava is the city where the machine of the industrial age stopped and was left open — you can walk inside the blast furnace, go down the shaft with the men who worked it, and climb a spoil heap that is still hot inside. The soul of Ostrava — the essay
A café on a blast furnace, a border town of three languages and one greeting, and the city's own beer in its last year at home
THE ESSAY

Late afternoon on furnace no. 1, a coffee in your hand 71 metres up, and below you a works that has made no iron since 1998. Ostrava does not hide what made it: it lets you sit on top of it.

The city is built on what was underneath it. Coal was found in the Burňa valley in 1763, and the archbishop of Olomouc founded the Vítkovice ironworks in 1828. Over two centuries Ostrava mined, coked and smelted, and the ground sank under it. The find, the furnaces and the last cart are told where they happened, under the ironworks and the mine.

It is a border town that grew three languages. The Ostravice divided Moravia from Silesia, the bishop's town from Polish Ostrava on the far bank, and when coal and iron pulled in workers in the nineteenth century, the city's own history records that the share of German- and Polish-speaking inhabitants rose sharply. The border stayed in the mouth: the Silesian dialects of this corner put the stress on the second-to-last syllable and keep the vowels short, as Polish does, where Prague Czech leans on the first. Listen for it at a bus stop.

Zdař Bůh!The miner's greeting — God grant success

What was left was kept, and the city learned to laugh at itself. The furnace became a café, the gas holder a concert hall, a mine was left as the shift went home, and in 2008 Ostrava put three exclamation marks after its own name and let the rest of the country make of it what it would.

Then the table. Ostrava eats Czech — svíčková on the weekday lunch menu, smoked tongue with horseradish, St Martin's goose in November — and drinks its own lager for one more year: Ostravar leaves for Prague in 2027. South of the city the Beskydy rise, with sour cabbage kyselica on the ridge, the region's two MICHELIN tables in Čeladná, and Štramberk's ears of honey and clove. East, over the Olza, Polish Cieszyn bakes the Silesian wedding cake.

Come in October, when the furnace tours are quiet and the goose is coming; or on 4 December, St Barbara's day, when the coalfield remembers its miners.

From the coal hearth to the red guide
A mammoth hunters' fire of coal, soup and cake from the hills, a café for miners — and the stars that came late
23,000 years

Ostrava's food history is short on cookbooks and long on fuel, and most of its stories are told where you will eat them. This is the thread that ties them together, kept to what can be sourced.

The first hearth burned coal. On Landek hill, a mammoth hunters' camp of about 23,000 to 21,000 years ago, found in 1923 and dug on and off until the 1990s, has given up the oldest known evidence of people burning black coal, picked from seams that came to the surface. The same camp gave up the Petřkovice Venus in 1953: a woman's torso 48 mm high, carved from hematite. Ostrava cooked on coal before it was a city.

The hills sent down the sour and the sweet. Wallachia, an hour south, sent kyselica, the cabbage soup every family argues over; Štramberk the gingerbread ear; Silesia across the Olza the kołocz, a wedding cake that ended up in an EU court. Each has its story in La liste and the Larder.

The works drank. The city's lager, brewed here since 1898, leaves for Prague in 2027; the miners had a café in their own house; and a cola made to order for the state ended up with its head office in Poruba. La liste, the coffee and the lunch counters tell each one.

The incomers changed the counter. Vietnamese families from the 1950s on, Koreans with the car plant at Nošovice, pelmeni and vareniky on a Moravian street: each lane of the Tables carries its own story.

And the red guide arrived late. MICHELIN revealed its first nationwide Czech selection on 11 December 2025 at Mariánské Lázně: nine starred restaurants, 18 Bib Gourmands and four Green Stars, where before it had covered Prague only. Ostrava has no entry. The region's two are in Čeladná, under the Beskydy: MIURA, a Bib Gourmand on the first floor of a futuristic hotel, and Cattaleya, a round glass pavilion in the woods.

Moravská Ostravathe centreThe bishop's town.

Moravian Ostrava was the bishop's town on the Moravian bank, while Polish (later Silesian) Ostrava lay across the river under Silesian lords. Greater Ostrava was created on 1 January 1924 by merging seven Moravian communities, including Moravská Ostrava, Přívoz, Mariánské Hory and Vítkovice, and the interwar boom filled the centre with department stores, banks and offices. This is where the Old and New Town Halls, the cathedral, the House of Art, PLATO and Stodolní all lie within a short walk.

Further out

A planned socialist town, the station quarter and the devil's hamlet.

Porubathe new townThe socialist-realist new town.

Nová Ostrava was begun in 1952 and its first residents moved in in 1953. The main axis, Hlavní třída, was Lenin Street, and locals still call it 'Leninka'; its models were the Champs-Élysées, Tverskaya in Moscow and Nevsky Prospekt. On the façades, red stars replace coats of arms and workers and farmers replace the gods. The Oblouk (Arch), by Evžen Šteflíček, was modelled on Palace Square in St Petersburg and is sickle-shaped from above; its tower was meant to overshadow the church of St Nicholas. The zone has been protected since 2003, and the Arch was restored in 2021-2022.

Two protected names

What the region protects by EU law.

Štramberské uši: the gingerbread earProtected bakeA honey-spice cone that remembers severed ears; the town owns the name and sued a baker over it in 2008.

The legend: on 8 May 1241 Tatar raiders camped below Kotouč hill, a cloudburst (in other tellings, a broken fishpond dam) drowned them, and their sacks were found full of salted Christian ears meant for the khan. The record is later: baking began after 1660, when the Jesuits founded a pilgrimage to Kotouč and pilgrims at the Stations of the Cross were offered the sweet. Why Ostrava was called the steel heart of the republicHistoryA town of 2,000 speaking Czech, German and Polish became the empire's iron town, then the steel heart.

Moravian Ostrava had about 2,000 inhabitants in 1830 and more than 13,000 fifty years later. The hinge was 1847, when the Ferdinand Northern Railway reached Svinov and Přívoz and coal and iron could ride to Vienna. Read the city in layers: the Habsburg works at Vítkovice, the nations' houses in the centre, the postwar works at Kunčice and Poruba built to house them.

How it began

The stones that caught fireWho found the coalA smith's men built a noon fire of stones from a stump hole, and the stones caught. Or so the town tells it.

The city's own history puts it plainly: coal was found in 1763 in the Burňa valley of Polish Ostrava, today Slezská Ostrava. In 1767 the mining expert Jan Jakub Lutz confirmed the seam was worth working, and only in 1787 did the landowner, Count František Josef Wilczek, begin regular mining, twenty-four years after the find. The town tells it as a lunch break. The blacksmith Jan Keltička, who worked for the count and farmed a plot in the valley, had men grubbing out a stump; at noon they built a hearth from the stones they had dug, to warm their food, and the stones began to burn. Documents confirm Keltička existed; the burning hearth is unproven. His forge house, rebuilt in 1866, is a private museum on the street that carries his name.

★ The old hand's point: An outcrop is not a mine: the finder gets the story, the man who owns the land gets the coal.

The archbishop, the banker and the furnace of 1836Coke and capitalAn emperor's brother founded the works; the banker who wanted it was barred by law from owning land.

Ostrava's iron was begun by a churchman. Rudolf Jan of Habsburg, Archbishop of Olomouc and brother of Emperor Francis I, ordered an ironworks at Vítkovice; its charter is dated 9 December 1828, and the puddling works opened in September 1830 as Rudolfova huť. The metallurgist Franz Xaver Riepl drew the scheme. The archbishop died in 1831 and his heirs put the works up for sale. The obvious buyer was the Viennese banker Salomon Mayer Rothschild, but a Jew could not then own landed property. The Rothschilds leased the works from 1835 and only in 1843 won the exemption to buy. In 1836 Vítkovice lit what it records as the first coke furnace in the Habsburg Monarchy: iron smelted with Ostrava coal, not charcoal.

★ The old hand's point: Coke was the hinge. Charcoal iron is limited by the forest; coke iron by the pit. Once a furnace burned coke, the ironworks had to stand on the coalfield, and a village became a steel city.

Three disasters, told with their numbers.

Karviná, 14 June 1894Karviná 1894The first blast killed the shift. The second, half an hour later, killed 60 of the men sent to save them.

About 1,090 men had gone down on the afternoon shift. At about 21:30 on 14 June 1894 firedamp exploded in the Františka pit at Karviná, one of the collieries of Count Heinrich Larisch-Mönnich, hard enough to throw smoke and dust out at the surface, and fire ran through the connected Jan-Karel and Hlubina workings. At about 22:00 a second blast killed 60 of the rescue party. They had gone in with cloths soaked in vinegar held over their faces; there was no breathing apparatus. 235 men died, about 80 per cent of them suffocated, leaving 130 widows and 194 orphans. The fire was still burning on the second day; the first recovered dead were buried on 17 June. Investigators named no culprit. A social-democratic paper later called the count an 'old vampire' who drained thousands of miners.

★ The old hand's point: Most of the dead suffocated, and the rescuers who went in without their own air became the second list. A rescuer without breathing apparatus is one more casualty: modern mine rescue was written in graves like these.

Dukla, 7 July 1961Dukla 1961A belt ran empty until it caught fire. Six hours later, the commission sealed the seam with the men inside.

Near the end of the morning shift on 7 July 1961, at the Dukla mine in Dolní Suchá by Havířov, a miner accidentally set off the start mechanism of a conveyor in the 11th seam, third level. The rubber belt ran empty and unwatched until friction set it alight and the timber supports caught. The afternoon shift, 338 men, went down while it burned. Around 15:30 men on the second level smelled burning rubber; nobody reported it. Shortly after 17:00 the dispatcher heard of thick smoke and called the rescue brigade. About 700 rescuers worked in heat and near-zero visibility, finding only suffocated men. At 23:10 the commission decided to seal the area; methane could explode. Seven dams went up from more than 30,000 sandbags, and nitrogen was pumped in for over a month. 108 miners died, the youngest not yet 17, the oldest 56.

★ The old hand's point: Every disaster has a quiet hour: here, the ninety minutes between a smell nobody reported and the dispatcher's call. The hardest order underground is the one that closes the door on men who may still be alive, so the fire and gas do not take everyone.

Stonava, 20 December 2018Stonava 2018Five days before Christmas, methane at 4.5 times the permitted limit, 800 metres down: thirteen dead.

On 20 December 2018 methane exploded at the ČSM mine in Stonava, near Karviná, some 800 to 880 metres below the surface. Thirteen miners died and ten were injured. OKD first said eleven Poles and two Czechs had died; one of the 'Czechs' turned out to be Polish, so twelve Poles and one Czech, men from both sides of a border that runs through the same coal seam. The Czech Mining Authority found methane over the permitted concentration more than 4.5 times; the spark that set it off was still unresolved two years later. The last four bodies came up on 13 May 2019. Every year since, Stonava's uniformed miners' society, founded more than a century ago, has kept St Barbara's feast in the village's Polish cultural house, the Dům PZKO: a tombola, a band, cakes and, as one guest put it, 'now and then a good little shot'.

★ The old hand's point: Firedamp is invisible until it is fatal, which is why a gassy mine lives by its instruments and its fans. What is left

Mine MichalDůl MichalEach miner hoisted his clothes to the ceiling on a chain. The ones still hanging were the men still below.

In 1842 the state mining authority marked out a concession at Michálkovice, and in 1843 two shafts were begun. The first, Ferdinand, closed in the 1880s; the second was named Michal after the court councillor Michael Layer. From 1856 the Ferdinand Northern Railway owned it, until nationalisation in 1945; in 1946 it was renamed after the Social Democrat Petr Cingr. In 1913-1915 it was completely rebuilt to designs by František Fiala, one of the most modern electrified collieries of its day. Coal stopped on 30 May 1993, and then, unusually, nothing was cleared away: the chain changing room, where each man hauled his clothes to the ceiling on a chain and padlock, the washrooms, the engine houses. Before a shift a miner ate what he could at home; underground, the cookbook of the coalfield says, a slice of bread often had to last twelve hours.

★ The old hand's point: The chain room is a muster list in iron: what went down the shaft at the start of a shift had to come back up at the end, and the clothes left hanging told you who had not.

How to read a Czech tapHow-toTankové, nefiltrované, dvanáctka: three words on the board that tell you what is in the glass.

'Tankové pivo' is unpasteurised lager delivered by chilled tanker and served from a tank, not a keg; the brewery has about 48 hours to get it to the pub. Look for 'tankovna' on the door or the tanks behind the bar. 'Nefiltrované' means unfiltered, hazy and yeastier on the tongue. The degrees on the board, 10, 11, 12, measure the strength of the wort before fermentation, not the alcohol; a dvanáctka (12°) is the fuller-bodied lager, a desítka the lighter session beer. In Ostrava, multitaps such as Kurník šopa pour unfiltered, unpasteurised beer from small breweries.

Read the degrees as body, not strength, and order accordingly.

Šipka cave and the Neanderthal childPrehistoryA cave mouth on Kotouč hill where, in 1880, a Neanderthal child's jaw came out of its own hearth layer.

Karel Maška dug at Šipka from 1879 to 1893. In 1880, about 12 metres in from the entrance and in the oldest layer, he found the lower jaw of a Neanderthal child of nine or ten, lying among Mousterian stone tools, hearths and the bones of the animals they ate. It is often described as the first Neanderthal remain found in its cultural context, only 24 years after the Neander valley discovery. The original jaw was lost when Mikulov castle burned in 1945. What survives is the cave itself, a pale limestone mouth you can stand in, and in town a scaled-down replica of it.

Stand in the shelter where a child's jaw put Neanderthals beside their own fires, then see the replica in town.

Leoš Janáček was born on 3 July 1854 in the Hukvaldy school, where his father taught, and left at twelve for Brno. Above the village you climb through a game preserve of 1566, past the bronze vixen Bystrouška, to a castle ruin founded around 1230, counted among the largest in Europe.

Pustevny to Radhošť: the ridge of the godBeskydy walkJurkovič's painted timber lodges, rebuilt after a fire, then a ridge path to a horned god.

Pustevny takes its name from hermits whose hermitage closed in 1784. Lysá hora: the bald mountainHighest peakThe roof of the Moravian-Silesian Beskydy, where 491 cm of snow was measured in 1911.

Lysá hora, 1,323 metres (newer surveys say 1,324), is the highest peak of the Moravian-Silesian Beskydy; lysá means bald, for its treeless top. It catches around 1,500 mm of precipitation a year and in 1911 recorded the deepest snow ever measured in the Czech lands, 491 cm. The summit carries a weather station, a transmitter and huts. Bezruč's hut burned in 1978; the Czech Tourist Club rebuilt it with a public collection and reopened it on 25 November 2015. In the dry summer of 2026 the summit's borehole ran short: the Emil Zátopek hut served soup in paper bowls to save washing up, and Bezruč's hut trucked in drinking water.

Karviná: the church that leansCoal countryThe last building of old Karviná, freshly re-roofed in grey, white and red; tours run to the end of October.

Český Těšín and Cieszyn: one town, two countriesBorderSplit down the Olza river in 1920; walk over the Friendship Bridge to a Habsburg castle brewery.

On 28 July 1920 the Conference of Ambassadors divided the disputed Těšín region along the Olza, Olše in Czech. The old town on the east bank stayed Polish as Cieszyn; the western suburbs, with the railway station, became Czechoslovak Český Těšín. The town lived under one flag again from 1938 to 1945, then was split once more. The Friendship Bridge joins the halves, and boards mark the stops of the tram that once crossed it. Over the bridge, below the castle hill, stands Browar Zamkowy, the castle brewery of the Habsburg dukes of Teschen, founded in 1846 to plans by the Viennese architect Joseph Kornhäusel; the town council says it has brewed through every border change since.

Follow the vanished tram's boards into Poland, and drink at the brewery that outlasted every border.

Opava's Silesian Museum traces itself to 1 May 1814 and the Gymnasium Museum in the town's grammar school, which would make it the oldest museum in the Czech Republic, four years ahead of Prague's National Museum. Either way, the building on Komenského holds a serious collection of Silesian natural and human history.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The stories behind the canon, and where they are argued rather than settled.

#1
DISH

Kyselica

Wallachia's white kyselica is a sour cabbage soup that eats like a main course. In the version Forbes printed from a Rožnov family, onion and smoked sausage — the writer used ostravská, the Ostrava sausage — fry in lard; potatoes, bay, allspice, peppercorns and caraway follow with stock and up to 800 g of sauerkraut; cream whisked with a little flour goes in at the end, and the kraut's own brine or a pinch of sugar sets the balance between sour and sweet. Cook a double batch: it is best the next day. On the ridge, ČTK reported it on the menu at Libušín, Jurkovič's rebuilt dining lodge at Pustevny.

#2
DISH

Ostravar, 1898–2027

Ostravar's brewery records its first mention in 1897 and its first brew in 1898, and still ferments in open vats in the spilka, the fermenting hall, before many weeks in cold, dark lagering cellars. On 8 October 2026 its owner, Pivovary Staropramen, part of Molson Coors, announced that brewing will leave Ostrava in stages during 2027 for the Smíchov brewery in Prague; about 120 production jobs go, while sales, storage and distribution stay in the region. Ostravar Original, the Mustang 11° and 12° and the dark Černá Barbora go on — brewed in Prague. The brewery's own line, 'every half-litre reflects the real Ostrava', has a year left to be literally true.
WhereAny Ostrava pub this winter — the brewery has no public tap · Maps →
#3
DISH

Kołocz across the border

Kołocz śląski is the Silesian wedding cake: a rectangle of yeast dough about 3.5 cm high, plain or filled with curd cheese, poppy seed or apple, under a thick, buttery crumble; elsewhere in Poland kołacze are round. In Cieszyn Silesia people still carry wedding kołocz round to the invited guests. The EU protected the name for Poland in July 2011, and then the argument began: the German text of the register read 'Schlesischer Streuselkuchen', German bakers feared for their own Silesian crumble cake, and their trade association went to the EU General Court, which ruled on 7 October 2015 that the German wording was an editorial error. Cross the Olza to Cieszyn for it.
WhereA bakery in Cieszyn, over the Olza from Český Těšín · Maps →
