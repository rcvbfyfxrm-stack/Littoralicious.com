The soul of Ostrava — the essay

THE ESSAY

It is five in the afternoon on furnace no. 1 and the coffee comes with a view of the whole works: the gas holder that is now a concert hall, the coke batteries, the power plant, and on a clear day the Beskydy on the horizon. Below, the grated walkway you came up on is seventy metres above the ground. Nobody has made iron here since 1998.

The city is built on what was underneath it. Coal was found in the Burňa valley in 1763 — the legend says labourers lit a fire of stones and the stones burned — and an archbishop founded the ironworks at Vítkovice in 1828. Salomon Rothschild, who could not own land as a Jew, leased the works from 1835 and bought them in 1843. For a century and a half the city mined, coked and smelted; the ground sank under it — the Silesian castle sits sixteen metres lower than it was built — and Halda Ema, the heap of a shaft named after a countess, is still burning inside. The last coal from Ostrava itself came up the Odra shaft on 30 June 1994. The last from the whole country came up on 4 February 2026.

What was left was kept. Dolní Vítkovice became the city's living room: a café on the furnace, a concert hall in the gas holder, Colours of Ostrava among the chimneys every July. Důl Michal was shut in 1993 and left as if the shift had just gone home, chains and all. Landek, where mammoth hunters burned coal from the surface seams more than twenty thousand years ago, is the mining museum, and its guides went down the shaft for a living.

Then the table. Ostrava eats Czech — svíčková on the weekday lunch menu, smoked tongue with horseradish, the St Martin's goose in November — and drinks its own lager, Ostravar, brewed here since 1898. Not for much longer: on 8 October 2026 the owner announced that brewing moves to Prague during 2027. South of the city the Beskydy rise in an hour, with the region's two MICHELIN tables in Čeladná, and Štramberk, where a dozen licensed bakers still roll honey dough into gingerbread ears for a legend of 1241.

Come in October, when the furnace tours are quiet and the goose is coming; or on 4 December, St Barbara's day, when the coalfield remembers its miners.

From the coal hearth to the red guide
A mammoth hunters' fire of coal, five centuries of chateau beer, the gingerbread ear, phở on the main square — and the stars that came late
23,000 years

Ostrava's food history is short on cookbooks and long on fuel. What follows keeps to what can be sourced, and says so where the region's tables are thinner than its stories.

The first hearth burned coal. On Landek hill, excavators in the 1950s found coal and coke in the hearths of a mammoth hunters' camp — the oldest known evidence of people burning black coal. A 2008 monograph dates the camp to 21,000–23,000 radiocarbon years. The same camp gave up the Petřkovice Venus, 4.5 cm of hematite, in 1953.

Beer is the old continuity. Zámek Zábřeh says beer was brewed there more than 500 years ago, recorded in an urbarium of the Olomouc bishopric, and it brews again since 2007. Ostravar, the city's lager, has been brewed here since 1898. Radegast has brewed at Nošovice under the Beskydy since 1970.

The hills fed the city its sweet and its sour. In Štramberk the gingerbread ears are tied to the Jesuit pilgrimage to Kotouč after 1660 and to a legend of the Mongols in 1241; the name has been EU-protected since 2007 and a guild licenses about a dozen bakers. Wallachia, south again, gave the frgál, a broad thin tart with one filling, EU-protected since 2013, and kyselica, the sauerkraut soup that was a woodcutter's breakfast.

The incomers changed the counter. Czechoslovakia trained North Vietnamese students and workers from the 1950s; many stayed after 1989, and by the 2000s phở had become the Czech default bowl. The Hyundai plant brought a Korean community, and with it kimbap for about 100 CZK. Kofola, still headquartered in Ostrava, bought 52 % of the city's own coffee chain, CØKAFE, in August 2026.

And the red guide arrived late. MICHELIN published its first nationwide Czech selection in 2025. Ostrava has no entry; the region's two — MIURA, a Bib Gourmand, and Cattaleya — are in Čeladná, cooking venison, gingerbread and slivovice into the menu. The new results are expected in late November 2026.

Halda Ema: climb the hill that is still burningSmouldering heapA hill of 28 million tonnes of mine waste, still burning inside: steam and warm ground near the top.

Ema grew beside a coal shaft opened in 1861 by Count Wilczek, who named it after his wife, Countess Emma. It was built mainly from the waste of the Trojice mine; mining at the site ended in 1933 and the heap has been protected since 1995. The tailings still burn below the surface, up to 1,500 °C inside according to the city, and that heat has made rare porcelanite and jasper. Near the summit puffs of gas rise, snow seldom lies, and plants and insects from warmer climates survive. At roughly 314 m it is the city's third-highest point, with views to the Beskydy, the Jeseníky and Poland.

Go up at sunset, as the locals do, and look for the spots where the snow or frost has not settled: that is the fire underneath.

Every story is sourced, with the arguments left in. Each ends with what an old hand would want you to understand — and where you can still stand and see it. For a sailor most of them are already familiar: a closed space, a gas you cannot smell, a watch that cannot be left.

How it began

The stones that caught fireWho found the coalA smith's men built a noon fire of stones from a stump hole, and the stones caught. Or so the town tells it.

The city's own history puts it plainly: coal was found in 1763 in the Burňa valley of Polish Ostrava, today Slezská Ostrava. In 1767 the mining expert Jan Jakub Lutz confirmed the seam was worth working, and in 1787 the landowner, Count František Josef Wilczek, began regular mining, twenty-four years after the find. The town tells it differently. The blacksmith Jan Keltička, who worked for the count and farmed a plot in the valley, had men grubbing out a stump; at noon they built a hearth from the stones they had dug, and the stones began to burn. Documents confirm Keltička existed; the burning hearth is unproven. His forge house, rebuilt in 1866, is now a private museum, slowly sinking into the ground the coal came out of.

★ The old hand's point: An outcrop is not a mine: the finder gets the story, the man who owns the land gets the coal.

Touch it today
Muzeum Keltičkova kovárna, Keltičkova 136/6, Slezská Ostrava: a private family museum (founded 2000) with a memorial to the discovery in the garden; visits by arrangement with the owners, groups up to twelve, voluntary entry fee. The Burňa valley lies below Halda Ema.
Where the sources argue
The city and ČTK give 1763 without naming a finder; the Keltička story is a legend (told by Czech Radio and the forge museum; documents confirm only that he lived and worked here). The CzechTourism listing tells it under 1776. A version naming a miller 'Jan Augustin' of Klimkovice circulates online with no primary source we could find. Andrea Pokludová has published a study of the legend's cultural background.

The archbishop, the banker and the furnace of 1836Coke and capitalAn emperor's brother founded the works; the banker who wanted it was barred by law from owning land.

Ostrava's iron was begun by a churchman. Rudolf Jan of Habsburg, Archbishop of Olomouc and brother of Emperor Francis I, ordered an ironworks at Vítkovice; its charter is dated 9 December 1828, and the puddling works opened in September 1830 as Rudolfova huť. The metallurgist Franz Xaver Riepl drew the scheme. The archbishop died in 1831 and his heirs put the works up for sale. The obvious buyer was the Viennese banker Salomon Mayer Rothschild, but a Jew could not then own landed property. The Rothschilds leased the works from 1835 and only in 1843 won the exemption to buy. In 1836 Vítkovice lit what it records as the first coke-fired furnace in the Habsburg Monarchy: iron smelted with Ostrava coal, not charcoal.

★ The old hand's point: Coke was the hinge. Charcoal iron is limited by the forest; coke iron by the pit. Once a furnace burned coke, the ironworks had to stand on the coalfield, and a village became a steel city.

Touch it today
Dolní Vítkovice, national cultural monument since 2002: blast furnace no. 1 and Bolt Tower. The Anselm pit at Landek Park, Ostrava-Petřkovice, opened in 1835 and named after a Rothschild son, fed the furnaces.
Where the sources argue
Radio Prague says the archbishop commissioned the furnaces in 1826; the city and e15 give 1828 (e15: charter of 9 December 1828). Dolní Vítkovice calls the 1836 event 'the first coke furnace ignited in the Habsburg Monarchy'; other accounts loosely call it Vítkovice's first blast furnace. The often-repeated claim of the first puddling works in Austria was not confirmed in what we read.

When the furnaces went coldLast tappingVítkovice tapped its last iron in 1998. In 2023 the city's other big furnace went cold, maybe for good.

For 162 years the Vítkovice blast furnaces were tapped. The last tapping came in September 1998. In about 160 years they had made some 90 million tonnes of pig iron; in 1977 furnace no. 1 alone made a record 1.7 million tonnes. A quarter-century later the city's other giant followed. The postwar Nová huť works, later ArcelorMittal Ostrava, then Liberty Ostrava, hot-idled its only working blast furnace from 23 October 2023; its energy supplier cut supplies from 21 December 2023 and production stopped. The coke plant shut in 2024. On 1 October 2025 the bankrupt plant passed to a company controlled by former interior minister Martin Pecina and became Nová Huť. It now only rolls imported slabs. Its director said restarting the one furnace that could in theory be relit 'would cost more than building a completely new one'.

★ The old hand's point: A blast furnace is not simply switched off: it is banked hot, which only works for days, or blown down and emptied. Once it has gone truly cold, restarting is a costly operation in its own right, which is why Ostrava's director talks of building new.

Touch it today
Dolní Vítkovice: walk the path the ore took up blast furnace no. 1. The idle Nová Huť furnaces stand in Ostrava-Kunčice, visible from outside the works, not open to visitors.
Where the sources argue
The day of the last Vítkovice tapping: Wikipedia and Komoot give 27 September 1998; Dolní Vítkovice gives only the year. The planned electric arc furnace (up to 1.5 million t a year) is costed at about 17 billion CZK (Forbes/gmk) or 'roughly 20 billion' (director Radek Strouhal to e15, Oct 2025), and was not yet financed in the latest reports read (Jan 2026).

The price

Three disasters, told with their numbers.

Karviná, 14 June 1894Karviná 1894The first blast killed the shift. The second, half an hour later, killed 60 of the men sent to save them.

On the evening of 14 June 1894 firedamp exploded in the Františka pit at Karviná, one of the collieries of Count Heinrich Larisch-Mönnich, and fire and blasts ran through the connected Jan-Karel and Hlubina workings. Seznam Zprávy puts the first blast at about 21:30 and a second at about 22:00, which killed 60 of the rescue party; they had gone in with vinegar-soaked cloths over their faces, having no breathing apparatus. 235 men died, about 80 per cent of them suffocated, leaving 130 widows and 194 orphans. Fifty-four were never brought out. Investigators named no culprit. The social-democratic press called the count 'the Těšín vampire'. The disaster pushed the region towards proper rescue breathing gear; Karin Lednická's trilogy Šikmý kostel opens in this year.

★ The old hand's point: Most of the dead suffocated, and the rescuers who went in without their own air became the second list. A rescuer without breathing apparatus is one more casualty: modern mine rescue was written in graves like these.

Touch it today
The Chapel of the Sacred Heart of Jesus over the former Františka mine in Karviná-Doly, reached by a maple alley; the common grave and monument with the victims' names in the old Karviná-Doly cemetery. Landek Park shows breathing apparatus from 1884.
Where the sources argue
Timing varies: Seznam Zprávy gives 21:30 and 22:00; Deník 'half past nine', with a third blast at 7 the next morning; CNN Prima 'around 10 p.m.'; one Czech calendar gives 15 June. Bodies left below: 54 (CNN Prima, 130th anniversary) or 123 (one Czech source). Methane is generally accepted as the cause; one source suggests shot-firing as the trigger; the inquiry named no culprit.

Dukla, 7 July 1961Dukla 1961A belt ran empty until it caught fire. Six hours later, the commission sealed the seam with the men inside.

Near the end of the morning shift on 7 July 1961, at the Dukla mine in Dolní Suchá by Havířov, a passing miner knocked the start mechanism of a conveyor. The rubber belt ran empty and unwatched in the 11th seam until friction set it alight and the timber supports caught. The afternoon shift, 338 men, went down while it burned. Around 15:30 a burning smell was noticed on the second level; nobody reported it. Shortly after 17:00 the dispatcher heard of thick smoke and called the rescue brigade. About 700 rescuers worked in heat and near-zero visibility, finding only suffocated men. At 23:10 the commission decided to seal the area; methane could explode. Seven dams were built from over 30,000 sandbags and nitrogen pumped in for over a month. 108 miners died; the youngest was not yet 17.

★ The old hand's point: Every disaster has a quiet hour: here, the ninety minutes between a smell nobody reported and the dispatcher's call. The hardest order underground is the one that closes the door on men who may still be alive, so the fire and gas do not take everyone.

Touch it today
The Dukla memorial with all 108 names, moved from the old mine site (now an industrial zone) to the Šumbark cemetery in Havířov, with a wooden statue of St Barbara; the commemoration is held there each July. David Ondříček's 2018 film Dukla 61 won a Czech Lion.
Where the sources argue
Which seam held the victims: oEnergetice says the fire began in the 11th seam (never reached by rescuers) and the dead were found in the 8th; Wikipedia says the victims were from the 8th seam. Communist-era reporting of the disaster was not examined in the sources read.

Stonava, 20 December 2018Stonava 2018Five days before Christmas, methane at 4.5 times the permitted limit, 800 metres down: thirteen dead.

On 20 December 2018 methane exploded at the ČSM mine in Stonava, near Karviná, some 800 to 880 metres below the surface. Thirteen miners died and ten were injured. OKD first said eleven Poles and two Czechs had died; one of the 'Czechs' turned out to be Polish, so twelve Poles and one Czech, men from both sides of a border that runs through the same coal seam. The Czech Mining Authority found methane over the permitted concentration more than 4.5 times; the spark that set it off was still unresolved two years later. The last four bodies were brought up on 13 May 2019. It was the worst Czech mining accident since 1990, when 30 miners died in a fire near Karviná. Seven years on, ČSM was the mine that raised the last Czech coal.

★ The old hand's point: Firedamp is invisible until it is fatal, which is why a gassy mine lives by its instruments and its fans. Methane is explosive between about 5 and 15 per cent in air, per NIOSH; ventilation exists to keep the face far below that band. At 4.5 times the limit, the only open question is which spark.

Touch it today
The memorial obelisk in Stonava, unveiled 20 December 2019; the ČSM headframes, now in technical closure, seen from the road.
Where the sources argue
The nationality count changed (11 Poles and 2 Czechs at first, then 12 and 1). Depth is given as about 800 m (Radio Prague) or 880 m (OKD). The ignition source was never publicly established.

What is left

Mine MichalDůl MichalEach miner hoisted his clothes to the ceiling on a chain. The ones still hanging were the men still below.

In 1843 the Austrian state mining fund opened pit no. 3 at Michálkovice. It was later named after the imperial adviser Michael Laier, owned from 1856 by the Ferdinand Northern Railway until nationalisation in 1945, and renamed after the left-wing leader Petr Cingr in 1946. In 1912-1915 it was rebuilt to designs by František Fiala, a pupil of Otto Wagner's school, as one of the most modern fully electrified collieries of its day. In June 1993 it was shut, and then, unusually, nothing was cleared away: the chain changing room where each man hoisted his clothes to the ceiling on a chain and padlock, the washrooms, the engine houses. It became a national cultural monument in 1995 and opened to the public in April 2000. ERIH records that Michal's foremen used canaries to check the air before they allowed blasting.

★ The old hand's point: The chain room is a muster list in iron: what went down the shaft at the start of a shift had to come back up at the end, and the clothes left hanging told you who had not.

Touch it today
Důl Michal, Ostrava-Michálkovice (NPÚ): the basic tour passes through the chain room, washrooms and engine houses. Open in October 2026 at weekends and daily 28 Oct-1 Nov; 1 Nov is the last published day.
Where the sources argue
No dispute on the main dates. The canary detail rests on ERIH's description of the site; we found no NPÚ page that repeats it.

The church that sank 37 metresŠikmý kostelA Baroque church in Karviná has sunk about 37 metres and leans seven degrees. Mass is still said every Sunday.

The Baroque church of St Peter of Alcantara was built in 1736 on the site of a wooden church recorded in 1447, on a low hill in the mining town of Karvinná. Mining began about a century later, and the ground under it fell. Czech Television reports it has sunk about 37 metres and leans about seven degrees to the south. Its tower was lowered in the 1960s because of the tilt and can never be rebuilt to full height; the parish technician says the church would fall. In the early 1990s it faced collapse, and a reinforced-concrete ring was cast around it. The town around it is gone: houses, mining colonies, the town hall, the Larisch family's chateau and the school were swallowed by subsidence. In July 2026 a seven-million-crown restoration was completed, with more than 20,000 clay tiles.

★ The old hand's point: Coal taken from below is paid for above. Longwall mining lets the roof behind the face collapse on purpose, and the surface follows it down years later, so a mining town sinks on someone else's schedule.

Touch it today
Kostel sv. Petra z Alkantary, Karviná-Doly: Sunday Mass; guided tours Friday to Sunday until the end of October 2026 (per ČT24). You feel the slope the moment you step inside.
Where the sources argue
The tilt is 6.8 degrees (tourism and university sources) or 'about 7' (ČT24); subsidence 37 m (ČT24, Czech Radio) or 36 m (one tourism listing); Seznam Zprávy says it once stood 'about 40 metres higher'. No primary geodetic report was read; a VŠB-TUO thesis on the church's measured movement exists.

The last cartThe last cartOstrava raised its last coal in 1994. The country raised its last cart from 1,300 metres on 4 February 2026.

On 30 June 1994 the last coal car came up the Odra shaft in Přívoz, the former František mine, ending more than two centuries of mining in Ostrava itself. Mining moved east into the Karviná part of the basin and lasted thirty-two more years. OKD employed about 98,000 people in 1990, about 7,000 at the end of 2020, and some 2,300 near the end. The machines in the final longwall at the ČSM mine in Stonava were scheduled to stop on the night shift of 31 January 2026. On 4 February 2026 miners brought the last cart up from 1,300 metres; in Ostrava, Bishop Martin David said a service for the miners, with the Permoník Karviná choir. ČSM alone had raised almost 124.3 million tonnes. The last coal-preparation plant closed on 28 February.

★ The old hand's point: A pit is not closed when the coal stops; it is closed when it is safe to walk away. Shafts must be filled, gas drained and water managed for years after the last cart.

Touch it today
Landek Park and Důl Michal tell the Ostrava end; the ČSM complex in Stonava is in technical closure, expected to take about three years, and not open. The Odra site in Přívoz is a former mine, not a museum.
Where the sources argue
'The end' has two dates: 31 January 2026 (last mining shift, per OKD/oEnergetice) and 4 February 2026 (the symbolic last cart). OKD's retained workforce is given as about 600 (oEnergetice) or nearly 700 (ČTK).

Along the Ostravice: Komenského sady and the Sýkora bridgeRiver walkYoga on the river piers, a lime alley, and a steel bridge that leads straight to the burning heap.

Behind the New Town Hall the Ostravice runs past Komenského sady, the large park where, as the official guide puts it, children cycle, students picnic and people do yoga on the river piers; a lime alley closes its far end. Upstream, the steel Miloš Sýkora bridge crosses to Slezská Ostrava and is the start of the climb up Halda Ema. Under the bridge, the Ostrava Elegant association has held a free swing-dance evening, Summer Blues, every summer since 2020, and in 2026 a new Bistro Pod Sýkorákem opened beside the Cyklobar Elegant.

The easiest hour of green in the centre, and the natural first leg of the Halda Ema walk.

Bělský les: 160 hectares of forest inside the cityForestA city forest between housing estates, with a slag-stone Lourdes chapel and a five-metre wooden giantess.

Bělský les covers 160 hectares between Stará Bělá and Výškovice. A loop from the Zábřeh tram stop follows blue local markers to Starobělské Lourdes, a pilgrimage spot with Stations of the Cross by the art blacksmith David Sekula and a chapel rebuilt in 1928 from slag stones, the waste of the ironworks turned into a sanctuary. Heřmanický rybník: fishermen's huts on mine waterPondAbout 70 fishing huts on stilts over a 116-hectare pond filled with mineral water pumped from the mines.

The Heřmanice pond is fed with strongly mineralised water containing boron and iodine, pumped from the shafts of the Ostrava-Karviná mines, beside the Heřmanice mine and its heap. Fish still thrive in it, and fishermen have built about 70 huts over the water, each reached by a narrow footbridge, many with a drawbridge they can raise behind them. Up to 250 bird species have been recorded around the pond, which the city's tourist office calls, with a straight face, the Ostrava Maldives.

Mine water, not spring water: a working landscape that turned, by accident, into one of the city's best places for birds.

The centre

New Town Hall tower: the tallest town-hall tower in the countryViewing towerA lift to 73 m inside a steel tower, built of steel because the ground was too soft for brick.

The New Town Hall opened on 28 October 1930 as the largest town-hall complex in the country. The tower was meant to be brick, but the gravel subsoil forced a ribbed concrete raft and a lighter tower, so it was built as a steel skeleton clad in copper, made at Vítkovice. It weighs 155 tonnes and rises to 85.6 m; the terrace is at 73 m, reached by a lift that was the tallest in the country when built. Four statues on the façade stand for Mining, Metallurgy, Science and Trade. Inside, a wooden paternoster from 1928 still runs between floors one and four.

Ride the 1928 paternoster on the way out: an open lift that never stops, still in daily use.

Masarykovo náměstí and the Old Town HallMain squareThe square a bishop laid out before 1279, with the city museum and an astronomical clock in the old town hall.

Moravian Ostrava is first named in the 1267 will of Bruno of Schauenburg, Bishop of Olomouc, and had town status by 1279; its core is the square now called Masarykovo náměstí. The Old Town Hall, built in the 15th century, served until the early 20th century, when the boom town outgrew it. Since 1931 it has housed the Ostrava Museum, founded in 1924 by merging three earlier museums. Its collection of about 300,000 items includes the Mašek astronomical clock and a 200-year-old orchestrion. In winter the square holds the main Christmas market.

The square is the medieval town the coal boom swallowed: start here to see what Ostrava was before the mines.

Cathedral of the Divine SaviourCathedralThe second-largest church in Moravia, built for a boom town and made a cathedral only in 1996.

The Neo-Renaissance basilica, with two 67 m towers dated 1889, was designed by Gustav Meretta, the Olomouc archbishop's architect, with interiors by Max von Ferstel. It seats 4,000, a size that only makes sense in a town that was filling with miners and steelworkers. The fresco above the main altar, by J. Stalmach, shows the Adoration of the Lamb, and the statue of the Saviour on the gable is surrounded by seven figures of the blind and the sick. When Pope John Paul II created the Ostrava-Opava diocese on 30 May 1996, the basilica became its cathedral on 1 September 1996.

Look up at the gable before you go in: the Saviour among the blind and the sick.

Silesian Ostrava Castle: the fortress that sank 16 metresCastleA 13th-century border castle where two rivers meet, pulled down about 16 metres by the mines beneath it.

The stone castle was built on the promontory above the confluence of the Lučina and the Ostravice to guard the border between the Polish and Bohemian states. Mining underneath made the old noble seat sink by about 16 metres over its life, and it was damaged in the 1944 bombing. It took its present form after a reconstruction completed in 2004. Inside are exhibitions on the castle, the town and the region; the courtyard and amphitheatre host summer Shakespeare, Jazz Open and the winter Bethlehem exhibition. Walk there from Černá louka over the footbridge and round the confluence.

Approach on foot from Černá louka: the river view is the best way to understand why a border castle stood here.

Villa Grossmann: a builder's fairy-tale house and how it endedVillaThe largest villa garden of interwar Ostrava, built by a man the Depression ruined.

František Grossmann, born in 1871 in Pustějov, became one of Ostrava's leading builders in partnership with the architect František Fiala; their firm built the Church of the Virgin Mary in Mariánské Hory. In 1922-1924 he built himself this villa in the decorative style, with a garden of exotic trees, sculptures, a gazebo, a pool and a fountain. About ten years later, caught by the economic crisis and pressed by creditors, he and his wife Otília took their own lives, and the villa went to the creditors. After the war it was a kindergarten, then a school until 2005; the city restored it for guided tours.

Walk the garden after the tour: it is the part Grossmann built for show, and it is still the largest of its kind in interwar Ostrava.

The little beautiful things

Přívoz: a square planned by Camillo Sitte, and the canariesSitte's squareA Viennese master planner drew the square, the church and the town hall, and canaries still sing beside them.

Přívoz takes its name from převoz, a ferry over the Oder. Around Svatopluk Čech Square, the Viennese architect Camillo Sitte planned the streets and designed both the Church of the Immaculate Conception and the former town hall, now the city archive; the square and its Art Nouveau houses are a heritage zone. To the right of the archive, an aviary of canaries and parakeets has stood for more than 60 years in memory of the birds miners took underground to detect gas. The official guide also records that the first European deportation of Jews to the camps left from Ostrava's main station.

Few towns have a square by Sitte, the man who taught Europe how to plan one; this one comes with a living memorial to the pits.

How to read a Czech tapHow-toTankové, nefiltrované, dvanáctka: three words on the board that tell you what is in the glass.

'Tankové pivo' is unpasteurised lager delivered by chilled tanker and served from a tank, not a keg; the brewery has about 48 hours to get it to the pub. Look for 'tankovna' on the door or a tank behind the bar. 'Nefiltrované' means unfiltered. The degrees on the board, 10, 11, 12, measure the strength of the wort before fermentation, not the alcohol; a dvanáctka (12°) is the fuller-bodied lager. In Ostrava, multitaps such as Kurník šopa pour unfiltered, unpasteurised beer from small breweries.

Read the degrees as body, not strength, and order accordingly.

Šipka cave and the Neanderthal childPrehistoryA cave mouth on Kotouč hill where, in 1880, a Neanderthal child's jaw came out of its own hearth layer.

Karel Maška dug at Šipka from 1879 to 1893. In 1880, about 12 metres in from the entrance and in the oldest layer, he found the lower jaw of a Neanderthal child of nine or ten, lying among Mousterian stone tools, hearths and animal bones. It is often described as the first Neanderthal remain found in its cultural context, only 24 years after the Neander valley discovery, and Hermann Schaaffhausen examined it that same year. The original jaw was lost when Mikulov castle burned in 1945. What survives is the cave itself, an open rock shelter you can stand in, and a replica in the museum below.

Stand in the shelter where a child's jaw put Neanderthals beside their own fires, then see the replica in town.

Kopřivnice: Tatra's two museumsEngineeringA carriage workshop of 1850 that grew into Tatra, maker of air-cooled cars and all-terrain trucks.

Next door stands the depository of the Slovenská strela, the streamlined 1930s railcar also built in this town.

Go for the foundry hall of trucks; stay for the Präsident, where the empire's motoring began.

Hukvaldy: the ruin, the deer park and the vixenJanáčekOne of Europe's largest castle ruins, reached only on foot through a deer park founded in 1566.

Hukvaldy castle was founded around 1230, and its ruins, among the largest in Europe, look out over the Beskydy and the Moravian Gate. Fulnek: where Comenius lost his libraryReformationThe Brethren's house where Comenius taught until 1620, when soldiers burned his books in the square.

Jan Amos Komenský, Comenius, the teacher of nations, led the Unity of Brethren congregation in Fulnek from 1618 to 1621. Its house, from the late 15th century, held a prayer room, a school and a garden. In 1620 Spanish soldiers sacked the town and his library and manuscripts were burned in the square; he fled, and the congregation ended with him. After the Brethren were expelled the building became a granary. The former chapel turned into a Comenius memorial in 1954, and today the house is run by the Muzeum Novojičínska as Svět Komenského, the World of Comenius.

Poodří: the Odra floodplainBirds and pondsA free-running river, oxbows and fishponds holding 22,000–25,000 waterbirds at Ostrava's door.

Poodří became a Protected Landscape Area in 1991 and is a Ramsar wetland of international importance. Pustevny to Radhošť: the ridge of the godBeskydy walkFrom Jurkovič's carved wooden lodges along the ridge to a horned god holding a cornucopia and an axe.

Pustevny takes its name from hermits, poustevníci, whose hermitage closed in 1784; the hiking club Pohorská jednota Radhošť built its first refuge here in 1891. Dušan Jurkovič designed Maměnka (1898) and the dining lodge Libušín (1899) with the builder Michal Urbánek. Lysá hora: the bald mountainHighest peakThe roof of the Moravian-Silesian Beskydy, where 491 cm of snow was measured in 1911, climbed from a station.

Lysá hora, 1,323 metres (newer surveys say 1,324), is the highest peak of the Moravian-Silesian Beskydy; lysá means bald, for its treeless top. It catches around 1,500 mm of precipitation a year, among the most in the country, and in 1911 it recorded the deepest snow ever measured in the Czech lands, 491 cm. The summit carries a weather station, a transmitter and huts: the Albrecht Habsburg refuge went up in 1880, and today there are Bezruč's hut and a hut named after the runner Emil Zátopek. The road to the top is closed to traffic, so everyone arrives on foot.

The one summit where everyone walks, and the weather is the show.

Rožnov: the Wallachian Open Air MuseumFolk architectureTimber houses carried off Rožnov's own square and the hills, and a valley of water mills, since 1925.

The museum opened in 1925 during a folklore festival called the Wallachian Year, founded by Bohumír Jaroněk. It has three parts. For the centenary in 2025, 1,449 people in folk costume gathered here, a Czech record. The same museum looks after the Jurkovič lodges at Pustevny.

Karviná: the church that leansCoal countryA 1736 church tilted 6.8 degrees by the coal mined beneath it, still holding Mass.

St Peter of Alcantara in Karviná-Doly was built in 1736, in the old town of Karviná. Coal mining beneath it lowered the ground, and the church now leans 6.8 degrees to the south, enough to see from the road and to feel underfoot. Engineers from VŠB–Technical University of Ostrava have tracked its movement with levelling and total-station surveys and report that the main phase is over and the building has stabilised. It is a protected monument and still a working church, with weekly Mass in Czech and Polish.

Stand inside and feel the floor tilt under you: the clearest picture of what mining did to this land.

Český Těšín and Cieszyn: one town, two countriesBorderSplit down the Olza river in 1920; walk over the Friendship Bridge and have lunch in Poland.

On 28 July 1920 the Conference of Ambassadors divided the disputed Těšín region along the Olza, Olše in Czech. The old town on the east bank stayed Polish as Cieszyn; the western suburbs, with the railway station and most of the region's coal mines, went to Czechoslovakia and became Český Těšín. The town lived under one flag again from 1938 to 1945, then was split once more after the war. The Friendship Bridge joins the halves today, and a cross-border project marks the route of the old tram that once crossed it, with boards at the former stops.

Opava: a museum from 1814Silesian capitalThe Silesian Museum says it is the oldest in the country; a historian says not quite.

Opava's Silesian Museum traces itself to 1 May 1814 and the Gymnasium Museum in the town's grammar school, which would make it the oldest museum in the Czech Republic, four years ahead of Prague's National Museum. It stayed independent until 1939, when it was merged into a Reichsgaumuseum; the present name dates from 1949. In 2014 the historian Pavel Šopák argued that 1814 marks only the first written report of the collection, not a founding act, and that Brno and Prague have the stronger claim. Either way, the building holds a serious collection of Silesian natural and human history.

Visit the museum that started a quarrel about being first; judge the collection for yourself.

The Dish — where the list comes from

The stories behind the canon, and where they are argued rather than settled.

#1
DISH

Kyselica

Wallachia's thick sauerkraut soup: the kraut and its brine, potatoes, diced sausage, caraway, often finished with sour cream. Local lore says it was the breakfast of men going to a day's work in the forest, and every cook claims the only correct method. Forbes.cz printed a Rožnov family recipe built on sausage, lard, potatoes, bay and allspice, with the advice to cook a double batch: it is better the next day.
WhereBeskydy pubs — Pustevny and the ridge · Maps →
#2
DISH

Ostravar, 1898–2027

Ostravar records its first mention in 1897 and its first brew in 1898, and still ferments in open vessels before weeks of cold lagering. Its dark special is Černá Barbora, for the miners' patron saint. On 8 October 2026 the owner, Pivovary Staropramen, announced that brewing leaves Ostrava in stages during 2027 for Prague. The beer will keep the name; it will no longer be the city's.
WhereAny Ostrava pub · Maps →
#3
DISH

Kołocz across the border

The Silesian kołocz is a protected cake on the Polish side of the border: the EU registered 'kołocz śląski / kołacz śląski' as a Polish PGI in July 2011. Cieszyn, on the Polish bank, faces Český Těšín across the Olza — the town split in two in 1920 — a short drive east of Ostrava. Cross the bridge for it; the fillings vary by baker.
