Beaune

Côte-d’Or · Bourgogne · France
"A ring of ramparts seven hundred metres across, a hospital roofed in glazed tiles, and under every street a cellar. The vines start where the houses stop."
The name Beaune is usually traced to Belenos, a Gaulish god of light and springs; the town itself does not insist.
The old forms are Belno and Belna, and the most often repeated story links them to Belenos, the Celtic god whose cult gathered at springs — the Bouzaize rises at the edge of town. It is a story, not a proof. The climat, the word this town is built on, is plainer: in Burgundy it means a named plot of vines with its own soil, slope and sun, and its own name on the label.

Beaune is the wine capital of the Côte d’Or, a walled town small enough to cross in fifteen minutes, with the vineyards starting at its back door: the Saturday market under the Halle, jambon persillé on rue Monge, eggs poached in red wine, mustard ground in stone, and the cellars under every street. Make or break: taste in a cellar with someone who made the wine, and walk up into the premier crus. The soul of the place — the essay
A fault line, the monks who named the plots, a duke’s market town, and a hospital that lived on its vines
THE ESSAY

Beaune sits on a fault line. When the Alps rose, the land to the east sank into the Saône plain and the limestone of the Côte was tilted towards the morning sun: a long, east-facing slope of marl and stone, drained by its own angle. That slope is the reason for the town, and you can see it from the ramparts.

The monks came first. Benedictines planted on the Côte in the tenth century, and the Cistercians of Cîteaux, founded in 1098 on the plain, walled the Clos de Vougeot in the decades after and spent centuries tasting it parcel by parcel. Those parcels, the climats, still carry the names the monks and the peasants gave them. Then the dukes of Burgundy made Beaune the market of the Côte: their state ran from Mâcon to the North Sea, and the wine went with their court.

In 1443 the duke’s chancellor, Nicolas Rolin, and his wife Guigone de Salins founded a hospital for the poor inside the walls, and gave it vines to live on. The hospital, its roof and its painting have cards of their own below, and the story of how it came to sell its wine at auction every November is the history fold. What it means at table is that this town has been pricing a barrel of wine in public for longer than almost anywhere.

The quintessence. The kitchen is the wine. The slope was too poor for wheat and the valleys rich in pasture, so the canon is a few braises built on the village red that would not sell as cru: beef cooked for hours in it, an old cockerel in it, eggs poached in it. Around them, the pig on Easter tables as jambon persillé, the cheese of the Auxois washed in marc, a choux puff of cheese served with the first glass, mustard ground with the region’s own sour wine, and blackcurrants turned into crème de cassis.

Where it comes from

The town inside the ramparts is small, and quiet after dark except around the Place Madeleine. The trade lives a few steps south-west of the Halle, in rue Maufoux, rue Paradis and rue d’Enfer, where the négociants’ doors open onto vaults that run under the street. The charcutiers are on rue and Place Monge; the mustard mill is out through the old Porte Bretonnière.

Two grapes do nearly all the work: Pinot Noir for the reds and Chardonnay for the whites, with Aligoté for the Kir. Where each came from, and the peasant grape both families share, is told on the curiosity cards.

Where the money sits

The money in Beaune is in the ground. A row of vines in a grand cru costs more than a house in town, and the houses that sell wine own the cellars under the streets. Most visitors see the Hôtel-Dieu and a tasting cellar, eat on a terrace on the Place Carnot, and drive on.

That is the line you can taste. The value is at the Saturday market before ten, a slice of jambon persillé from the charcutier, a bouchon with a menu that has not changed in years, and a glass poured by someone who knows whose vines it came from — a cellar at the end of the day, not a terrace at noon.

And the clock is the vine. Bud-break comes in April and the flowering in June; the harvest is in September, when the slope works from dawn and the town smells of must. In late October the new wine is in barrel and the cellars are busy again, the game is on the menus, and on the third weekend of November the town holds its auction and fills to the walls. Whoever comes in May gets a green slope and an empty town; whoever comes in November gets the new wine, the braises and the crowd.

1859 — The year the hospital's wine went under the candle
How a hospital for the poor came to be paid for by its vines, and how the sale of those vines' wine became the most watched auction in Burgundy.
THE STORY

The Hôtel-Dieu of Beaune, founded in 1443 by Nicolas Rolin and Guigone de Salins, ran on gifts, and the gifts that lasted were vines. From a first plot in the 1450s the estate grew to about 60 hectares, and since 1859 its new wine has been sold each November, barrel by barrel, to fund the hospital.

Rolin, chancellor to Philip the Good, and his wife founded the hospital in 1443, and patients were nursed in the Hôtel-Dieu from 1452 until 21 April 1971, when the modern hospital opened. Vines came as bequests. Sotheby's and Vinfolio date the first to 1457, a plot called Beaumont-le-Franc from Guillot or Jean Guillotte le Verrier; others give 1459 and Jean Plampays and his wife. The estate now holds about 60 hectares, some 50 of them Pinot Noir (Justerini & Brooks says over 61).

The wine was sold to pay for care. Sotheby's says the Hospices first sold by auction in 1795, then switched between auctions and private sales until 1859, the first of the annual sales; Berry Bros. dates the first auction itself to 1859. The new wine goes young, in 228-litre barrels called pièces, and the buyer raises and bottles it. In 1859 the bidding ran by candle: the last bid before the flame went out took the lot. One account says two candles had to burn down.

For decades négociants did the buying. Christie's took the sale over in 2005, on Sunday 20 November, and for the first time private buyers could bid without a négociant; Christie's ran it to 2020, and Sotheby's won the tender from 2021. The candle survives for the charity barrel. On 16 November 2025 the 165th sale made EUR 18,754,670 at the hammer, the third-best total on record, including EUR 400,000 for a Pommard premier cru Les Rugiens Pièce des Présidents, for EHCO and the Institut Robert-Debré.

Taste what the auction is about at the source: the Beaune premier cru in the five-wine route of the Marché aux Vins, opposite the Hôtel-Dieu, or a whole morning at the Saturday market under the Halle, where the sale itself is held.

The grapes, corrected

A peasant grape, a white Pinot, and a vine named for a royal birth.

The peasant grape that fathered the whitescuriosityChardonnay's other parent is Gouais Blanc, a grape so despised a duke tried to banish it

In 1999 a UC Davis team led by Carole Meredith, with John Bowers as first author, published DNA fingerprints in Science showing that 16 old northeastern French varieties, Chardonnay, Aligoté, Gamay Noir, Melon and Auxerrois among them, are the offspring of Pinot and Gouais Blanc. Gouais was a field grape of peasants, thought so poor that it is no longer planted in France. Burgundy's dukes had already chosen sides: on 31 July 1395 Philip the Bold ordered Gamay out of his vineyards around Beaune and Dijon, calling it 'a very bad and very disloyal plant'. The banished grape turned out to be Chardonnay's sibling.

Why go. Order a Bourgogne Aligoté and remember it shares both parents with the Chardonnay on the same list.

Touch it today
Bourgogne Aligoté, Gamay and Chardonnay on any Beaune wine list: three children of the same two parents
Where the sources argue

The Pinot that turned whitecuriosityA Nuits-Saint-Georges grower found Pinot Noir vines bearing white grapes, and made a wine of them

In the 1930s Henri Gouges, a grower in Nuits-Saint-Georges, noticed Pinot Noir vines carrying pale, white-skinned bunches: a natural colour mutation. He took cuttings, built a nursery from them and in time replanted his premier cru La Perrière with the white selection, which Clive Coates nicknamed 'Pinot Gouges'. The domaine made one or two casks of it in 1947 and did not sell it until the 1950s; Gouges shared cuttings with neighbours, so other village growers farm it too. It is not Pinot Blanc bought from a nursery but a Burgundian vine that changed colour on its own, in a red village.

Why go. Ask a Beaune wine list for Gouges's white La Perrière: a rare Nuits-Saint-Georges premier cru made from a red grape gone white.

The vine named for a child kingcuriosityA Beaune premier cru named for a royal birth a Carmelite nun is said to have foretold

Inside Beaune Les Grèves, a premier cru of about 32 hectares, lies a parcel of 3.92 hectares called the Vigne de l'Enfant Jésus. It belonged to the Carmelites of Beaune. The story told is that Marguerite du Saint-Sacrement, a Carmelite nun devoted to the Child Jesus, foretold the birth of a son to Anne of Austria, thought unable to bear children; the boy, born in 1638, became Louis XIV, and the vine took the Child's name. What is on record is the sale: in 1791, when Church land was sold off as biens nationaux, Bouchard Père & Fils bought it, and the house still holds it alone.

Why go. The bottle carries the name on its label: an 18th-century land sale you can drink.

Born in Beaune

The man who filmed a flying bird, and the man who taught France to draw in three dimensions.

Étienne-Jules Mareyborn hereThe Beaune physiologist who shot birds in flight with a camera shaped like a rifle

Marey was born in Beaune in 1830 to a wine-merchant family and became a physiologist who wanted to see movement the eye cannot catch. In 1882 he built the fusil photographique, a camera shaped like a rifle that took 12 pictures a second of a bird in flight. In October 1888 he showed the Académie des Sciences images on a strip of sensitised paper, moving at 20 a second. His chronophotographs, a runner or a gull broken into overlapping phases on one plate, still look modern. The town put up a monument to him on Place Marey, and the Musée des Beaux-Arts has long kept his name.

Why go. Stand at his monument, then ask at the Musée des Beaux-Arts what of Marey is on show this season.

The honest warning

What is sold as Burgundy-made, and is not.

What is sold as Burgundy-madethe warning'Moutarde de Dijon' is a recipe, not a place: most of the seed in it is Canadian

The shelves around the Hôtel-Dieu and Place Carnot sell jar after jar of 'moutarde de Dijon', and the name promises less than it seems. It is a method, brown seed ground with an acid, and the term is not legally tied to Dijon or to Burgundy. Canada supplies roughly 80 per cent of the seed used in French mustard, and French growers, almost all in Burgundy, cover only around a fifth, which is why the 2022 Canadian drought emptied French supermarket shelves. The jar that promises Burgundian seed carries the protected name 'Moutarde de Bourgogne' IGP. The snail on the souvenir menu is the plate's own warning.

The climatterroir42 premier crus and not one grand cru: why Beaune's slope is read parcel by parcel

A climat is a named, mapped plot of vines whose wine has been tasted and judged as distinct for centuries: the work of monks, dukes and growers who kept walking the same rows. When UNESCO inscribed the Climats of Burgundy on 4 July 2015, the site took in 1,247 of them along the Côte from Dijon to the Maranges. Beaune's own appellation counts 42 climats ranked premier cru, running in a band from the Pommard border to Savigny, and no grand cru at all. Read the system first at the Cité des Climats et Vins, then walk up behind the ramparts.

Why go. Learn three climat names before your first tasting; every Burgundy label then starts to make sense.

Before and after

KirapéritifA measure of Dijon cassis under cold Aligoté, named for the priest who was mayor of Dijon

Félix Kir, canon of the Church, Resistance figure and mayor of Dijon from 1945 to 1968, served white wine and blackcurrant liqueur at every reception, and the drink took his name. The liqueur is Dijon's own: the town's crème de cassis was first made in 1841 by Denis Lagoute, who macerated blackcurrants cold in alcohol. Today Cassis de Dijon is a geographical indication with rules, approved in 2013: made within Dijon, at least 400 g of sugar a litre and 15 percent alcohol, with Noir de Bourgogne or Royal de Naples berries in the mix. One part of it to four or five of sharp Aligoté.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates and their honest passports: which are Beaune’s own, which the whole of Burgundy shares, and which arrived from elsewhere.

ORIGIN

Œufs en meurette

Eggs poached gently in a reduction of red wine, the meurette, finished with lardons, button mushrooms and small onions and served on a croûton rubbed with garlic. It is a first course and a breakfast dish, and the clearest lesson in how Burgundy cooks: the wine is not poured beside the plate but reduced into it. Origin. Burgundy-wide; the sauce is the Côte’s Pinot Noir

Eat it — Le Bistrot Bourguignon, rue Monge
ORIGIN

Bœuf bourguignon

Beef shoulder or cheek browned, then braised slowly in red wine with a bouquet garni, lardons, small onions and mushrooms until the meat gives and the sauce is glossy and dark. The same logic as the meurette with more time: a dish that wants the afternoon. The beef of the region is the Charolais of the pastures to the south and west. Origin. Burgundy-wide; the beef is Charolais

Eat it — Le Bistrot Bourguignon, rue Monge
ORIGIN

Coq au vin

A mature cockerel, tougher and deeper in flavour than a young bird, jointed and braised slowly in red wine with lardons, onions and mushrooms; old recipes bind the sauce with the bird’s blood. A farm dish for using up a bird past its best and a wine past its youth. No table kept in this guide lists it in 2026: ask before you count on it. Origin. Burgundy and much of France claim it

ORIGIN

Jambon persillé

Shoulder ham simmered for hours in white wine with tarragon and thyme, torn into rough pieces and set in a terrine in its own jelly, green with parsley, then served cold in thick slices with cornichons and mustard. Traditionally made for Easter; on rue Monge it is sold every week of the year. Origin. Burgundy’s own: the Easter terrine

Eat it — Charcuterie Raillard, 4 rue Monge, or the Saturday market
ORIGIN

Gougères

Choux pastry made with a hard cheese, Comté or Gruyère, baked into light golden puffs and served warm. On the Côte the gougère is set out with the first glass at a tasting, to reset the palate; it travels badly, so eat it the hour it is baked. Origin. Burgundy’s own; often credited to the Yonne, to the north

ORIGIN

Escargots de Bourgogne

The Burgundy snail, Helix pomatia, baked in its shell under butter creamed with garlic and parsley. The wild snail has been protected in France since 1979, with picking restricted, so most escargots de Bourgogne served today are imported: the name is the recipe and the species, not always the origin. Ask, and buy them persillés from the charcutier. Origin. The recipe is Burgundy’s; most snails are not

Eat it — Charcuterie Raillard, persillés to bake aboard
ORIGIN

Époisses

A soft cow’s-milk cheese of the Auxois, its rind washed through its ageing with brine and then marc de Bourgogne until it turns orange and strong. Its making had nearly stopped by the Second World War; the Berthaut family revived it at Époisses in the 1950s, and it is now a protected appellation. The stories of Napoleon and of a ban on trains are stories. Origin. The village of Époisses, in the Auxois

Eat it — the Saturday market, or the affineur on the Place Carnot
ORIGIN

Pain d’épices

Spice bread of flour, honey and spices, kept for weeks, sliced thin with cheese or foie gras, and shaped by the Dijon bakers into nonnettes, small iced cakes with a fruit filling. Mulot & Petitjean in Dijon have made it since the eighteenth century and keep a shop on the Place Carnot. Origin. Dijon, by way of the medieval spice trade
