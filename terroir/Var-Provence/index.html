The soul of the place — the essay
A warship town, a forest of cork oak, a black grape that ages for decades, and the gulf that made the rest of the coast famous
THE ESSAY

The Var is the Côte d’Azur’s other half — the half that never needed the sunglasses. Wedged between Nice and Monaco to the east and Marseille to the west, it keeps the largest forests of the region: the cork oak and chestnut of the Maures, the red porphyry of the Esterel. More than half the department is wooded, a share only the Landes beats in France. This is a coast that still works for a living.

Its capital is a warship town, not a resort: the roadstead of Toulon is the French navy’s Mediterranean home, and its card among the curiosities has the numbers. Offshore lie the Îles d’Hyères — Porquerolles, Port-Cros, Le Levant — much of them national park, and Hyères itself was the Riviera’s first winter resort, all palms and Belle Époque villas, decades before Cannes learned the trick.

Saint-Tropez is here too, with the yachts walled along its quay and Club 55 on the sand. But twenty minutes’ drive puts you in a vigneron’s cellar under the Castellet, or in a market where the cade is still baked in a copper pan over wood. The Var wears both without apology.

The quintessence. The ritual is the apéro at the hour the light goes amber: a cold bottle of Provence pink — or a serious Bandol — on a café table by the port at Sanary or Le Brusc, a saucer of tapenade and olives, pastis for the older men, the boules clicking on the gravel. The morning version is the market, heaped with tomatoes, figs and chickpea galettes. And to walk it off, the old customs path round the capes.

Where it comes from

The vine came by sea. Greek sailors from Phocaea, the founders of Marseille, brought it to this coast in the sixth century BC; the Romans terraced the slopes above what is now Bandol and shipped the wine from its port. At Fréjus they raised Forum Julii around 49 BC, and Octavian brought home there the galleys he took from Mark Antony at Actium in 31 BC.

Toulon’s deep, sheltered roadstead made it the navy’s, from Henri IV’s shipyard of 1599 onward. Inland, the Maures lived for centuries on cork, chestnuts and wine — the cork oak of the massif stoppered the bottles of the south. Then in 1941 one grower won Bandol its own appellation, built on an old, stubborn black grape: that story is told just below.

Two later chapters made the modern Var, and each has its own card: the interwar avant-garde that the Noailles drew to their villa above Hyères, the German writers who found refuge in Sanary after 1933 — and, on 15 August 1944, the landings that Provence remembers as its own D-Day.

Where the money sits

Three economies share this coast. The oldest and steadiest is the navy: permanent, unglamorous money that has nothing to do with the season. The loudest is summer and the yacht trade — the port of Saint-Tropez takes boats to about 70 metres, and a week’s charter on the gulf runs from tens of thousands of euros to a million (seen July 2026).

The quiet fortune is wine. Provence pours a large share of the world’s rosé, and pale pink became a luxury category: that is where the markup lives, and a fashionable bottle can cost more than a red of real substance. The value sits west, in Bandol — age-worthy Mourvèdre reds, still a fraction of the price of a comparable Burgundy or Bordeaux. And Toulon, for all its want of glamour, is where you eat and drink honestly for a third of a Saint-Tropez bill.

And the clock is the season. The palace tables and the Pampelonne clubs open in spring and close in autumn; the hills run the other way, with the chestnut Sundays in October and the black truffle from late November. Whoever slips to a working port, inland to the cellars, or onto a ferry to a car-free island gets the Var the August crowd never sees.

1941 — the grower who saved a black grape
Not a château legend but a farm at Le Plan-du-Castellet: how Bandol was rebuilt round Mourvèdre, and how a Provençal kitchen table reached California
THE STORY

Before Lucien and Lulu Peyraud, Mourvèdre was being torn out of Bandol for grapes that yielded more; after them, it became one of the great red wines of France.

The bastide at Domaine Tempier had stood in the Tempier family since 1834, but the wine was ordinary and the future looked pinker. Across Bandol, growers were pulling up their old Mourvèdre — low-yielding, slow, stubborn to ripen — and replanting with varieties that gave more juice for less trouble. When Lucie 'Lulu' Tempier married Lucien Peyraud in 1936 and her father handed the young couple the estate, Lucien made exactly the opposite bet.

He was convinced Mourvèdre was the historic grape of this coast and its salvation: slow to ripen but resistant to oxidation, capable of wines that could age for decades. He championed a protected appellation, and on 11 November 1941 Bandol was granted its AOC — with Mourvèdre written into the rules. Lucien Peyraud is remembered as the father of the modern Bandol appellation; the appellation's insistence on Mourvèdre, and its long rest in old oak foudres, is essentially his doing.

The second chapter is a kitchen. The American food writer Richard Olney lived nearby and, in the 1970s, introduced the Peyrauds to a young California importer, Kermit Lynch — who has shipped Tempier to the United States since 1976 — and to Alice Waters of Chez Panisse. Lulu's unfussy Provençal cooking, laid out under the plane trees, became a touchstone of the American farm-to-table movement; Olney devoted a whole book to it, Lulu's Provençal Table, in 1994. Lulu Peyraud died in 2020, aged 102, having watched a grape she and her husband refused to give up become a benchmark of France.

The rule Lucien Peyraud wrote is still the law of the glass: at least half Mourvèdre in the red, and a long rest in wood before it is sold. Tempier is not a table in this guide: it receives by appointment, and the Bandol wine country card under Autour says how. Two of the appellation’s other references are, in the cellar doors among the tables: Château de Pibarnon on its amphitheatre of terraces, and Domaine de Terrebrune at Ollioules. La liste at the foot pours it.

The gulf of Saint-Tropez

The fishing port that became a myth, the two perched villages over Pampelonne, and the medieval lanes of Bormes.

Saint-Tropezfishing portUnder the yachts, a fortified fishing port that still sells its catch on marble every morning

Strip away August and Saint-Tropez is a small fortified port on the south shore of its own gulf, named for Torpes, the martyr whose headless body, legend says, drifted ashore here in a boat. Every May since 1558 the Bravade carries his gilded bust through the streets to drums and musket fire. In the mornings fishermen lay out bream, red mullet and octopus on marble at the fish market behind the quay; on Tuesdays and Saturdays the plane trees of the Place des Lices shade the market, and by afternoon the gravel is a pétanque court again. By mid-October the town is its residents' again.

Why go. Come on a Saturday morning in autumn: the market under the planes, fish on the slabs, and room to walk the quay.

Toulon is the Var's capital and a warship town, not a resort: grey hulls in the Rade, sailors on the quays, and tables that cost about a third of what the gulf charges. What grows here

A fig with its own appellation, a bark that outlives the fire, and a yellow flower that came from the other side of the world.

The fig with its own appellationcuriosityFrance grows one fig with an appellation, and nobody agrees how much of the country's crop it is

France has exactly one fig with its own appellation: the Figue de Solliès, the near-black Bourjassotte Noire of the Gapeau valley northeast of Toulon. It became an AOC in 2006 and a European AOP in 2011. It is picked from about 15 August into October and sold dead-ripe from the orchards and village stalls of the valley. What it weighs in the national harvest is a number everyone quotes and nobody quite agrees on.

Why go. Eat one in the valley in its last weeks; the larder card tells you where to buy.

Touch it today
Mid-October is the tail of the harvest: the last AOP figs are on the stalls around Solliès-Pont (where to buy is the card 'The Solliès fig growers')
Where the sources argue
The Ministry of Agriculture says the appellation is 80% of France's fig production; other sources give about three-quarters; a 2025 trade report puts the Solliès basin at two-thirds to three-quarters; the cooperative alone is put at about 60%. The old guide said both 'three-quarters' and 'well over half'
The cork oak and the firecuriosityA tree that loses its leaves to a forest fire and puts out green shoots a few weeks later

Drive into the Maures and the rock changes under the wheels: this massif is old crystalline schist, not the limestone of the rest of Provence, and its non-limestone soils grow chestnut and cork oak. The cork oak is built for fire. Its thick bark insulates the trunk, so when a blaze runs through, the tree loses its leaves and most of its small branches but the trunk is only scorched; within weeks green shoots break from the blackened limbs. The same bark, stripped from living trees, was the Maures' industry for centuries and corked the bottles of the south.

Why go. Look at the trunks along the Maures roads: blackened bark with green growth on top is the whole story.

Touch it today
The cork-oak woods around Collobrières and Gonfaron, where the massif's cork trade was based; the Maures burned badly in 2021, and the regrowth is visible from the roads
Where the sources argue
The State environment office (DREAL PACA) says cork-oak forests recover well only if fires are not too frequent, and abandoned, uncleared stands burn readily: 'survives the fire' holds for one fire, not for repeated ones
The mimosa is AustraliancuriosityThe yellow that defines a Var winter came over the garden walls from Australia

The mimosa that turns the Maures yellow every winter is not from Provence at all. Acacia dealbata is Australian: it arrived on the Riviera as a garden plant in the 1800s, escaped over the walls and spread across the hills, until the coast came to think of it as its own. Bormes made the adoption official in 1968, when the commune renamed itself Bormes-les-Mimosas. Its corso began in 1920, with the flowers heaped on carts drawn by donkeys; tractors and then floats took over, and the 2026 parade was billed as the 83rd.

Why go. Come in February and the import is the most local-looking thing in the Var.

Touch it today
Nothing in flower in October: the trees bloom from January to March, and the corso is in mid-February in Bormes (14-15 Feb in 2026)
Where the sources argue
The 1920 start is Bormes's own date; a Var tourism page dates Provençal flower corsos generally to the late 19th century, and the old guide's 'oldest flower parade on the Côte d'Azur' was not confirmed

The sea and the navy

Why the islands are called golden, the fleet in the roadstead, and the river that gave the department its name and then left it.

Why the islands are goldencuriosityNo treasure: the Îles d'Or are said to take their name from mica flashing in the rock

The Greeks called them the Stoechades, the islands 'in a row', one behind another: Porquerolles, Port-Cros and Le Levant strung along the horizon off Hyères. The golden name came later, usually dated to the Renaissance, and the usual explanation is the rock, not treasure: the islands are built of mica-schist, and under a low, slanting light the mica glints gold. Hyères keeps a folk tale as well, of a nobleman whose four daughters were famous for their beauty and for swimming off the coast. Neither version rests on a document the sources produce.

Why go. Watch the island shores from the boat late in the day and decide for yourself.

Touch it today
From the boat to Porquerolles or Port-Cros in late-afternoon light, watch the schist of the shore cliffs (the islands trip card owns the ferry)
Where the sources argue
The Renaissance date and the mica-schist explanation are widely repeated (Routard, regional guides) but no primary source was found; the folk tale of the four daughters is the alternative
Most of the French fleet sleeps in TouloncuriosityOver 60% of the French navy's tonnage ties up in one Var roadstead

Toulon's fate was its roadstead, deep and sheltered. Henri IV founded a shipyard here in 1599; Richelieu and then Colbert enlarged it, and Vauban designed the new basin, the darse Vauban. Today the arsenal covers some 268 hectares behind about 10 km of quays: the largest naval base in the Mediterranean and in Western Europe, home port of the carrier Charles de Gaulle and of the navy's nuclear attack submarines, and the berth of more than 60% of the French fleet's tonnage. Its two largest drydocks are given as 422 m by 40 m; work on the 400 m docks began in 1911.

Why go. Take the boat round the Rade: the grey hulls are the most permanent thing on this coast.

Touch it today
The arsenal is a military zone; see it from a boat tour of the Rade from Quai Cronstadt on the old port, or from Mont Faron above (its own card)
Where the sources argue
Tonnage share is usually given as over 60% (Assystem, GlobalMilitary), some older sources say 70%; the old guide's 20,000 staff was not re-checked

The river the Var lost in 1860curiosityThe Var is named for a river that has not flowed through it since 1860

Until 1860 the river Var was a frontier: the department's eastern edge ran along it, with the Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia on the far bank. When Nice was annexed that year, France rebuilt the Alpes-Maritimes from the county of Nice and, taken from the Var, the arrondissement of Grasse. The river ended up entirely outside the department, and the Var kept its name anyway, a department named for water that no longer touches it. The same line explains a sandwich: the pan bagnat sold on Var beaches is Niçois, from across a border barely a century and a half old.

Why go. Drop it at lunch over a pan bagnat: the department, and the sandwich, belong to two sides of an 1860 border.

Touch it today
Order a pan bagnat on a Var beach and know it is an import: pan banhat, 'bathed bread', in the old Nissart tongue
Where the sources argue
One source calls a department not watered by its namesake river unique in France; that was not confirmed

The other D-Day, and the exiles

August 1944 on these beaches, and the German writers who sat out the 1930s on Sanary’s quay.

15 August 1944curiosityProvence's own D-Day: the landing that freed Toulon and Marseille in under a fortnight

Normandy has its D-Day; Provence has 15 August 1944. At 8am Operation Dragoon put Allied troops ashore on beaches between Toulon and Cannes, the main landings strung from Cavalaire to Saint-Raphaël. Some 350,000 men were engaged, about 250,000 of them in General de Lattre de Tassigny's French Army B. The prize was the two great ports, which could then land men and supplies for the drive north: Marseille was freed on 28 August, and Toulon fell in the same last days of the month, under a fortnight after the landing. Provence remembers it as its own liberation.

Why go. Stand at the memorial on Mont Faron and look down at the port the landing was fought for.

Touch it today
The Mémorial du Débarquement et de la Libération en Provence on the summit of Mont Faron (the cable car belongs to the Mont Faron walk card; check opening); the landing beaches of the Cavalaire bay are in the sister guide at /terroir/Cavalaire-sur-Mer/
Where the sources argue
Troop numbers: about 350,000 for the operation (Ministry of the Armed Forces), the French share given as 250,000 or 260,000 (Hérodote); the old guide's 'half a million' is not supported. The exact day of Toulon's liberation was not pinned in the sources checked

Sanary, capital of German letterscuriosityIn 1933 a Var fishing port became the capital of German literature in exile

After Hitler took power in 1933, a small fishing port in the Var filled with German-speaking writers. The critic Ludwig Marcuse called Sanary the 'capital of German literature in exile': Thomas Mann and his son Klaus, Lion Feuchtwanger, Franz Werfel and Alma Mahler, Stefan Zweig among those who came. Aldous Huxley was there before them: he bought a house in 1930, wrote Brave New World in it, and from 1933 welcomed the exiles. The war scattered the colony again. A plaque now lists 36 German-language writers and artists who lived here in those years.

Why go. Read the 36 names on the plaque, then look at the quiet port they chose to wait in.

Touch it today
The plaque with the 36 names, in the patio of the Sanary tourist office (town heritage guide); the town's exile heritage panels

The Vieux Moulin à huile du Partégal stands on the chemin des Laures at La Farlède, in the valley east of Toulon, and dates from the 16th century. The guided visit, in French or English, takes about an hour and a quarter: the old mill, how the olives become oil, then the tasting table, where several oils are poured side by side before tapenades and other local products. For a cook stocking a galley, it is the simplest way to taste Provençal oils against each other before choosing which bottles to buy.

Along the sea

The double sandbar and its flamingos, the customs path round the capes, and the mountain over the roadstead.

Presqu'île de Gienswalk · tomboloTwo sand bars tie Giens to the mainland, with flamingos standing in the old salt pans between them

Giens is an island that got caught. Two parallel sand bars, a rare double tombolo, fasten it to the coast at Hyères and enclose the old Salins des Pesquiers, where the Salins du Midi company made salt until 1995. The Conservatoire du littoral has owned the marsh since 2001, and pink flamingos stand in it all year, at their most numerous, around 2,000, in late summer. The path round the wild west and south shores follows the old customs patrols' beat, and a 1976 law keeps a three-metre strip along the French shore open to walkers, even across private land.

Why go. Walk the cliffs of the south shore with the wind in your face, then come back past the salt pans in the late light for the birds.

Gigaro to L'Escaletwalk · customs pathThe old customs path from La Croix-Valmer to Ramatuelle, cape by cape past Lardier and Taillat

Long before the beach clubs, men in uniform walked this shore watching for smugglers. The sentier du littoral is often dated to 1791, the year the revolutionary state founded its customs service, though no source pins down when this stretch was first cut. Why go. Start early from Gigaro, swim at Cap Taillat at midday, and arrange a pick-up at L'Escalet so you do not have to walk back.

The canyon and the forest

Down into the Verdon by ladder and tunnel, and up through the chestnuts to a monastery of green stone.

Sentier Blanc-Martelwalk · canyonSixteen kilometres down through the Verdon gorge, with steel ladders and a 670 m tunnel in the dark

In August 1905 the speleologist Édouard-Alfred Martel, sent by the Ministry of Agriculture to look for drinking water for Provence, made the first full traverse of the Verdon gorge, guided by Isidore Blanc, the schoolmaster of Rougon. The Touring Club de France built the path between 1926 and 1928, ladders at the Brèche Imbert included, and Blanc worked on it until his accidental death in 1933; it has carried both names since 2005. It runs on the Alpes-de-Haute-Provence bank, down from the Chalet de la Maline to the river and out through unlit tunnels near the Couloir Samson.

Why go. Take the shuttle and walk it one way; the long tunnel at the end, torch in hand, is the part you will remember.

Chartreuse de la Vernewalk · monasteryA dark-stone monastery on a Maures ridge, founded around 1170 and silent again since 1983

The Carthusians came to this ridge above the chestnut woods around 1170, under the bishops of Toulon and Fréjus, and their first church was consecrated on 3 October 1174. The lords of Bormes pillaged it in 1416; the Revolution confiscated it in 1789 and the monks left. The green-black serpentine framing its doorways was quarried nearby in the Maures. Listed as a historic monument since 1921, it has been home since 1983 to the nuns of the monastic Family of Bethlehem, so you visit the edges of a working silence, booklet in hand, with the forest close on every side.

Why go. Leave the car at the end of the road, walk the last ten minutes in, and keep your voice down once inside the walls.

The island water

The pine-backed beach of Porquerolles, and the marked snorkel trail of Port-Cros.

Plage Notre-Damecove · porquerollesEight hundred metres of pale sand under the pines, on the Porquerolles bay that turns its back to the mistral

On 22 February 1912 François-Joseph Fournier, who had made his fortune from a gold lode in Mexico, bought almost all of Porquerolles at auction as a present for his wife Sylvia, then planted the eucalyptus, mimosa and umbrella pines that still shade its tracks. The French state bought most of the island in 1971. Notre-Dame is the long bay east of the village, about 800 m of pale sand with Aleppo pines growing almost to the water, angled so it is sheltered from both the mistral and the east wind. European Best Destinations named it Europe's most beautiful beach in 2015.

Why go. Come in the shoulder months; in peak summer the island caps visitors at 6,000 a day, and the bay feels it.

Sentier sous-marin de la Paludsnorkel · port-crosFrance's first underwater trail, laid in 1979: six buoys, a mask and fins, and grouper that hold their ground

Port-Cros is a national park at sea as well as on land (the islands card tells how it began), and this is where you read it underwater. A path from the harbour reaches the Plage de la Palud in 40 to 45 minutes; from the beach, a marked snorkel loop of about 40 minutes swims out past six buoys with explanation panels, in a few metres of water. The mainland sand

A sandbar tying a rock to the coast, and the five kilometres of Pampelonne without a club bill.

Cap Taillatcove · ramatuelleA sand neck ties a scrubby islet to the shore, with a swim on either side of it

Cap Taillat came close to being a holiday village. In 1970 a company bought 27 hectares here for 400 Club Med bungalows and a marina; Ramatuelle's mayor called in the Conservatoire du littoral, which bought the cape in 1987. What survived is a sand isthmus joining a scrubby islet to the shore, with clear water on both flanks, and on the ridge above it the Dolmen de Briande, a Neolithic tomb excavated in 1935, the southernmost in the Var. The maquis looks young because it is: the fire of 24 July 2017 burned some 550 hectares across Taillat and Cap Lardier.

Why go. Swim on whichever side of the tombolo is out of the wind that day.

The patrons and the gardener

The Noailles’ white villa, a collector’s galleries under an island farmhouse, and the world’s Mediterranean gardens on one slope.

Villa Noaillesmodernism on a hillA white cubist house above Hyères where the Noailles paid for Buñuel, Man Ray and a garden in a triangle

Charles and Marie-Laure de Noailles gave the young Robert Mallet-Stevens a slope above the old town and asked, so the story goes, for an interesting little house to live in. From 1923 to 1933 he kept adding to it: an indoor pool, a gymnasium, a squash court, terraces of white cubes. In 1926-27 Gabriel Guévrékian laid out the triangular Cubist garden of coloured tiles and squares of planting. They also paid for Buñuel's L'Âge d'or, and Man Ray filmed Les Mystères du Château de Dé here in 1929. Hyères bought the house in 1973; it is now an art centre for design, fashion, architecture and photography.

Why go. Walk into the Cubist garden and look back at the house: the whole idea of a joyful modernism fits in one view.

Around 1910 a businessman, Alfred Courmes, built the estate and its great pergola above the sea. Why go. Take the cypress stair to the little bay; in summer there is a guided snorkel trail in it.

Saint-Tropez under the glamour

The Citadellethe town's sea museumA six-sided keep of 1602-08 above the port, with the widest view of the gulf and the Maures

The keep went up between 1602 and 1608 on the hill behind the town, with ramparts that still look down on the harbour and across the gulf to Sainte-Maxime. Inside, the maritime history museum tells the story of Saint-Tropez sailors rather than yachts: the Bailli de Suffren, the fishing boats, and the coastal trade that kept thousands of local men at sea for centuries. The climb up through the old town is short and steep, and on a clear autumn day the Maures stand out sharp behind the water.

Why go. Even with the museum shut, walk up to the ramparts for the whole gulf at your feet.

The honest warning

What is sold as Provençal-made, and is not.

What is sold as Provençal craftthe warning'Savon de Marseille' on a bar is not a protected name, and the soap makers have fought over it for years

Anyone may stamp Savon de Marseille on soap. In 2015 and 2016 three rival associations filed specifications with France's industrial-property office, the INPI, to protect the name. The four makers of the UPSM, Fer à Cheval, Le Sérail and Du Midi in Marseille and Marius Fabre in Salon-de-Provence, argued for vegetable oils only, the traditional cauldron method, no additives and production in the Bouches-du-Rhône. Twelve rival firms wanted room for perfumes and colourings; a third group, asking for all of France, was refused. Why go. Buy soap with a maker's name and town on it, and turn over any 'Provence' print or sachet for the line that says where it was made.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Ten plates and their honest, often contested, passports: which are the Var’s own, and which it borrowed from Marseille and Nice.

ORIGIN

Cade toulonnaise

A chickpea-flour galette baked thick in wide copper pans over wood, peppered and eaten hot from a paper cone. Nice’s socca is its thin, crisp cousin; Toulon’s is thicker and soft underneath. Buy it in the morning: the carts work until lunch. Origin. Toulon

Eat it — La Cade à Dédé, on the Cours Lafayette
ORIGIN

Chichi frégi

A ring of wheat-and-chickpea dough fried and rolled in sugar, sold from kiosks by the sea and at the fairground. With the cade, the dish Toulon claims as wholly its own, and defends. Origin. Toulon (disputed with L’Estaque)

ORIGIN

Tarte tropézienne

A sugar-crusted brioche split and filled with two creams, pastry cream and buttercream, sold by the slice or whole in its box. Eat it the day it is made. Origin. Saint-Tropez

ORIGIN

Grand aïoli

Less a sauce than an occasion: raw garlic, egg yolk and olive oil whipped stiff by hand, with poached salt cod, boiled vegetables, eggs and sea snails to dip, shared at a long table. Traditionally a Friday. Origin. Provence

Eat it — Le Pont d’Or, Barjols, on Fridays
ORIGIN

Daube provençale

Beef braised for hours in red wine with orange peel and herbs, by tradition in a bellied daubière that rains its steam back over the meat. The name comes from the Provençal adobar, to prepare: carters’ food, eaten in the hills in winter. Origin. Provence, inland

Eat it — La Petite Fontaine, Collobrières
ORIGIN

Soupe de poissons

What most Var bistros actually pour: rockfish simmered and strained into a rust-coloured broth, with rouille, garlic croutons and grated cheese to float on top. Bouillabaisse’s everyday cousin, and no whole fish. Origin. The Provençal coast

Eat it — La Ponche, Saint-Tropez
ORIGIN

Bouillabaisse

The rockfish stew of fishermen’s unsold catch, rascasse essential, served in two acts since the 1980 Marseille charter: the saffron broth with rouille first, the fish on a platter after. Eaten along the Var, but honest about its passport. Origin. Marseille — not a Var dish

ORIGIN

Tapenade

Black olives, capers and anchovies pounded with olive oil: the smear of the apéro. The name comes from tapeno, Provençal for the caper; chef Meynier of La Maison Dorée in Marseille is credited with it in 1880, and his first version carried tuna. Origin. Marseille

ORIGIN

Pieds et paquets

Sheep’s trotters and little parcels of stuffed tripe, stewed most of a day in white wine and tomato: poverty cooking that wasted nothing. The Var eats it; Marseille and Sisteron own it. Origin. Marseille and Sisteron

ORIGIN

Thon à la provençale

Mediterranean bluefin, grilled, or braised with tomato, olives and capers. From the seventeenth century the tuna was taken in madragues, vast net-mazes off this coast; Bardot’s house at Saint-Tropez is named for one. The traps are all but gone; the fish stayed on the menus. Origin. The gulf of Saint-Tropez

Eat it — La Voile, its spice-rubbed bluefin, in season
