Girona

Catalunya · Spain
"Four rivers, a Roman road and the widest Gothic vault ever raised — a stone city that doubles itself in the Onyar and eats off the Empordà plain."
The name From the Latin Gerunda, the garrison Rome set on the Via Augusta around 77 BC — but the name is older than the town.
It is almost certainly Iberian, most plausibly a Latin rendering of *Kerunta, the hillfort at Sant Julià de Ramis whose people were moved downhill into the new grid (the asterisk is the archaeologists’ honesty: a reconstructed word, never found written). The thirteenth-century chronicles still waver — Gerona in Jaume I, Girona already in Desclot and Muntaner — until Girona held. The story that it remembers Geryon, the three-bodied giant, is a fifteenth-century bishop’s flourish: legend, not etymology.

Girona is a fortress that learned to be gentle: a stone town on a rock where four rivers meet, its coloured houses hanging over the Onyar, and one family — the Rocas — cooking at every rung from an €18 menú to three stars. Make or break: El Celler de Can Roca opens its books eleven months out; book it, or Esperit Roca ninety days out, before the flights. The warning: the terraces of the big squares are priced for the view, and Temps de Flors in May and the Fires in late October fill the old town by mid-morning. Walk it before ten.

The soul of the place — the essay
A fortress on four rivers, a table on the seam of plain and sea, a frontier you can taste, and who pays for what
THE ESSAY

Girona stands on a promontory where the Onyar, the Ter, the Güell and the Galligants meet. Rome planted a garrison there on the road to Hispania; the river split the town in two; the rock gave it height on three sides and the water closed the fourth. Everything the visitor loves about it — the stepped lanes, the walls you can walk, the houses that double themselves in the river — grew out of that defensive arithmetic.

And yet the texture is soft. Worn steps, weathered Romanesque stone, ivy on the bastions, the low warm light of the Ter valley on the cathedral’s Baroque staircase, and the slow river doing all the framing. Josep Pla, who studied here, wrote that he had felt the weight of its stones in every fibre of his life. The most besieged town in Catalonia turns out to be one of the easiest to be in.

The table answers the stone. Girona eats off two landscapes at once — the Empordà plain behind it, the Costa Brava an hour to the east — and its cooking lives on that seam: sea and mountain in one pot, a fishermen’s stew thickened with potato and almond, a sweet sausage nobody else makes, and the small beans grown on the volcanic soil of the Garrotxa. Feast days are kept on the plate: canelons on 26 December, from the meat of Christmas Day.

The quintessence. Each spring the Tarlà, an acrobat puppet, turns somersaults on a bar across Carrer de l’Argenteria. Legend says the original Tarlà, hiding in a flour sack, sneezed ‘xui-xui’ and named the city’s pastry; the ledger says a confectioner three streets away invented it around the end of the Great War. Eat one warm at mid-morning, sugar on your fingers in a medieval lane, and you know which city you are in: one that keeps the legend and the ledger side by side.

Where it comes from

The oldest facts here are the rock and the water; Rome only ratified them. The Carolingian walls laid over the Roman line are among the longest early-medieval circuits in Europe and you can walk most of them. Below the cathedral, along Carrer de la Força, the Call kept one of the best-preserved Jewish quarters of medieval Europe, where Bonastruc ça Porta — Nahmanides — was born in 1194. The sieges, the flies and 1809 are told under the curiosities; the stones under Culture.

What you taste is a frontier. A plain that had to feed a besieged town — the Girona apples, the beans of Santa Pau, the Empordà wine on slate — a coast near enough for the prawns of Palamós and the anchovies of L’Escala, and a French border close enough to hand the city its pastry.

Where the money sits

Three currencies feed these tables: the day-trip euro that arrives from Barcelona on the half-hourly high-speed train, the cycling euro of the professional riders who live here and spend like residents, and the local euro that keeps the menú del dia honest. The first pays for the photo-menu terraces on the big squares; the other two pay for everything worth eating.

So spend where repeat custom disciplines the cooking: the market house opposite the Mercat del Lleó, the chefs’ night-off bistros, the €18 lunch in the Roca parents’ bar in Taialà. The money that remains is the one booking that needs a season’s notice.

And the clock is the calendar. September is the year’s best stretch — the Empordà harvest inland, the coast emptying, the apples coming in. Two weeks overload the old town: Temps de Flors in mid-May, when the private patios open, and the Fires de Sant Narcís at the end of October, the city’s own week. Come for either if you like crowds and flowers or fairs; otherwise come on a weekday, and walk before ten.

Universo Roca — the family that grades the city
The parents’ bar, the plain bistro, the cone, the chocolate, the fort and the three stars: one family’s ladder, every rung open
THE STORY

Girona’s gravity is a family that never left. Three brothers grew up in their parents’ workers’ restaurant in Taialà, on the edge of town, and built the most decorated table in Spain a few hundred metres away. Around it sits a ladder anyone can climb.

The bottom rung is the original: Can Roca, the bar their parents opened in 1967, a weekday menú house for the neighbourhood and, by the family’s own account, the Celler’s staff canteen. Then Normal, on Plaça de l’Oli, where the brothers cook their mother’s Catalan stews straight on purpose; Jordi’s gelateria on Carrer de Santa Clara, the three-star dessert language at pocket money; his chocolate house over the Onyar, where the beans are roasted and conched on the premises.

Higher up, in the fort above the city at Sant Julià de Ramis, Esperit Roca serves the Celler’s back catalogue under its own MICHELIN star, bookable a season ahead rather than a year. At the top is the Celler itself. All of them have a card in the tables.

The point is the ladder. The same intelligence reaches you in a cone on the walk home, a stew at lunch, or the one dinner you plan a year around — the whole city graded by one family’s hand. And the Rocas send you onward: two of their former cooks run one of the bistros in ‘Where the chefs eat’.

The east bank — the stone city

The old town on the hill, the Jewish quarter inside it, and the abbey quarter at its northern gate.

Barri Vellthe stone cityThe old town climbs the hill in stone stairs, from the Roman road to the steep lanes below Sant Feliu

Rome planted Gerunda here around 77 BCE, a garrison on the road that became the Via Augusta, and the Barri Vell still climbs that same promontory on the Onyar's east bank. Carrer de la Força runs along the old line of the road; everything else is stairs. Towards the north the lanes drop steeply around the basilica of Sant Feliu, shaded even at noon, then open onto the square below the cathedral's Baroque flight. The cathedral, the baths, the walls and Sant Feliu each have their own card: this one is for getting lost between them.

Why go. Walk it before ten, while the stone is cool and the steps are empty of tour groups.

El Callthe Jewish quarterThe Jewish quarter inside the Barri Vell: a dozen steep lanes off one street, quiet after six

The Call is not a separate district but a knot of lanes inside the old town, hung off Carrer de la Força on the slope below the cathedral. Girona's Jewish community lived here until the expulsion of 1492; Nachmanides, Bonastruc ça Porta, was born here in 1194 and made the town one of the first centres of Kabbalah in Europe. Today the houses are homes, small hotels and a museum, and the tour groups thin out by late afternoon, when the lanes go quiet and the stone holds the day's warmth. The walk through it and the museum each have their own card.

Why go. Come back after six, when the groups have gone and the lanes are the neighbours' again.

Sant Pere — the abbey quarterthe north edgeThe quiet north end of the old town, where a stream meets the walls and a Romanesque abbey sits on the bank

North of the cathedral the Barri Vell loosens. The lanes run down to the Galligants, the smallest of Girona's four rivers, and to the Romanesque abbey that takes its name from it. In the eleventh and twelfth centuries this was one of the new burgs that grew outside the old city on its northern side, joined to Pedret by a new bridge over the Galligants finished around 1200. Today it is a quarter of low stone houses, garden walls and the sound of water, a few minutes and a world away from the cathedral steps. The abbey and the Arab baths each have their own card.

Why go. Come here when the cathedral square is full: the north end stays quiet even in May.

The river and the west bank

The painted houses on the water, the market grid opposite, and the northern edge where the Rocas cook.

The Onyar riverfrontthe river housesTwo banks of ochre, rust and pink houses facing each other across the water, the seam between the two Gironas

The cases de l'Onyar turn their backs on the old streets and their faces to the river: tall, narrow houses with balconies and washing lines hanging straight over the water. Their colours, ochre, rust, saffron and pink, were picked when the fronts were restored in 1983 and 1984, and on a still morning the whole row repeats itself upside down in the river. This strip is the seam of the city, the Barri Vell on one bank and the Mercadal on the other, with the footbridges stitching them together. The bridges have their own walk card: this is where to stand, eat and look.

Why go. See the houses from both banks: from the west the cathedral rises behind them, from the east the reflections face you.

Mercadalthe west bankThe flat bank across the river: the 1944 market hall, the arcaded square and the shopping streets

Cross any bridge westward and the ground goes flat. The Mercadal grew in the Middle Ages as Girona's market ground outside the walls, and it still feeds the city: the Mercat del Lleó, a 1944 hall of about sixty stalls, opens at seven and is picked over by mid-morning. A few streets north the Plaça de la Independència closes in a rectangle of nineteenth-century arcades, and the pedestrian streets around Santa Clara carry the shops. This is where Girona lives on weekdays, with shopping bags, school runs and coffee at the bar. The Devesa park lies beyond, with its own walk card.

Why go. Do the market before nine, then take your coffee under the arcades of the square.

Pedretthe north gateThe old road out of town to the north, where the river heads for the Ter

Pedret is the long street-village that leads out of the old town to the north, below the cliffs of the Barri Vell. It grew as one of the medieval burgs beyond the walls, joined to Sant Pere over the Galligants around 1200, and it still has the feel of a road out: low houses, workshops, the river on one side. Just beyond, the Onyar runs past the site of the old Pedret mill into the Ter, at the edge of the Devesa, where Girona's rivers meet. Come for a walk, not a meal: El Celler de Can Roca, often placed here, stands west of the centre in Taialà.

Why go. Walk it north to see where the Onyar ends and the city's four-river geography makes sense.

The green edges

The plane-tree park, the hill above the walls, and the valley the city walks into on Sundays.

La Devesaunder the planesThe west-bank park quarter: the plane-tree avenues, the Tuesday and Saturday market, the autumn fair

West of the Mercadal the grid stops at the Devesa, the park the city has used as its common ground since the nineteenth century, with the Ter and the Güell running past its edges. On Tuesday and Saturday mornings the open-air Mercat de la Devesa sets up nearby; at the end of October the avenues are given over to the rides, stalls and concert stages of the Fires de Sant Narcís, 23 October to 1 November in 2026. The rest of the year it is where Girona runs, walks its dogs and plays petanca. The walk under the trees, the market and the fair each have their own card.

Why go. This fortnight it is the fairground; come in the evening for the lights, by day for the market.

Montjuïcthe high groundThe wooded hill above the old town: the city's fort, a medieval cemetery and the path up to Sant Miquel

Climb out of the Barri Vell's north-east corner and the houses stop at the slopes of Montjuïc, a hill of holm oak, terraces and broken fortifications looking straight down on the cathedral roof. The name is usually read as the hill of the Jews: the Call's medieval cemetery lay up here, and gravestones from it are now shown in the Call museum. The ruined fort on the summit was the key to every siege of the city, which is a story for its own curiosity card. From the top, paths run on into the Vall de Sant Daniel and up to the Castell de Sant Miquel.

Why go. Go up for the view back over the old town's rooftops, the best free one in Girona.

Behind Montjuïc the road dips into a valley where Girona simply ends. Plane trees line a stream, kitchen gardens run along the bottom, and a string of springs, the iron-tasting Font del Ferro among them, have drawn families with picnic baskets for generations. At the top stands the Romanesque monastery of Sant Daniel, its square bell tower above the trees. In July and August, when the stone city holds the heat, this is where locals come for shade and running water. The Sant Daniel walk has its own card with the route.

The city that would not fall

Why Girona is called immortal, and the flies that are said to have helped.

The Immortal CitycuriositySeven months under French guns in 1809, and a governor who banned the word surrender

Girona calls itself la immortal, and the old guides give it some twenty-five sieges in its history, taken only seven times. The one that fixed the name began in May 1809, when a French and allied army sat down below the walls. The governor, Mariano Álvarez de Castro, organised the townspeople into companies, a women's company among them, and decreed death for anyone who spoke of surrender. The city held for seven months. Famine and disease, not assault, ended it: Álvarez fell ill, handed command to Brigadier Juan Bolívar, and Girona capitulated in December. The walls have their own walk card.

Why go. Look at the old town from below and picture it starving behind its walls for seven months.

Touch it today
The Museu d'Història de Girona's room on the sieges; and the Plaça de la Independència, named for the war

The flies of Sant NarcíscuriosityThe legend says flies poured out of a saint's tomb in 1285 and drove off a French army

In the summer of 1285 Philip III of France laid siege to Girona on his crusade against Aragon. The legend says his soldiers broke open the tomb of Sant Narcís, the city's patron, in the church of Sant Feliu, and a swarm of flies burst out and fell on the men and their horses until the army withdrew. The history is plainer: the French did take the city that year, the Sant Feliu suburb was badly damaged, and the king fell ill at Girona and died at Perpignan in October. The fly stayed anyway, and every October it is all over the Fires de Sant Narcís (its own calendar card).

Why go. Treat it as Girona does: a legend told with a straight face and a fly on every Fires poster.

Touch it today
The chapel of Sant Narcís in the Basílica de Sant Feliu (the church has its own card); a stone fly in the old town that visitors stroke for luck, its street not confirmed this pass
Where the sources argue
Some retellings put the miracle in 1286; historians note the tomb was largely an imagined one and that the story took centuries to grow, with later fly episodes claimed for 1653 and 1808

Who lives here now

The professional riders who made it home, and the television crews who borrowed its steps.

The cycling capitalcuriosityA small Catalan city where professional road racers outnumber tourists at the morning coffee bar

In the late 1990s a handful of American professionals, George Hincapie and Lance Armstrong among them, settled in Girona for its quiet back roads, and others followed. The reasons have not changed: low-traffic lanes in every direction, the Pyrenean foothills to the north, the Mediterranean about half an hour's ride east, a mild winter and an airport down the road, all cheaper than Monaco or Switzerland. Word of mouth did the rest. Today, at mid-morning, the café tables in the old town fill with team kit, and the sound is cleats on stone. La Fàbrica, a former pro's café, has its own card.

Why go. Sit at a café terrace mid-morning and watch the bunches roll in: the riders at the next table may be racing the Tour next summer.

Touch it today
Café terraces in the Barri Vell at mid-morning, full of riders in team kit; La Fàbrica on Carrer de la Llebre
Where the sources argue
Nobody keeps an official count: estimates run from about 90 professionals to 'more than 100' to over 180, and a local guide told Catalan News in 2023 there may be 100 to 150 living or having lived here
A season of dragons on the stepscuriosityIn September 2015 a horse climbed the cathedral steps and Girona became King's Landing, Braavos and Oldtown

Game of Thrones filmed in Girona in September 2015 for its sixth season, broadcast in 2016. The cathedral's Baroque staircase became the steps of the Great Sept of Baelor: the crew covered the statues and laid a surface on the stairs so Jaime Lannister could ride his horse up them. The cloister of Sant Pere de Galligants played the Citadel at Oldtown, where Samwell goes to train, and Arya's chase with the Waif ran through the old town's stairways and ended at the Arab baths. The Sept's later destruction was computer-made. Each building has its own card.

Why go. Stand at the foot of the cathedral steps and count them before you picture the horse.

Touch it today
The cathedral steps, the Arab baths and the cloister of Sant Pere de Galligants, all open to visit
Where the sources argue
Arya's fall into the market is placed by some on the steps of Sant Martí Sacosta, by others on the Pujada de Sant Domènec; the blind-beggar scenes are put on Carrer del Bisbe Josep Cartañà behind the cathedral

Born in the province

The volcanic beans, the apple of the Ter valley, and the grey-rinded goat’s cheese.

Fesols de Santa Pauvolcano beansSmall white beans grown in the crater country of the Garrotxa, and sold only in sealed DOP packs

Around Santa Pau the fields are dark, crumbly volcanic earth, low in acid, and three old bean varieties grow there: Tavella Brisa, Setsetmanera and Gra Petit. The EU protected them as a DOP in 2015. The beans are small with thin skins, so they cook to a creamy centre without splitting. Only a dozen or so growers remain, and in a lean year the harvest comes to only 20 to 25 tonnes, which is why the packs sell out before the next crop. Sold loose, a bean could be from anywhere; the sealed pack is the guarantee.

Why go. Eat them once with grilled botifarra in a Garrotxa village, then buy packs for the galley.

Poma de Gironathe plain's appleFirm, sharp apples from the orchards between Girona and the Empordà, under an EU seal since 2003

Drive out of Girona towards the sea in September and the plain is ruled into orchards, nets over the rows, bins stacked at the field edges. The picking lasts about three months, yet the fruit is eaten all year from cold store. The IGP, registered in 2003, covers a small set of familiar varieties grown here; warm days and cool nights in the Ter valley give them a firmer bite and more acid than the same apples from elsewhere. A recent harvest came to around 89,000 tonnes.

Why go. Buy a bag of Granny Smith for the galley; they hold up in a tart and in a long week aboard.

The wine estates of the Empordà, the oil of the Argudell olive, and the cheese counter of the old town.

The Empordà estatescellar doorLow bush vines bent by the tramuntana, and family cellars an hour north that pour for visitors who book

North of Girona the Empordà plain rises towards the Albera and the Rodes hills, and the vines there are short, gnarled bushes kept low against the north wind. The walls and the stone

The rampart path above the roofs, the cathedral-to-Sant Feliu climb, and the lanes of the Call.

Passeig de la Murallawall-top walkWalk the top of the old city wall, tower to tower, with the cathedral on one side and the plain on the other

The rampart path runs along walls first raised in the 9th and 10th centuries, when Girona was a Carolingian frontier town facing al-Andalus, laid on Roman lines from the 1st century BCE. You climb a stair and the city drops away: terracotta roofs, washing lines, the cathedral's grey bulk close enough to count its buttresses. Every so often a tower lets you climb higher, and from the top the view runs across the Ter plain to the hills. The path dips and climbs on narrow steps, and costs nothing.

Why go. Climb every tower you pass; each frames the cathedral from a new angle.

The river and the green

The bridges over the Onyar, the plane trees of the Devesa, and the valley of Sant Daniel.

The Onyar bridgesriver crossingCross the Onyar bridge by bridge, with the ochre and rust river houses doubled in the water below

The houses along the Onyar stand straight out of the river in a row of ochre, rust, pink and cream, a palette fixed during a 1980s restoration led by the painter Enric Ansesa with a team of architects. Zig-zag across the footbridges and watch them shift. Stop on the Pont de les Peixateries Velles, the red riveted-iron bridge put up in 1877 by Gustave Eiffel's company; whether Eiffel drew it himself is disputed, but the firm and the year are documented. From its middle the cathedral rises behind the façades.

Why go. Stand mid-span on the iron bridge at golden hour, when the reflection is sharpest.

The hill of churches

The widest Gothic nave, the basilica of the saint and his flies, and the Romanesque abbey that holds the archaeology.

Girona Cathedral (Santa Maria)one spanAlmost 23 metres of vault with no pillar to hold it: the widest Gothic nave anywhere

For about half a century the chapter argued over how to roof the church: three aisles, as everyone built, or one daring span. In 1417 the architect Guillem Bofill won, and the single vault went up 22.98 metres wide and 35 high, a width among church naves beaten only by St Peter's in Rome. Six hundred years later it has not moved. Outside, 90 Baroque steps laid in 1607 climb from the square; the television story they starred in is under the curiosities.

Why go. Walk to the middle of the nave and look up: the absence of columns is the whole point.

Basílica de Sant Feliuroman stoneEight carved Roman and early-Christian tombs built into one presbytery wall, and usually nobody looking

Before the cathedral took the title, this was Girona's great church, raised over the grave of the martyr Felix, killed in 304. You can read its age in layers: a Romanesque core, a Gothic nave, a Baroque front, and in 2011 Benedict XVI made it a minor basilica. The reason to step in is in the presbytery. Eight sarcophagi of the 3rd and 4th centuries, Roman and early Christian, are set into the walls; no other church in Iberia shows a run like it. The saint linked to the flies legend lies here too; that story has its own card.

Why go. Stand close to the presbytery wall: the tombs are within arm's reach, carved some seventeen centuries ago.

Sant Pere de GalligantsarchaeologyA Benedictine church of the 1100s, now the Girona home of Catalonia's archaeology museum

The monastery is first named in 988, but the church you enter dates from the first half of the 12th century, crowned by an octagonal bell tower ringed with Lombard bands. It has been a museum since 1857 and is now the Girona seat of the Museu d'Arqueologia de Catalunya, so Iberian pots and Roman mosaics stand under Romanesque arches. The prize is the 4th-century Sepulcre de les Estacions, a sarcophagus from Empúries carved with the seasons. The cloister had a screen career too: Game of Thrones used it as the Citadel of Oldtown.

Why go. Sit in the cloister among the carved capitals; it is the quietest corner of the north end of town.

The sardana

The dance that became a banned anthem, and the cobla that plays it best.

La Santa Espinathe sardana anthemA sardana tune so loaded with meaning that two dictatorships forbade it

Enric Morera wrote the music and Àngel Guimerà the words, and the piece was first heard in 1907. Its sardana became the most charged in the repertoire: the melody of a circle dance that reads, to many Catalans, as a national hymn. Both the Primo de Rivera regime and Franco's barred it, which only fixed it deeper in memory. Today a cobla usually saves it for a significant moment, and you will notice the change in the square: hands join a little more firmly, the older dancers are first into the ring, and some people sing along under their breath.

Why go. If you hear the opening bars at an audició, watch the oldest dancers rather than the band.

Havaneres are slow songs in the swaying habanera rhythm that Catalan crews carried home from the nineteenth-century Cuba trade, sung to guitar and accordion over a burning cremat. Narcís is Girona's patron, and the story told about him is the reason for the fly emblems seen all over town in late October: during a siege, flies poured out of the saint's tomb and drove off the attackers. In the hills and the woods

The Garrotxa beeches colour, the Gavarres give their mushrooms, and the Empordà olives are picked.

The beeches of the Fageda d'en Jordà turnGarrotxaThe beech wood on a lava flow near Olot colours from late October to mid-November; come early, parking fills

The Fageda d'en Jordà is a beech forest growing flat on an old lava flow in the Garrotxa volcanic park, about an hour north-west of Girona. Beeches usually grow in the mountains; here they stand on low ground. Guides put the colour change between late October and mid-November, the leaves going from yellow to copper and rust. The first crowds tend to arrive on the last weekend of October, with queues on the road and the paid car parks full. The usual start is the Can Serra information centre on the Olot to Santa Pau road, and the Joan Maragall trail is the short, easy loop.

Why go. Go on a weekday morning in the first half of November, when the colour is deepest.

The Dish — where the list comes from

Eight plates with their honest passports: the city’s own pastry, the seam of sea and mountain, the province’s feast days, and what the coast sends inland.

ORIGIN

Xuixo

A yeasted, laminated dough rolled into a cylinder, deep-fried, piped full of crema catalana and rolled in sugar while still hot so the crystals catch. The documented version: the confectioner Emili Puig made it on Carrer de la Cort Reial in the years around 1918–1920, after a French pastry cook showed him a cream-filled technique. The Tarlà’s sneeze is the folklore version. It carries the Catalan Producte de la Terra mark, and ‘the best in Girona’ is a rivalry, not a fact. Eat it warm: the sugar must crackle. Origin. Girona, Carrer de la Cort Reial

Eat it — Can Castelló, warm, or Tornés
ORIGIN

Mar i muntanya

Sea and mountain in one pot: chicken with langoustines, or rabbit with prawns, over a slow sofregit, bound at the very end with a picada pounded from fried bread, almonds, hazelnuts, garlic and parsley — sometimes the fried liver, sometimes a square of dark chocolate. Born of a fishing family’s economy, chicken stretched with shellfish. Add the picada too early and the sauce splits. Origin. The Empordà and the Costa Brava; a feast-day main

ORIGIN

Suquet de peix

The fishermen’s stew, first cooked aboard from fish that would not sell: rock fish, then monkfish or hake, simmered with potatoes in a garlic-and-tomato sofregit and finished with an almond picada. Potato starch and almond, never flour, give it body; the name comes from suquejar, to give up juice. Eat it in the harbour towns an hour east. Origin. The Costa Brava ports

ORIGIN

Niu de Palafrugell

A winter pot of cod tripe and stockfish with potato and egg, first a meatless Lenten dish; the cork workers added game birds and sausage, the fishermen cuttlefish. Five hours on the stove, an hour of it on the sofregit, finished with allioli. It surfaces only during the organised winter campaign at a handful of Palafrugell tables. Origin. Palafrugell, on the coast

ORIGIN

Canelons de Sant Esteve

Pasta rolled round the beef, chicken and pork left from Christmas Day’s escudella, under béchamel and cheese, gratinéed and eaten on 26 December, Catalonia’s second Christmas. Canelons came with Italian cooks to the Barcelona fondes of the late eighteenth century; the Sant Esteve habit is a twentieth-century one. Origin. All Catalonia; the family table

Eat it — Casa Marieta or Fonda Cal Ros, all year
ORIGIN

Botifarra amb fesols

A raw pork botifarra grilled over coals, with the small beans of Santa Pau simmered until creamy and dressed in the sausage fat. The pairing is in print by 1830, in the Nou manual per cuinar; the beans, grown on the volcanic soils of the Garrotxa, have been a protected origin since 2015. Origin. The Garrotxa and every Girona market house

Eat it — Ca l’Àngel, at breakfast, with white beans
ORIGIN

Anxoves de l'Escala

Anchovies gutted by hand, laid in salt in wooden barrels, pressed and left for months before they are filleted and laid in oil; the wood, not plastic, is held essential to the cure. The salting houses go back to the nineteenth century — Solés since 1888. A Catalan quality mark, not an EU protection. Origin. L’Escala, forty minutes north-east

ORIGIN

Gamba de Palamós

Aristeus antennatus, the red prawn of the deep canyon off Palamós, landed by a specialised trawler fleet and sold at the town’s auction; the Gamba de Palamós guarantee mark checks the ground, the count per kilo and the landing temperature. Seared seconds a side on the planxa to keep the head juices; overcooking it is the local sin. Prices are high and swing with the season. Origin. Palamós, on the coast

Eat it — Esperit Roca, Toda la gamba
