Istanbul

Boğaziçi · Türkiye
"The only city on two continents, and a table built largely by the people it lost."

Istanbul is not a city you eat your way through. It is a city that eats in two directions at once, across a strait, on a boat — and the single most useful thing to understand before your first meal is that the cuisine everybody calls Turkish was for four hundred years cooked and poured, in this city, mostly by Greeks, Armenians and Jews, and that almost all of them are gone. What is left is the food. That gap between the plate and the people who invented it is the subject of this guide.

Make or break: cross to Kadıköy, eat at a twelve-seat fire counter or at a table of Anatolian dishes nobody else bothered to write down, and take the last ferry back with the city lit on both sides. Everything else here is arrangement around that one crossing.

The survivor list. A handful of houses carry the line unbroken. Agora Meyhanesi in Balat has traded since 1890. Yanyalı Fehmi Lokantası in Kadıköy has been open since 1919 — and took a Bib Gourmand in the 2026 MICHELIN selection, putting the oldest surviving lokanta on the Asian side in the same list as the newest fire counter.

THE SOUL OF THE PLACE
Why the ferry is the point

The city's spine is not a street. It is the vapur, the commuter ferry, and everything worth knowing about Istanbul is legible from its deck: the fishermen on the Galata Bridge, tea in tulip glasses from a tray that never spills, the gulls following the boat for simit thrown by passengers who have done it their whole lives. Twenty minutes, the price of a bus ride, and the best view in the city is included.

That geography sets the table. Istanbul has always eaten what came in on the water and what came down from Anatolia, and it has never fully reconciled the two. The restrained, palace-descended cooking — vegetables stuffed in olive oil, fish cooked plainly, sherbet-sweet desserts — is the city's own. The fire, the chilli, the lamb and the bulgur are the south-east's, carried here by twentieth-century migration and now so naturalised that visitors think ocakbaşı means Istanbul. Both are true; neither is the whole city.

The quintessence: Istanbul is a capital that stopped being one. When the Republic moved the capital to Ankara in 1923 the city lost its function and kept its appetite — and for fifty years that appetite was serviced by a merchant class who were then removed from it. The largest city in Europe is still working out what to do with a cuisine whose authors left.

HISTORY
The spine you need for the food to make sense

Founded as Byzantion around 660 BC by settlers from Megara. Refounded by Constantine in 330 AD as the Christian capital of the Roman world, and for eleven centuries the richest city in it. Taken by Mehmed II in 1453 — and here the food history properly begins. Under the Ottoman millet system the empire's non-Muslim communities governed their own affairs, and because alcohol was forbidden to Muslims, the licence to run a drinking house was effectively a non-Muslim one.

For centuries the tavern was therefore a Greek, Armenian or Jewish institution: the long table of cold mezes, the rakı poured with water and ice, the fasıl musicians moving between tables. Meyhane culture spread out of Galata into Beyoğlu, into Asmalımescit, Çiçek Pasajı and Nevizade, and became the thing outsiders now think most Turkish about an Istanbul night.

Then it was dismantled. The Varlık Vergisi — the 1942 wealth tax levied at ruinous rates on non-Muslims — broke the merchant class financially. The pogrom of 6–7 September 1955 broke it physically: two days of organised mob violence against Greek homes, businesses, churches and cemeteries after a false report that Atatürk's birthplace in Thessaloniki had been bombed. Twelve dead, some three hundred injured, and a community of around a hundred thousand reduced over the following decades to a few thousand. The 1964 expulsions of Greek citizens finished it.

Read Çiçek Pasajı with that in mind and it stops being quaint. Built in 1876 as the Cité de Péra, an elegant arcade of European shops; as Pera declined the shops became rough meyhanes with beer barrels rolled onto the pavement and marble slabs laid across them; part of the building collapsed in the late 1970s. It was rebuilt. What could not be rebuilt was the clientele.

The restaurant that came the other way

Yanyalı Fehmi Lokantası has been open in Kadıköy since 1919, founded by Fehmi Efendi, whose family migrated from Yanya — Ioannina, in Greece. Yanyalı means 'from Yanya'. In a city whose Greek community was removed by a wealth tax, a pogrom and an expulsion, the oldest surviving lokanta on the Asian side was founded by a family who came the other way in the same exchange. Third generation, still family-run, 140–150 dishes a day.

WHY NOW
The star crossed the water

The MICHELIN Guide Türkiye published its 2026 selection on 4 December 2025 — 54 new restaurants, 115 recommended venues, the guide extended to Cappadocia for the first time, 17 starred restaurants nationally. In Istanbul that reads: TURK Fatih Tütak holds two stars and took a Green Star in this edition, and seven houses hold one — Araf, Araka, Arkestra, Mikla, Neolokal, Nicole and Sankai by Nagaya. Two Istanbul Bib Gourmands are new: Casius Antioch Kitchen and the 1919 veteran Yanyalı Fehmi.

But the story is Araf. A twelve-seat counter in Kadıköy built around one open fire, cooking offal and large cuts, one seating a night, no booking app — direct contact only. A decade ago this city's prestige sat on the European side in hotel rooftops. It has crossed the water to a room with twelve chairs.

Meanwhile the 18th Istanbul Biennial is in its quiet year. It runs as a three-year project: the first leg ran 20 September to 23 November 2025; 2026 is dedicated to building an academy and a quarterly public programme with local art initiatives; the whole thing culminates September–November 2027. So there is no big biennial exhibition this summer — there is something better, which is the independent spaces doing the programming instead.

WHAT TO EAT
The dishes with an address
  • Balık ekmek — grilled fish in bread. ⚠ The painted boats at Eminönü are a tourist operation; eat the Karaköy or Kadıköy version, from somewhere with a queue of people going to work.
  • Midye dolma — mussels stuffed with spiced rice and lemon, sold from trays after dark. Armenian in origin, now the most Istanbul thing there is.
  • Kokoreç — seasoned offal wrapped and grilled, chopped into bread. The real late-night food and the most divisive.
  • İskender kebap — worth knowing it is not an Istanbul dish. Bursa, 1867, invented by İskender Efendi. Istanbul serves it everywhere and rarely serves it well.
  • Lahmacun and pide — lahmacun with lemon and parsley, rolled. Anything else is an affectation.
  • Mantı — tiny dumplings under garlic yoghurt and chilli butter; the Kayseri version is the standard.
  • Serpme kahvaltı — the sprawled Turkish breakfast, twenty small plates. An institution rather than a meal. This is the Sunday.
  • Boza — fermented millet, thick and faintly sour, with cinnamon and roasted chickpeas. A winter drink. Vefa Bozacısı has sold it since 1876 and the point of going is the room, unchanged.
Hot this month — August 2026

Lina Anatolian has just opened in Karaköy, regional Anatolian through contemporary technique with the strait in the window — the most watched opening of the summer. And Lokanta Stambouli opened in Pera working explicitly in the Turkish and Rum meyhane tradition: mezes, daily fresh seafood, warm courses, in the Union Française building. Given the history above, a Rum meyhane revival opening in 2026 is a loaded act, and it is the most interesting new room in the city.

WHAT TO DRINK
Ask for the native grape
  • Rakı — and more importantly the rakı sofrası, the ritual table where the drink paces the food across hours rather than accompanying it. Poured with water, which turns it white: aslan sütü, lion's milk. Ordering rakı then eating quickly is the commonest visitor mistake.
  • Turkish wine from indigenous grapes — the real discovery, and almost nobody orders it. Öküzgözü and Boğazkere from the east, Kalecik Karası from near Ankara, Narince and Emir for whites, Papazkarası and Çalkarası in Thrace and the Aegean. Ask for the native grape, not the Cabernet.
  • Türk kahvesi — inscribed on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2013, and the inscription is about the ritual as much as the coffee.
  • Çay — the tulip glass, held by the rim, all day, and never refused.
Hot this month — the natural wine turn

The layer thickened noticeably this year: Bağ Pera in Asmalımescit (lunch Mon–Sat, dinner daily, plus tastings and workshops) and Lips in Teşvikiye — closed Sundays — both opened in the current wave. The anchor remains WAYANA in Moda: 500+ labels served entirely by the glass, and as of July 2026, 143 Turkish wineries and 90 endemic grape varieties. Go there first; it will reorganise what you think Turkish wine is.

BOIRE & SORTIR
Listening bars, the night, and the jazz room

Listening bars and the hi-fi turn. Arkaoda in Kadıköy has been the anchor since 1999 — selectors moving between Turkish psych, jazz and electronic, with a garden. Karga runs vinyl sessions upstairs; Nayah works the reggae and dub end. The new generation is June in Teşvikiye and Dama in Nişantaşı — a café by day that turns hi-fi at night — and Frankhan in Karaköy has built a dedicated audiophile Sound Room alongside its main floor.

The night. FLUX in Maslak is the serious techno room, Funktion-One, and the programming actually splits — Fridays lean house, Saturdays go techno; the 2026 season has carried Ben Klock, Anja Schneider, Gerd Janson and Cormac alongside local artists. Şahika in Beyoğlu is multi-level, queer-friendly, with a rooftop terrace and the experimental end of the programming. NOH Radio Bar is the compact one where the music goes into the street, with NOH Extended as its late club and art space. POPSTEL in Galatasaray runs the Stay Local series on Y2K hip-hop, UK garage and dark disco.

Jazz. Nardis Jazz Club at the foot of the Galata Tower is the room — roughly 120 seats, live jazz from local and international acts practically every night, weekdays 21:30–00:30 and weekends 22:30–01:30, already programmed into 2026–27. It is the reason Wednesday is on the week grid.

Hot this month — August 2026

Sazzou has opened inside the Hilton İstanbul Bosphorus in Harbiye — live jazz plus vinyl DJ sets, the first serious new jazz room in a while. And İlhan Erşahin's Istanbul Sessions released their sixth album, Mahalle, on 16 January 2026 via Nublu Records — a record built neighbourhood by neighbourhood across this city. Listen to it before you come; it is the best possible preparation for the ferry. Note the 33rd Istanbul Jazz Festival has already been and gone — it ran 30 June to 13 July.

ROOFTOPS
The hour before sundown

The window is the hour before sundown into blue hour, and they fill. The Bank Roof Bar in Karaköy looks over the Golden Horn, Hagia Sophia, Topkapı and the Blue Mosque, open daily midday to late. Roof Mezzepotamia gives 270° across the Bosphorus and the Golden Horn with DJs nightly — and note it is on the terrace of the Orient Occident in Sirkeci, not Karaköy, which most English-language listings get wrong. Roof106 sits atop the Galata Times Hotel and closes on Tuesdays. Sky Karaköy is on the ninth floor of the JW Marriott, 17:00–01:00, but Marriott's own listing has carried a seasonally closed label — check first. Manifest Roof in Bereketzade is the summer's new entry, dinner from 18:00.

Honest on money: expect roughly 400–700 TL for a cocktail at a hotel rooftop in 2026, and 180–350 TL for a beer or a glass of wine at the more relaxed Karaköy, Galata and Kadıköy roofs. The gap is the view, not the drink.

COLLECTIVES
Where the biennial actually is this year

Istanbul's independent layer is unusually strong, and in 2026 it is doing the work the Biennial is not.

  • Suma Han — Beyoğlu. A creative hub rather than a venue: offices, artist studios, residences and common rooms, plus a club room with a 500-capacity floor and real acoustics. The clearest example in the city of artists building their own infrastructure.
  • KOLİ Art Space — Kadıköy. Founded 2021 by Elçin Acun and Yasemin Kalaycı; a non-profit production and exhibition space supporting feminist and queer artists on identity and gender fluidity. Showed at Contemporary Istanbul CI BLOOM 2026.
  • NOKS — Kadıköy. Began in 2015 as TOZ, founded by Ece Elder, Elvan Ekren, Sinem Dişli and Volkan Kızıltunç in their own studio; NOKS after 2018, run by Kızıltunç and Ekren.
  • BAS and AVTO — the long-running independent havens: artist books and experimental programming.
  • Nublu Istanbul / Nublu Records — İlhan Erşahin's label and room, founded 2005 as an extension of the Nublu sound from New York's Lower East Side.
Hot this month

With the 18th Biennial in its academy year, its 2026 programme is explicitly built in collaboration with local art initiatives — meaning the spaces above are where the biennial actually is. Check what each is running the week you arrive; there is no central listing that covers them all, and that is rather the point.

FLÂNER
The city on foot
  • Kadıköy — the Asian side, and the centre of gravity of everything good happening now. Market streets by day, Kadife Sokak and Barlar Sokağı by night, Moda for the sea walk, Yeldeğirmeni for the murals and the quieter rooms.
  • Balat and Fener — the old Greek and Jewish quarters on the Golden Horn: steep, coloured, increasingly photographed. Go early.
  • Kuzguncuk — the single loveliest street in Istanbul: a mosque, a synagogue and a Greek Orthodox church along the same short road, wooden houses, small bakeries, tiny art spaces. The physical proof of the city this guide keeps describing.
  • Arnavutköy to Bebek — pastel wooden yalı on the water, then a 3 km walk north through narrow streets into Bebek. The best hour on the European shore.
  • Büyükada, Princes' Islands — about 5 × 1 km, no cars, wooden houses of a kind vanishing on the mainland, and the Monastery of St George on the hill. Roughly 90 minutes by ferry from the European piers, 45 from the Asian side.
Hot this month

August is when Istanbul's own residents leave for the coast — so the Bosphorus villages and the islands are at their most crowded with day-trippers while the city centre is at its emptiest. Invert the normal advice: Beyoğlu and Karaköy at the weekend, the islands midweek.

BEFORE YOU GO
What to skip
  • The Eminönü fish-bread boats. Photogenic, industrialised, and not what anyone local eats.
  • Sultanahmet as a dining district. The greatest concentration of monuments in Europe and the worst concentration of restaurants in this guide's area. Eat before or after, not there.
  • Ordering İskender in Istanbul and believing you have eaten the dish. Bursa, 1867.
  • Anywhere with a man outside the door inviting you in. Universal rule; absolute in Beyoğlu.
  • August itself, if you have a choice. Hot, humid, and the month the city's own kitchens take their holiday. May–June and September–October are when this city is best.
