General information, checked on 1 September 2026 against the guides and the Convention text named at the end; not legal or financial advice. Pay is set by contract, flag and vessel. Before you sign, have the Seafarer Employment Agreement read by Nautilus International, the PYA or a maritime employment lawyer.

The Situation

The dock quotes one monthly number for the job. In 2026 there are three, and the hours behind them are the part nobody quotes.

Ask on the pontoon what a chef earns on a 50-metre and you get a round figure followed by “plus tips, all in, tax-free”. Each word does something different: the figure is a recruiter's ceiling, the tips are discretionary under the charter industry's own guidance, and “all in” is not a term the SEA recognises.

What changed is that the guides multiplied and then converged. Two of the 2026 euro guides, YPI Crew's and Northrop & Johnson's, print the same galley rows band for band; a third, Lighthouse Careers', runs lower under 50 metres and up to €2,500 a month higher above it. The number you quote depends on whose PDF you opened, and the recruiter opposite knows which one.

The Convention behind every yacht contract carries two ceilings, not one: a flag may cap work at 72 hours a week, or guarantee 77 hours of rest, which permits 91 (MLC 2006, Standard A2.3, paragraph 5). The UK applies the rest version (MSN 1877 (M) Amendment 2, paragraph 4.2.2). Divide the monthly figure by the ceiling your flag chose before you call it a good number.

The Rules and Reality

Five contract lines, two hour ceilings, one leave floor, one wage floor. The Convention does not set a chef's pay; it sets how the pay is written, paid and worked.

Key Concept One — the agreement and the monthly account

Standard A2.1, paragraph 4, lists what every seafarer employment agreement must contain; five lines are the whole negotiation: wages or the formula for them (e), paid annual leave or its formula (f), termination and notice (g), health and social security benefits (h), repatriation (i). Notice is never shorter than seven days, nor shorter for the owner than for you (paragraphs 4(g) and 5). Wages arrive at least monthly, with a written account of what was due and paid, exchange rate included (Standard A2.2, paragraphs 1–2).

The hours mechanism: Standard A2.3, paragraph 5, lets a flag fix either a maximum of 14 hours' work in any 24 and 72 in any seven days, or a minimum of ten hours' rest in any 24 and 77 in seven, in no more than two periods, one of six hours (paragraph 6). The two are not equivalent: 168 minus 77 is 91. A chef on a UK-flag yacht can lawfully be worked 91 hours a week; under a maximum-hours flag, 72. At the YPI Crew and Northrop & Johnson 60–80 m band, €7,000–9,000 a month, that is €18–23 an hour at the 91-hour ceiling and €22–29 at the 72-hour one, before tips.

Two monthly figures compare only once you know which ceiling each flag applies.

Leave is a floor, not a perk: 2.5 calendar days for every month of employment (Standard A2.4, paragraph 2) — 30 days a year — plus eight public-holiday days on the UK flag (MSN 1877, paragraphs 10.2–10.3). An offer listing “38 days' leave” is quoting you the statutory minimum.

Key Concept Two — the wage floor and the tip regime

There is a statutory wage floor, and it does not protect a chef. The Joint Maritime Commission agreed on 16 April 2025 to raise the minimum basic wage for an able seafarer from US$673 to US$690 a month from 1 January 2026, US$704 in 2027, US$715 in 2028. That buys at most 48 hours a week, overtime at 1.25 times the rate or more (Guideline B2.2.2, paragraph 1): even at the 91-hour ceiling, roughly US$1,460, under a third of the lowest chef band in any 2026 guide. The market sets your number; the Convention sets how it is written down.

  1. Food — free of charge for the engagement (Regulation 3.2, paragraph 2).
  2. Medical care and sick pay — costs for at least 16 weeks; full wages while sick on board (Standard A4.2.1, paragraphs 1(c), 2 and 3(a)).
  3. The flight home — at no cost when the agreement expires abroad or the owner ends it (Regulation 2.5, paragraph 1; Standard A2.5.1).

Tips are a different regime. MYBA's guidance to charterers is a gratuity of 5–15% of the contracted gross charter fee if the crew has given excellent service, handed to the captain to divide across the crew; lower end in the Mediterranean, higher in the United States and Caribbean (Boat International, 21 December 2021). None of it is in the SEA; none of it is enforceable.

Those are the rules on paper. Here is what they mean the day the offer lands.

The only number a sick-pay claim, a notice period or a port-state inspector is calculated on is the one in the SEA. Everything said beside it is weather.

Find Your Case

One rule, four cases, two currencies. Read yours and skip the rest.

Case One — the sole chef under 40 m, Mediterranean, charter, non-rotational

One chef, crew and guests, every service. The euro guides put 30–40 m at €5,000–7,500 (YPI Crew; Northrop & Johnson) or €4,500–6,500 (Lighthouse Careers); under 30 m at €4,500–6,000, where Lighthouse lists only a chef/stew at €4,000–6,000 — the two-jobs-one-salary ad decoded in the hybrid piece. The crew list usually sits under ten, so the ship's cook certificate is not yet the condition of the job; the Level 2 hygiene card is.

  • Base and hours — the SEA line, paid monthly (Standard A2.2, paragraph 1), buying up to 91 hours a week on a UK flag. Where the wage is consolidated, the agreement should state the hours it covers (Guideline B2.2.2, paragraph 2(a)); a guideline, so ask for the sentence.
  • Tips — 5–15% of the gross charter fee, split across the whole crew by the captain; a fixed share per charter week exists only if the contract says so.

The all-in offer: say €8,000 a month on a busy charter boat where the SEA reads €5,500 and the rest is a tip estimate. MYBA's guidance makes the gratuity discretionary and conditional on excellent service; a yard period, a cancelled season or an owner's summer aboard drops the package to the SEA line, and the SEA line is also what your sick pay and your notice are calculated on. The real cost is the difference, every month the boat does not charter.

The Lighter Comparison Set

Private, non-rotational

Base and package only: no MYBA regime, no tips to estimate, none to lose. Foreland Marine's 2026 guide says crew moving from charter to private “may expect a higher base salary to compensate for the absence of tip income”; it puts no number on the premium, and neither should you until it is in the SEA.

Charter

Base plus a discretionary 5–15% of the gross charter fee, divided by the captain across every crew member: fee × percentage ÷ headcount. A €150,000 week at 10% across twelve crew is €1,250 each — put your own boat's fee and headcount in. Foreland Marine's “20–50% a year” is a management company's synthesis, not a survey.

Rotational (2:2, mostly 60 m and up)

Two salaries per role, each paid every month. YPI Crew: yachts “continue to offer rotation, at all levels in the galley”; Foreland Marine: each rotational salary “typically 10–15 percent lower”; a January 2025 trade piece built on job posts called rotation “still rare”. Three weak sources pointing two ways: count the rotational ads on your own size of boat before you price the life back.

Sole chef or brigade

A sous chef first appears at 40–50 m (Northrop & Johnson), 50–60 m (YPI Crew), 50–70 m (Lighthouse); below that one chef carries crew and guests. Above, every guide puts the sous band at about two-thirds of the head band (60–80 m: €5,000–6,000 under €7,000–9,000), with a crew or third chef at €4,000–6,000 from 70–80 m.

Those cases are priced in euros because the guides that carry them are Mediterranean guides; the same publisher prints a second edition for a second market.

Where the Money Lives

Mediterranean — the euro editions

YPI Crew and Northrop & Johnson's euro guide print identical galley rows (head chef 50–60 m: €6,500–9,000); Lighthouse Careers runs higher from 50 m (€7,500–10,000). Gratuities at the lower end of MYBA's 5–15%.

United States and Caribbean — the dollar edition

Northrop & Johnson's 2026 US-dollar edition: head chef 50–60 m US$10,000–13,000, 60–80 m US$10,000–15,000, 80 m+ US$15,000+; sous US$5,000–9,000+. Against €6,500–9,000 in the same publisher's euro edition, a second market, not a conversion. Gratuities at the higher end.

And the rack's payoff: the ladder by metre, in euros a month, the guide that disagrees printed on each rung.

The Package, Priced

Every perk against the paragraph that guarantees it, or the contract line that must — priced from your own numbers.

The guides price base only; Lighthouse says so in its footnote. The package is where two offers with one headline part company, and the only honest way to price it is line by line from your own numbers — the fare from Antibes to your front door, your insurer's quote — not a rule of thumb.

Build Your Number

Six lines — base by size, the modifiers as ranges, the hourly at the ceiling — and the answer is a number you can say out loud.

Run it before the call, not during it. Where the guides disagree, write both: the lower is the floor you will not go under, the higher is the ask.

The Practical Path

Substance over the headline: get every figure that matters onto the page the Convention says must carry it.

A number exists only where an inspector could find it. Standard A2.1, paragraph 1(b), gives you the right to examine the agreement and seek advice before signing; use it.

  1. Read the flag before the figure. 72 or 91 hours, which leave floor, and the sentence stating the hours a consolidated wage covers (Guideline B2.2.2, paragraph 2(a)).
  2. Gross, in a named currency, on a named day. Standard A2.2 gives you the monthly account with the exchange rate. A “net” figure means nothing until you know whose tax it is net of; the deduction rules are in the tax piece.
  3. Put the tip regime in writing, or leave it out of the number. “X% of the charter fee, paid per charter” is a contract term; “discretionary, end of season” is MYBA's default and a wish.
  4. Get every promise beyond the floor into the SEA: leave above 30 days, flights, private cover, the training budget, the rotation pattern.
  5. Don't take advice at the dock bar. The 38-metre alongside quotes a different rung, a different flag and possibly a different guide; read your own.

This week: ask for the SEA before the call, find the five lines of Standard A2.1, paragraph 4, in it, and price the gap between what it says and what was said.

The Cost of Getting It Wrong

The mistake costs a season's difference; the fix costs an evening with a PDF and a contract.

Take the all-in offer from The Trap. If the boat charters twelve weeks instead of twenty, the tip estimate that made €5,500 look like €8,000 shrinks by forty per cent while the SEA line stays where it was written; fall ill in October and sick pay is calculated on €5,500 (Standard A4.2.1, paragraph 3), and so is your notice. Getting it right costs one evening: the ladder, the flag's ceiling, the package table and the five lines in the agreement.

The worst case is not a lowball. It is a good salary quoted on the wrong denominator — a 91-hour week, a discretionary tip and a leave floor sold as a perk — signed because the round number on the pontoon sounded like the one on the page.

That bill is not paid on the pontoon; it is paid by whoever is standing at the pass at eleven at night.

The person it lands on is the chef wiring money home on the fourth of the month, who counted the tip estimate into the school fees and the flight to see a parent, and who finds in a quiet September that the boat has kept every promise it wrote down. The money is for the people you feed and the people you send it to; put it where the Convention can see it.

The One Thing

Quote yourself from the rung, price the package from your own numbers, and sign only the figure in the SEA: the Convention enforces that line and nothing said beside it.
