The 50 Best 2026: Who Won, Who Paid, and What the Vote Cannot Prove

Asador Etxebarri was named number one in Lima on 4 November. How the 1,100-voter poll is actually made, what the chefs who walked away from it say, what the rules guard and what they cannot, and how a working chef should read it.

Read the 2026 list as a real vote held inside a business that tourism boards pay to host: the ballots are audited, the money around them is not published, and that gap is the honest question to ask of it.

On 4 November 2026, at the Teatro Municipal in Lima, Asador Etxebarri was named the best restaurant in the world: the grill in Atxondo, in the Basque hills, where Bittor Arguinzoniz does the cooking himself. This piece covers what the release says, how the vote is made, who pays for what around it, and what none of that can prove. Facts checked 5 and 6 November 2026. Not one host fee behind the ceremony has been made public.

The release

A Basque grill on top, Bangkok everywhere, America nearly gone.

The top ten, as announced:

  • 1. Asador Etxebarri, Atxondo · 2. Gaggan, Bangkok · 3. Alchemist, Copenhagen · 4. Quintonil, Mexico City · 5. Wing, Hong Kong
  • 6. The Chairman, Hong Kong · 7. Kjolle, Lima · 8. Kadeau, Copenhagen · 9. DiverXO, Madrid · 10. Plénitude, Paris

Bangkok has five places in the fifty, more than any city. The United States has one, Atomix at 11, for the first time. Disfrutar and Maido, the last two winners, are missing by rule. Etxebarri was second last year, and bettors on the prediction market Kalshi gave it 62 per cent before the envelope opened.

Twenty-four years in one paragraph

A magazine feature that became the trade's calendar.

The list began in 2002 as a feature in Restaurant, a British trade magazine owned by the same publisher that runs it today; elBulli was its first number one and won again in 2006, 2007, 2008 and 2009. Noma broke that run in 2010 and won in 2011, 2012, 2014 and 2021. Two kitchens held the top for ten of the first twenty years, which is the context for the 2019 rule that retires every winner: TIME reported that it came from chefs' lobbying, former winners among them, not from the organisers. Since then the list works as a queue: a winner leaves, the field moves up, and this year the runner-up took it.

How the vote is made

Ten names per voter, eaten in the last eighteen months, and no inspector anywhere.

The Academy is split into 28 regions, each with a chair who recruits the voters: more than 1,100 chefs, restaurateurs, writers and frequent diners, half of them women, a quarter replaced every year. Each names ten restaurants in order, eaten in the last eighteen months, no more than six in their own country, none they own or have money in. Only the chairs are named. Deloitte adjudicates. The organiser calls the result "an annual snapshot of the opinions and experiences of our experts". In 2013 it was blunter: "There are no criteria."

Three other ways to rank a kitchen

Each method measures something different; a restaurant high on all of them is rarely an accident.

Read the 50 Best beside the systems built on other principles:

  • Michelin employs its own inspectors, who dine anonymously, pay their bill and judge on five stated criteria, from the quality of the produce to consistency between visits.
  • La Liste, launched in Paris in 2015, has no voters: an algorithm averages guides, press reviews and diner reviews, weighting each source by how far it trusts it.
  • Opinionated About Dining polls nearly 5,000 members and gives their votes unequal weight: the more, and the better, restaurants a member has eaten in, the more that ballot counts.

The 50 Best is the only one of the four where every ballot weighs the same and the voter's travel may be paid by someone else. A kitchen that sits high on Michelin, La Liste and OAD as well has been judged by paid inspectors, by the press and by heavy diners at once; one that sits high only on the 50 Best has, at the least, been reached by its voters.

What a ranking is worth

Real money for the restaurant, and almost no measurement of how much.

Three years after its first win, Noma was still receiving more than 100,000 booking enquiries a month, according to the list's own publisher. The only careful price evidence is for Michelin: in New York, each star was associated with menu prices about 30 per cent higher, partly through spending on décor and wine. We found no peer-reviewed study of what a 50 Best rank does to bookings or prices. That gap matters: the demand a ranking creates is the reason a tourism board pays to host it, and nobody outside the business has measured it.

Follow the money

Four kinds of money touch the list; the 50 publishes none of the amounts.

  • The owner. The 50 belongs to William Reed, a British trade publisher. In September it renamed the brand to "strengthen connections between consumers, hospitality businesses and commercial partners".
  • The sponsor. S.Pellegrino and Acqua Panna, Nestlé's mineral waters, have their name on the ceremony. The 50 says no partner votes, influences the vote or sees the list before it is published.
  • The host. National tourism boards underwrite the ceremony and the week around it. Tourism Australia's 2017 sponsorship was reported at about US$600,000; Singapore's three-year deal for 2019 was never priced, the board citing "business confidentiality". This year Abu Dhabi's tourism department was announced as partner in February, then dropped, reportedly over the conflict in the region; Lima took the ceremony with PromPerú, which has published a forecast of about US$2 million in local spending and no fee.
  • The voters' trips. The 50 pays for no voter's meal, flight or room, and voters may accept free food and travel from tourism boards and promotion groups. The New York Times reported this week that the voters "are not anonymous".

What the money can and cannot buy

The rules guard the ballot; they cannot guard the invitation.

We found no public evidence of a bought vote, and the organiser's defence is real: an auditor checks the ballots. But Deloitte can check that a ballot is valid, not why that voter was in that dining room or who paid the flight. The list is not rigged at the ballot box; it is shaped before it, by who gets invited, flown and fed. Whether hosting moves votes cannot be proved from the outside, because the published list gives ranks, not voters.

Success within the ranking has come to depend increasingly on visibility within the system itself.

She stayed home from Lima. Bruno Verjus and Leonor Espinosa agreed under her post; Tom Colicchio wrote: "Someone said the quiet part out loud." Four disclosures would settle most of the doubt, and the 50 publishes none of them:

  1. The host fee, every year, with the name of the board that paid it.
  2. How many votes came from hosted meals, as a share, without naming anyone.
  3. The vote counts behind each rank, so a reader can see a landslide from a tie.
  4. The voter list, released a year after each vote.

How to read it from the galley

As a map of where the trade is looking. Then check the kitchen yourself.

Guests will ask about the list and book from it, so know it. For a stage, it shows where cooks are travelling to learn this year: Bangkok, Lima, Copenhagen. To judge a kitchen, it tells you almost nothing: eat there, or ask a cook who has worked the line. When a restaurant drops off, look first for the hall of fame, then for a change of chef, and last for a fall in the cooking. If what you want from Disfrutar is the technique, the volumes are on the modernist shelf.
