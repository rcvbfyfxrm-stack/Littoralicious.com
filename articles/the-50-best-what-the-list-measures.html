The 50 Best: What the List Measures, Who Votes, and What It Cannot Tell You

Asador Etxebarri was named number one in Lima on 4 November. How the 1,100-voter poll is actually made, what the chefs who walked away from it say, what the rules guard and what they cannot, and how a working chef should read it.

The 50 Best is a poll of about 1,100 people on where they ate best in the last eighteen months, not an inspection: read it as a map of where the trade is travelling, never as a score for a kitchen.

On 4 November 2026, at the Teatro Municipal in Lima, Asador Etxebarri was named number one: the grill in Atxondo, in the Basque hills, where Bittor Arguinzoniz does the cooking himself. This piece is how that list is made, what its critics say, and which parts of each side hold up. Facts were checked on 5 and 6 November 2026 against the organiser's own rules and the reporting from the week. Nothing in the rules asks a voter to have paid for the meal they vote for.

How the vote works

Ten names per voter, eaten in the last eighteen months, and no inspector anywhere.

The Academy is split into 28 regions, each with a chair who recruits the voters: more than 1,100 chefs, restaurateurs, writers and frequent diners, half of them women, a quarter replaced every year. Each voter names ten restaurants in order of preference, eaten in the last eighteen months, no more than six in their own country, none they own or have money in. Only the chairs are named. Deloitte adjudicates.

Since 2019, a restaurant that reaches number one leaves the annual list for good, for a hall of fame called the Best of the Best. TIME reported that the rule came from chefs' lobbying, former winners among them, not from the organisers. That is why Disfrutar (2024) and Maido (2025) are missing this year; their absence says nothing about their cooking.

What it actually measures

Where well-travelled people chose to eat, and remembered it.

The organiser says so itself: the list is "an annual snapshot of the opinions and experiences of our experts". In 2013 its organiser was blunter: "There are no criteria." Nobody tastes against a standard. A restaurant rises when enough voters have eaten there recently and liked it best, so before a kitchen can be voted for, it has to be reached.

The 2026 list shows what that does. Read it by city:

  • Bangkok, five. Gaggan at 2, Sorn, Sühring, Nusara and Potong: more than any other city.
  • Copenhagen and Lima, three each. Alchemist, Kadeau, Jordnær; Kjolle, Mérito, Mayta.
  • The United States, one. Atomix in New York at 11, the first year with a single American restaurant in the fifty.
  • Spain, four. Etxebarri, DiverXO at 9, Enigma at 29, Elkano at 33.

That is our reading, not a measurement: a city that voters fly to, with a tourism board that brings them, fills the list faster than a city with the same cooking that nobody visits.

What the critics say

Hosted trips, host-city money, and visibility counted as much as the cooking.

Three complaints come back every year, and this year each had a fresh example.

  • The trips. Unlike Michelin, the 50 does not pay for its voters' meals, flights or rooms, and voters may accept free food and travel from tourism boards and other promotional groups. The New York Times put it flatly this week: the voters "are not anonymous".
  • The host. In February the 2026 ceremony was announced for Abu Dhabi; chefs including Dominique Crenn objected on human rights and said they would not go. In July it moved to Lima, underwritten by PromPerú, Peru's promotion board, which projected about US$2 million of local spending. In September the owner, the publisher William Reed, renamed the brand The 50, to "strengthen connections between consumers, hospitality businesses and commercial partners".
  • The visibility. On 21 October Ana Roš, whose Hiša Franko had been on the list every year since 2017 and stood at 69 in 2025, said she would not travel to Lima. "Success within the ranking has come to depend increasingly on visibility within the system itself," she wrote. Bruno Verjus and Leonor Espinosa agreed under her post; Tom Colicchio wrote: "Someone said the quiet part out loud."

What holds up on both sides

The rules guard the ballot; they cannot guard the invitation.

The organiser's defence is real: sponsors and staff do not vote, partners do not see the list early, and an auditor checks the ballots. We found no public evidence of a bought vote. But Deloitte can check that a ballot is valid, not why that voter was in that dining room or who paid the flight. The list is not rigged at the ballot box; it is shaped before it, by who gets invited, flown and fed. Critics and organiser are describing two stages of one process.

Before the ceremony, bettors on the prediction market Kalshi gave Etxebarri a 62 per cent chance of winning. People bet on it like an election because it is one: last year's runner-up, with the winner retired, was the obvious next name.

How to read it from the galley

As a map of where the trade is looking. Then check the kitchen yourself.

Guests will ask about the list and book from it, so know it. For a stage, it shows where cooks are travelling to learn this year: Bangkok, Lima, Copenhagen. To judge a kitchen, it tells you almost nothing: eat there, or ask a cook who has worked the line. When a restaurant drops off, look first for the hall of fame, then for a change of chef, and last for a fall in the cooking. If what you want from Disfrutar is the technique, the volumes are on the modernist shelf.
