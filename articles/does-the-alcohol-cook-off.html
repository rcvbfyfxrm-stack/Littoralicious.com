The Lab: Does the Alcohol Cook Off

The belief that wine and spirits burn away in the pan, put under a published test: the USDA retention study that measured what stays — from 85 per cent to 5 — by method and by minute.

  1. The Hypothesis“The alcohol cooks off” — stated as a prediction that can fail.
  2. The MethodTen conditions, one assay, and the stirring trap.
  3. Trials & ResultsAll ten rows as the USDA prints them: 85 per cent to 5 — and the flambé that kept three quarters.
  4. What Held UpThe direction, the failed shortcuts, the honest limits, and the gram-a-portion arithmetic.

The Hypothesis

The belief everyone repeats in the galley — and the one time anyone weighed it.

Every galley has said it to a guest who does not drink, a pregnant charterer or a child's parent: the alcohol cooks off. The mechanism offered is the boiling point — ethanol boils at 78 °C, water at 100, so the ethanol goes first and goes completely. The variable to isolate is the cooking method — time on the heat, stirred or not, flamed or not — with the controlled variable being the measurement itself: the same alcohol assay on every dish. We did not run this test; the United States Department of Agriculture did, and it publishes the result as a table anyone can rerun.

The prediction: if the boiling-point story is right, a dish simmered for fifteen minutes or set alight keeps under 10 per cent of the alcohol it was made with. Anything above that, and the belief fails.

The Method

A protocol from 1992 that a competent chef could rerun tomorrow with a chromatograph and a saucepan.

The USDA's Table of Nutrient Retention Factors (Release 6, 2007) carries a set of factors for ethyl alcohol built on an analytical study by Augustin and colleagues, published in the Journal of the American Dietetic Association in 1992: dishes were prepared with alcohol under ten defined conditions — no heat, stirred into a hot liquid, flamed, and baked or simmered for set times — and the alcohol left in the food was measured by gas–liquid chromatography with 2-propanol as the internal standard.

Variable Setting Why Fixed
Measurement GLC, 2-propanol standard One assay for every dish, so the methods are compared on the same scale.
Heat treatment 10 conditions The variable under test: none, stirred into hot liquid, flamed, baked or simmered 15 min to 2.5 h, and one un-stirred bake.
Time on heat 15 min – 2.5 h Six steps, so the shape of the curve shows, not just two ends.
Trials per condition not stated The retention table publishes the factor, not the replicate count; the count sits in the 1992 paper.

The factor that fakes a result if you leave it loose is stirring. The table carries it as its own condition: alcohol stirred into a baked dish for 15 minutes kept 40 per cent; alcohol not stirred in and baked for 25 minutes kept 45. Surface and mixing decide how fast ethanol can leave, and a recipe that says “add the wine” without saying how is not one test but two.

Trials & Results

All ten conditions, as the USDA prints them — nothing dropped, including the ones that embarrass the belief.

Read the grid as per cent of the alcohol that was put in and is still in the dish. The prediction passes only if the number is under 10.

5001 No heat, stored overnight 0 70% Fail A third gone with no cooking at all — evaporation from an open dish.
5002 Stirred into a hot liquid < 1 min 85% Fail The wine added at the end of a sauce: almost all of it stays.
5003 Flamed seconds 75% Fail The flambé burns a quarter of the alcohol and all of the belief.

5005 Stirred, baked or simmered 30 min 35% Fail The curve flattens fast after the first quarter-hour.
5006 Stirred, baked or simmered 1 h 25% Fail A braise at the one-hour mark.
5007 Stirred, baked or simmered 1.5 h 20% Fail  
5008 Stirred, baked or simmered 2 h 10% Borderline The prediction's own threshold, reached after two hours.
5009 Stirred, baked or simmered 2.5 h 5% Pass The only condition that does what the belief says every condition does.
5010 Not stirred in, baked 25 min 45% Fail Longer than 5004, more alcohol kept: mixing beats minutes.

The headline is the shape, not one number: one condition in ten passed the prediction, and it took two and a half hours. Nothing under an hour on the heat got below a quarter, and the two methods a chef reaches for to “burn it off” — the flame and the splash into a hot pan — kept 75 and 85 per cent (USDA Table of Nutrient Retention Factors, Release 6, codes 5001–5010).

The flambé. The most theatrical way of removing alcohol from a dish removed a quarter of it: three quarters of the brandy is still in the pan when the flame dies.

Ethanol boils at 78 °C on its own, but in a sauce it is not on its own. A mixture of ethanol and water boils somewhere between the two pure points and its vapour carries both; as the ethanol thins, each minute's vapour carries less of it, so the last fractions leave slowly — the flattening curve in the grid. A flame only burns the vapour above the surface: it cannot reach the ethanol still dissolved in the liquid, which is most of it. And the surface is where all of it leaves, which is why stirring and pan width matter more than the clock.

What Held Up

What replicated across the grid, what cracked, and the exact condition that breaks the result.

What replicated: the direction. Every extra step on the heat took alcohol out, and it never went back — 40, 35, 25, 20, 10, 5 per cent at 15 minutes, 30 minutes, one hour, one and a half, two, two and a half. Time in the pan is the one thing that reliably removes most of it.

What did not hold: the belief's two shortcuts. Flaming (75 per cent kept) and stirring into a hot liquid (85 per cent) removed less than an uncooked dish left overnight (70 per cent). The one condition that reached the prediction's threshold needed two and a half hours, which is a braise, not a sauce.

Finding Held In Confidence
Alcohol falls with time on the heat, without reversal 6 / 6 time points High — monotonic across the whole series.
“Cooked off” (under 10%) within an hour 0 / 3 Did not hold — 40, 35 and 25 per cent at 15, 30 and 60 minutes.
A flambé removes most of the alcohol 0 / 1 Did not hold — 75 per cent kept.

The limits: one laboratory, one study, published in 1992; the retention table gives the factor per condition but not the number of replicates, and the dishes behind each code — their pan, their volume, their alcohol — sit in the paper, not the table. What would falsify it is a replication showing under 10 per cent at fifteen minutes; none is in the table.

Do this: when the table includes someone who is not drinking, count the wine as still in the dish. A 200 ml glass of 13 per cent wine carries about 20 g of ethanol; simmered and stirred for 30 minutes it keeps 35 per cent, about 7 g in the pan, roughly a gram a portion across six. Say so, or cook it two and a half hours, or leave it out.

Conclusion

Did it hold? No — and the one thing it changes at the pass.

The hypothesis failed nine conditions out of ten. Alcohol does not cook off; it cooks down, slowly, in proportion to time and surface, and the flame that looks like the end of it is not. The one usable change is a sentence: a wine sauce, a beurre blanc finished with a splash, a flamed dessert — each is an alcoholic dish, and the guest who asked deserves the number, not the belief.

Bench Notes

  • Verdict — did not hold: nine of ten cooking conditions kept more than a tenth of the alcohol.
  • The number — 75 per cent after a flambé, 40 per cent after fifteen minutes' simmer, 5 per cent only after two and a half hours (USDA Release 6, codes 5001–5010; replicate count unpublished in the table).
  • Do this — tell the non-drinker; if the sauce must be dry, simmer it into the braise, not the pan.

The test is the USDA's, not ours; the arithmetic on the glass is ours. Tested at sea.

The one thing: the flame removes a quarter; the clock removes the rest, and it needs hours. Until then the wine is still in the dish.
