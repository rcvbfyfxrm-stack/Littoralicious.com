Two Eggs, Two Temperatures: The Yolk Sets Before the White Firms

A firm poached white needs its bulk protein set, and that takes 80 °C or more; a custard yolk has set by 70. No bath sits in two places at once, so the hundred-cover egg is cooked ahead, in two lines.

The Problem

The usual way asks one pot to do two jobs; the protein refuses.

The usual way is to poach to order — a swirl of vinegared water, one egg at a time — or, since the circulator arrived, to hold a tray of eggs at 63 °C (145 °F) for the whole brunch and call it poached. Both break at volume. To order, one cook pushes out eight or ten before the whites feather and the pass backs up. At 63 °C the yolk is a custard, but the white’s bulk protein has not set, and the egg slides off the muffin in a milky puddle. A moving galley makes both worse: the swirl needs a still pot, and a still pot is the one thing a beam sea will not give you. There is a better move, and a French brigade printed it in 1907.

A 63 °C egg can never be a firm poached egg. Ovalbumin is 54% of the white’s protein (Mine 1995), and by calorimetry it does not unfold until 84 °C (Donovan et al. 1975) — no hold at 63 touches it. Serve that egg in a bowl, never on toast. The rolling boil breaks the other way: at 100 °C the outer white toughens and shreds before the centre has warmed.

The Technique

The Move

Decide the texture first; the temperature follows, and so does the line.

Two lines, one decision. An egg is two protein systems that set at different temperatures: the white’s first protein, ovotransferrin, from about 62 °C (144 °F); the yolk between 65 and 70; the white’s bulk, ovalbumin, at 80 to 84 (176–183 °F). Choose the texture the dish wants, and the texture chooses the bath. For a plate — a white that holds its shape round a liquid yolk — poach short and hot at 80–85 °C: the outside crosses ovalbumin’s set point long before the centre warms; ice stops the cook where it stands; a warm tray brings the egg back to order. For a bowl — custard yolk, loose silky white — hold the egg in its shell at 63 °C, where the yolk sets and the white’s bulk never will.

You are not poaching faster. You are choosing which of two textures the dish wants, then cooking that one ahead. The yolk sets before the bulk of the white does: its proteins aggregate sharply above 69 °C (Le Denmat et al. 1999) while ovalbumin waits for 84 (Donovan et al. 1975). That inversion is the whole reason one bath cannot serve both.

None of this is new. The make-ahead line is older than the thermometer that now runs it.

Escoffier’s brigade had it in print by 1907. Le Guide Culinaire’s notice on œufs pochés: water held at 95 °C with 10 g of salt and a spoon of vinegar per litre, three minutes, then rafraîchir, trim, and hold the eggs in hot salted water at 6 g per litre until the plate calls. Poach, shock, hold, reheat — a century before the circulator. The other line belongs to Japan’s hot-spring towns, where onsen tamago is cooked by the spring itself, at whatever temperature the ground holds.

The Steps

One line for the plate, one for the bowl; numbers, not adjectives.

  1. Strain every egg. Crack each into a fine-mesh strainer and swirl a few seconds: the thin outer white drains, the thick white stays on the yolk. That is the vortex’s job done for any number of eggs, and why a week-old egg still poaches clean — a white’s pH climbs from about 7.6 toward 9 as carbon dioxide leaves through the shell (Sharp & Powell 1931), a thin, alkaline white feathers, and the strainer removes what age has thinned.
  2. Par-poach at 82 °C (180 °F), six to eight at a time, 2 to 2½ minutes — small bubbles rising, never a boil; anything from 80 to 85 works. The surface white crosses ovalbumin’s set point and firms while the yolk’s centre has barely warmed. Pull them thirty seconds under done; the tray will finish them.
  3. Shock in ice water at once, then trim the wisps. Hold submerged in fresh cold water at 5 °C (41 °F) or below, up to three days — our cap, not a regulator’s; the reasoning is in the numbers below.
  4. Fire to order in a perforated tray lowered into water at 66 °C (150 °F) for three minutes; lift the whole tray, let it drip, and rest each egg on a towel before it touches the plate. The tray holds twenty minutes without the yolk setting — 66 sits under the yolk’s 69 °C line and above the 57 °C (135 °F) hot-holding floor.
  5. For the bowl, the other line: eggs in the shell at 63 °C (145 °F) for 45 to 60 minutes. The centre takes half an hour to arrive, so 45 is the floor; past the hour the yolk keeps thickening, because its texture runs on time as well as temperature (Vega & Mercadé-Prieto 2011). Crack straight into the dashi, the rice or the ramen.

Two probes, not one dial: one in the poach pan, one in the fire tray. An induction top’s dial names a setting, not a temperature; the probe names the temperature, and 82 and 66 are the two numbers written on the tape.

Once the two lines are in the hands, the same decision flexes across the plates a charter actually orders:

  • Toast, muffin, hash (Benedict, royale): the plate line only — a white that holds needs ovalbumin set, and ovalbumin has to see 80 °C; nothing held at 63 will do it.
  • Broth, rice, grains (ramen, dashi, the lunch bowl): the shell-on line straight from the bath — the loose white is wanted; it dissolves into the liquid and the custard yolk does the enriching.
  • The hybrid plate: hold at 63, crack, drain the loose white, then 45 to 60 seconds in the 82 °C pan — a thin skin of set ovalbumin over a custard yolk. The most delicate pickup on this page: a slotted spoon and a towel, never tongs.

Why It Works

By differential scanning calorimetry, egg white at pH 7 shows two peaks: ovotransferrin (conalbumin) at 65 °C, ovalbumin at 84 (Donovan et al. 1975, J. Sci. Food Agric.). Ovalbumin is 54% of the white’s protein, ovotransferrin 12% (Mine 1995, Trends Food Sci. Technol.): a 63 °C bath sets a twelfth of the white and leaves the bulk liquid. The yolk keeps its own clock: yolk and its plasma are unchanged below 69 °C, then solubility falls and viscosity rises sharply as the proteins aggregate; the granules hold to 76 (Le Denmat, Anton & Gandemer 1999, J. Food Sci.). The texture reached depends on time as well as temperature (Vega & Mercadé-Prieto 2011, Food Biophysics). Strength: strong; the 62 °C onset is a cook’s observation (Baldwin 2012).

Those numbers hold on a bench that stands still. At sea the water moves and the hob lies:

On a beam sea an open poach pan sloshes and a tray of ice water goes over the sole. Use a deep pan two-thirds full, lid on between batches, and read the probe, not the dial: an induction top’s “keep warm” setting cycles and a small pan swings well past 90 °C between pulses — at 90, a cup of cold water goes in before the next batch. For the shell-on line in weather, skip the open bath: a pre-warmed vacuum flask filled at 66 °C and sealed holds the eggs thirty minutes; probe at fifteen and top up if it has slid under 63.

Once it holds at sea, one morning’s eggs feed both decks:

Guest finish: the plate line — the fired egg on a toasted muffin under hollandaise, held the way you would hold a beurre monté, at 75 °C on a probe. Crew finish: the shell-on line from the same box of eggs, cracked over the lunch rice with soy and sesame — cook once, serve both.

One more number belongs in the log before service:

The Food Code’s line for an egg cooked ahead of the order is 63 °C for 3 minutes; to order, 63 for 15 seconds (FDA Food Code 2022, §3-401.11). The shell-on hold clears it fifteen times over; the shock-and-reheat egg does not — its yolk never comes near 63 — so it is a runny egg, served as one: shell eggs stored under 7 °C, and the pasteurised route for anyone pregnant, elderly or immunocompromised. Reheating for immediate service carries no hot-holding figure (§3-403.10).

The Proof

You know it worked when: the fired egg sits dry on the plate with a matte white that keeps its ruffle and a yolk that runs when cut; the shell-on egg slips out whole, its yolk a glossy custard that holds a spoon-mark; and the pass never waits for a pot.

That test is not for the egg; it is for the people the eggs are for.

Brunch for twelve on a rolling anchorage, a warm whole egg on every plate and nobody in the galley bent over a swirl — that is what two thermometers buy the people you feed.

Strip it to the one move worth keeping:

The one thing: decide the texture, then the bath — 80–85 °C, short and shocked, for the plate; 63 °C in the shell for the bowl — and fire the plate eggs from a 66 °C tray. Never ask one pot to do both. Tested at sea.
