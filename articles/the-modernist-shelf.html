The Modernist Shelf: What Is Inside Every Book Worth Owning

Every Modernist Cuisine book opened, volume by volume: who made them, what is inside, what they weigh and cost. Then the Barcelona shelf, Disfrutar, Compartir, Tickets, Candy, Roca and Camarena, book by book, and the free texts that hold the doses and the safety lines. What each one puts in a chef's hands, what it leaves out, and why no legal free PDF exists.

Carry Modernist Cuisine at Home and Roca's sous-vide book in the locker and the free Khymos and Baldwin PDFs on your phone; buy the Barcelona books for the plates once the technique is yours; and close any page offering any of them as a free PDF, because none exists.

Most chefs buy the wrong book first: a restaurant volume that shows the plate and assumes the dose. This page opens every book worth owning, the eight from Nathan Myhrvold's Cooking Lab and the Barcelona shelf, and says what is inside, who it is for and what it leaves out; then the free texts that hold the doses, the reasons and the safety lines. Every link goes to the publisher, the author's shop or a named bookseller; records and list prices were checked between 30 October and 7 November 2026.

The Modernist Cuisine library

Eight books from a laboratory in Bellevue, Washington, and what each one puts in your hands.

Nathan Myhrvold was Microsoft's chief technology officer, a physicist who had worked under Stephen Hawking, and a cook who had done shifts at Rover's in Seattle. In 2006 he began the book himself. By the time it appeared in March 2011, 36 researchers, chefs and editors were working on it at once, more than 50 people had contributed, 14 outside experts had reviewed it, and 1,522 recipes had been developed and tested.

The Lab sells every title from its own shop, in dollars; outside North America Phaidon distributes them. None has a legal ebook: the one app, Modernist Cuisine at Home for the iPad, is no longer sold or updated.

The photographs that made it famous are the cutaways: a pot, a wok, a barbecue sliced in half with the food apparently cooking inside. Each half was hand-cut in a metal shop nearby, the food was shot separately at high speed, and the two were joined afterwards: a moment no camera could see. Michael Ruhlman's review in The New York Times was headed "Cook From It? First, Try Lifting It", and that is still the honest line: 23.6 kilos, $625, and a publisher's FAQ that says the authors "would not simplify those recipes that illustrate the fascinating culinary applications of advanced ingredients", liquid nitrogen and centrifuges among them. It won the James Beard Cookbook of the Year in 2012 all the same. The eighth title, Natural Wonders (2025), is landscape photography; of the other seven, two matter to a galley, and the next section opens them.

Inside Modernist Cuisine, volume by volume

Where sous vide, the gels, the foams, the spheres and the nitrogen actually sit, and what the smaller book leaves out.

The set is five hardbacks in a steel case and a sixth, wire-bound volume that is waterproof and tear-proof: the Kitchen Manual, 374 pages, which repeats every recipe and reference table so that the only book near the stove is the one that can take it. The recipes come in three kinds, named in the publisher's notes: example recipes that show a technique, parametric recipes, and plated dishes.

  • Volume 1, History and Fundamentals: microbiology for cooks, food safety, food and health, heat and energy, the physics of food and water. The chapters a galley needs most and reads least.
  • Volume 2, Techniques and Equipment: traditional cooking, modern ovens, the sous-vide chapter (packing, equipment, chilling and reheating, searing after the bath) and The Modernist Kitchen: extracting, juicing, filtering, concentrating, drying, and the cryogenic freezing that is the book's nitrogen.
  • Volume 3, Animals and Plants: meat and seafood, salted, marinated, smoked or restructured; plant foods cooked sous vide, under pressure, fried or re-textured.
  • Volume 4, Ingredients and Preparations: thickeners, gels with spherification inside them, emulsions, foams, then wine and coffee. The dose volume: the one a chef on this page would open first.
  • Volume 5, Plated-Dish Recipes: tender and tough cuts, poultry, fish, shellfish, eggs, starches, fruit and vegetables.
  • The Kitchen Manual: every recipe and table again, spiral-bound, washable. The only volume that goes near the stove, and the one you will actually cook from.

Modernist Cuisine at Home takes the same thinking to a kitchen without a centrifuge. Part one is the kit: countertop tools, conventional gear, sous vide, ingredients. Part two begins with the basics, stocks, sauces, brines and spice mixes, then the dishes: steak, short ribs, roast chicken, pizza, risotto and paella, microwave dishes, custards and pies, with 114 step-by-step tutorials. What a chef takes from it: pressure-cooker stocks, sous vide in a pot with a probe, everything in grams. Its own publisher calls it the less complex book, says a scale and a probe thermometer are "absolutely essential", and lists sous vide and pressure cooking among its subjects, not gels or spheres. For those: the full set, or the free shelf.

The Barcelona shelf

Disfrutar, Compartir, Tickets, Candy: what is inside each one, and what it assumes you already know.

Three of these books come from the same three men. Oriol Castro, Eduard Xatruch and Mateu Casañas led elBulli's creativity, kitchen and pastry until it closed in July 2011; they opened Compartir in Cadaqués in April 2012 and Disfrutar in Barcelona, beside the Ninot market, at the end of 2014. A first Michelin star came in 2015, the third in 2024, and No. 1 on the World's 50 Best the same year. The other three are Albert Adrià's, from Tickets, the "neighbourhood Bulli" he opened in January 2011 with the Iglesias brothers and closed, with the rest of his elBarri group, in April 2021. All six are written for professionals, in metric, and none of them explains what a hydrocolloid is.

  • Disfrutar, Volumes 1 to 3 — Castro, Xatruch and Casañas, Abalon Books, 2021, 2023 and 2025. Each volume is two books in a slipcase: a small clothbound one on how they think, and a ring-bound catalogue of the dishes, photographed by Francesc Guillamet, whose pages come out for the kitchen. The closest thing in print to standing at their pass.
    • Volume 1, 2014–2017: 166 recipes and more than 200 photographs; gatefolds open out wherever a technique is unusual, so you see every step. Foreword by Ferran Adrià. English edition sold out at Kitchen Arts & Letters; the edition on sale at Casa del Libro.
    • Volume 2, 2018–2020: 96 dishes, among them a puff pastry made without flour and croquants made in the microwave, and about fifty QR codes that lead to videos of the hands at work. English edition sold out in New York.
    • Volume 3, 2021–2023: the years of the third star. 98 recipes, many with video, and the Living Table, where the table itself becomes part of the service. $350 at Kitchen Arts & Letters, in stock. No ebook of any volume exists.
  • Compartir — the same three, Planeta Gastro, 2017, 216 pages, more than 200 photographs by Guillamet, prologue by Ferran Adrià. The story of the move from elBulli to Cadaqués and the fifty dishes of a restaurant built for sharing: every plate designed for the whole table, which is how a yacht lunch is served. The cheapest way into how they think. Spanish and Catalan only. Planeta.
  • Tapas, The Tickets Cuisine — Albert Adrià, RBA Libros, 2013, 176 pages. The emblematic tapas of Tickets, written for cooking at home: modern technique at tapa scale, which is canapé scale. €35 signed, in English, from albertadria.com.
  • Tickets Evolution — Albert Adrià, RBA Libros, 2018, 272 pages, nearly 100 recipes. In the author's words, "this is not a tapas book": the dishes that outgrew the format, among them a strawberry tree with elderflower and kimchi, prawns in frozen salt, saffron sponge with bread soup, and a grilled oyster with black chanterelle tea. €35 signed, in English, from albertadria.com; a Spanish EPUB at Casa del Libro.
  • Candy. Los postres de elBarri — David Gil and Albert Adrià, RBA Libros, 2021, 512 pages, 25.5 by 32 centimetres. Ten years of desserts, 2011 to 2021, from the group that included Tickets, Bodega 1900, Hoja Santa, Pakta and Enigma, finished as the group closed. Two halves: a book of photographs, each dessert with a short note on its idea, and a recipe book with every step. The sweet side of foams and texturisers. Spanish only. €85 signed from albertadria.com; the cheapest legal digital book on this page, EPUB, €12.99 at Casa del Libro.
  • Natura — Albert Adrià, elBulli Books, 2008, 151 pages, out of stock at the author's own shop; and elBulli 2005–2011 — Ferran Adrià, Phaidon, 2014, seven volumes, 2,720 pages, from the elBullifoundation store: the archive these techniques came from, for the history rather than the galley. The foundation's Bullipedia runs past thirty volumes.

Sous vide, and the line that keeps it safe

Roca's book earns its place for its tables and its hazard plans; one free guide does the rest; the holding rules are in neither.

Joan Roca met vacuum cooking in Georges Blanc's kitchen in Vonnas in 1992. Back at the Girona catering school where he taught, he and a colleague, Salvador Brugués, spent 1996 researching low-temperature cooking, and in 1997 he and a schoolmate, Narcís Caner, built a thermostat that held a water bath at one exact temperature and kept it moving. They called it the Roner, from their two names, and it is the ancestor of every circulator on every yacht. The book came in 2003.

  • Sous-Vide Cuisine / La cocina al vacío — Joan Roca and Salvador Brugués, Montagud Editores. Spanish 2003, out of print; English 2005, 192 pages; bilingual 2014, 256 pages. The equipment and materials, the principles, then precise times and temperatures by ingredient, and plated dishes built on them. The 2014 edition widens the tables to many more ingredients, adds a brining stage for many of them and keeps the sample HACCP plans: the one cookbook on this page with a hazard plan you can adapt for a yacht's food-safety file. €110 from the Celler de Can Roca shop; $188 bilingual at Kitchen Arts & Letters. No ebook.
  • Free: A Practical Guide to Sous Vide Cooking by Douglas Baldwin, a mathematician, at douglasbaldwin.com: the time-at-temperature tables, pasteurisation included, that the paid books quote. His 2012 peer-reviewed review is free on the same site. If you read one thing on sous vide, read this.
  • Modernist Cuisine, Volume 2, chapter 9, above: the fullest treatment in print of packing, chilling, reheating and searing.
  • Under Pressure: Cooking Sous Vide — Thomas Keller, Artisan, 2008, 295 pages. The American restaurant version, a book of finished dishes rather than method. Hachette.
  • Free: the Low-Temperature and Sous-Vide Primer, Cooking Issues, for the reasoning in plain words.
  • Free: the safety texts no cookbook prints. FDA Hazards Guide, chapter 13: fish in a pouch held below 3.3 °C from packing to plate. FSA 2016 guidance on vacuum-packed chilled foods: ten days unless another barrier is proven. What that means for a boat fridge is in the sister piece.

Broth

The one technique the modernist shelf treats as a given, and the one book that treats it as the whole dish.

Ricard Camarena cooks in Valencia, and his broths, juices and stocks are made with hardly any water: the liquid comes out of the ingredients themselves, and the result is used as a seasoning, not a base. Caldos = Broths. El código del sabor, Montagud Editores, November 2015, 272 pages, bilingual Spanish and English, two kilos and now in its second edition, is his only cookbook, and it opens with four rules.

Don't think you're making a broth. Imagine that you're making lunch for your mother. And on top of that, imagine that you aren't going to give her anything else.

The other three, in short: a broth made as a preliminary comes out discreet, so make it as a finished dish; the broth is the result, so it must be worth dipping bread in; and never use a poor one because the other ingredients will cover it. Then the methods behind them, ingredient by ingredient. Casa del Libro; sold out at Kitchen Arts & Letters, $95 when in stock. For what happens in the pot, McGee, below.

The dose: gels, foams, spheres, textures

Where the grams per litre are, how far to trust them, and why most of it costs nothing.

The restaurant books assume the dose; these print it. In the paid shelf the same ground is Modernist Cuisine Volume 4; on the free shelf it is a 138-page PDF edited by a Norwegian chemist, and for a working chef that is the one that earns its keep.

  • Free: Texture: A hydrocolloid recipe collection, edited by Martin Lersch, version 3.0, 2014, 138 pages, Creative Commons, from khymos.org. Its quiet strength: within each chapter the recipes run in ascending order of hydrocolloid, every dose also given as a mass percentage, so you can see at a glance where a gel turns from wobble to slice. Its honest limit, stated by the editor: the recipes were gathered, not tested by him. Use it as the starting dose, then test before service.
  • Free: the Hydrocolloids Primer, Cooking Issues: ten powders, what each one does, the usual range, direct, reverse and agar cold-oil spheres. Read it beside Khymos: one gives the why, the other the number.
  • Free: Sosa's Texture Wheel, The mousse revolution, Naturfoam and Gracila Gel booklets at sosa.cat. The dose for their own products, which is what most galleys actually stock; a maker's sheet, so it never tells you a rival powder would do.
  • Ideas in Food — Aki Kamozawa and H. Alexander Talbot, Clarkson Potter, 2010, 320 pages. The working cook's book: technique as a way of thinking, not a catalogue. For the chef who has the doses and wants to invent with them. Penguin Random House.
  • Handbook of Hydrocolloids, third edition — Phillips and Williams, Woodhead/Elsevier, 2020, a chapter per hydrocolloid. Written for food scientists, priced for libraries; the book to borrow the day a gel fails and nothing above explains why. Elsevier.

The why

The books that explain what the powder and the bath are doing, so you know what to change when a recipe fails.

The dose tells you what works; these tell you why, which is what you need at sea when it does not and there is no one to ask.

  • On Food and Cooking — Harold McGee, Scribner. First written in 1984 at 704 pages, rewritten in 2004 at 896. Heat, proteins, starch, emulsions, in prose a tired chef can follow; Heston Blumenthal called it "the book that has had the greatest single impact on my cooking", and every author on this page cites it. Record and sellers.
  • Molecular Gastronomy: Exploring the Science of Flavour — Hervé This, Columbia University Press, 2006. The physical chemist who, with Nicholas Kurti, named the field in 1988, explaining his own questions one short chapter at a time; for the curious more than the practical. Columbia.
  • Handbook of Molecular Gastronomy — Burke, Kelly, Lavelle and This, CRC Press, 2021, 894 pages. The paper behind the plate, by the people who did the work: Baldwin on sous vide, Barham on cryogenics, three chapters on spherification, one of them the laboratory measurement of calcium moving into an alginate gel. Academic in tone and price; for the chef who teaches, or wants to. Routledge.
  • Free: the open textbooks, for the vocabulary: Chemistry of Cooking on LibreTexts; Modern Pastry and Plated Dessert Techniques and Understanding Ingredients for the Canadian Baker from BCcampus, both CC BY, both with a PDF. Written for culinary students, which is exactly their use: the words, defined once and correctly.
  • Free: Harvard's Science and Cooking lecture series: the physics spoken by the chefs themselves, for a long passage with a screen.

Nitrogen

There is no nitrogen cookbook worth the money; two chapters and two free texts cover it.

Read these before the dewar comes aboard, not after. The idea is older than any of these books: in 1901 Agnes Marshall, the London ice-cream maker, proposed freezing ice cream at the table with "liquid air".

  • Modernist Cuisine, Volume 2, chapter 10, cryogenic freezing and carbonating: the paid shelf's nitrogen.
  • Handbook of Molecular Gastronomy, above: Peter Barham's chapter Cryogenics in the Kitchen, and a restaurant chapter on liquid-nitrogen ice creams.
  • Free: the Liquid Nitrogen Primer, Cooking Issues: cold burns, asphyxiation, pressure, dewars, and the line "Do not use LN in an unventilated area".
  • Free: EIGA Doc 44/18, Hazards of Oxygen-Deficient Atmospheres: 19.5 per cent oxygen is the line, and one litre of liquid makes about 0.65 cubic metres of gas.

What goes aboard

A shelf that fits a locker, a library that fits a phone, and one warning.

Three steps, in this order; most galleys can stop after the first. Download the free shelf before you leave the marina's Wi-Fi: Khymos for the grams, Baldwin for the time and temperature, the Cooking Issues primers for the reasons, the FDA chapter and the FSA guidance for the dates. Together they cover every technique on this page and weigh nothing.

Then one reference, sized to the boat. Modernist Cuisine at Home, 4.5 kilos, fits a locker and answers most of what a charter menu asks; Roca's bilingual edition, for the tables and the HACCP plans, is the second book in. The full Modernist Cuisine comes aboard only with a shelf that will hold 23.6 kilos in a seaway and a crew mess with room to open it.

Watch for: a search for any Disfrutar volume, for the Roca book or for Modernist Cuisine returns a dozen pages offering it as a free PDF or EPUB. None is the book: they are pirated scans or bait, the royalties are the chefs', and a yacht's network is the last place to open an unknown file. The legal digital editions that exist are the two RBA ebooks above; Disfrutar, every Cooking Lab book, Sous-Vide Cuisine and Caldos have none.

Buy a plate book last, once the technique is already in your hands: Disfrutar if the budget allows, Tickets Evolution if it does not, Candy for the pastry side. They show what a world-class team does with the same powders, and they assume you know how.
