Bay Leaf: Better Dried, Never Frozen, Not Poison

Nine leaves in ten are picked by hand from Turkish maquis and cross the border at three dollars a kilo. The leaf rewards drying, punishes the freezer, and was never the poison you were told.

Bay is the leaf of the laurel, and the word carries its history. In Turkish it is defne, in Greek dáfni: both are the name of Daphne, the nymph who became the tree. English bay is the Old French baie, a berry; laurel is the Roman laurus. The family is the Lauraceae, which tells a cook two things: cinnamon and cassia are relatives, so the clove-warm edge is family; and the hedge that looks like it, cherry laurel, is no relation and no food.

0540, the dry store. A 250 g export bag from İzmir on the bench: matte olive-green leaves the length of a finger, whole, not a brown one among them. Snap one — it cracks clean and the galley smells of eucalyptus and clove. That is the test. The leaf costs nothing and makes the cheapest pot — pulses, potatoes, bones — taste of somewhere.

Laurus nobilis L., Lauraceae — sweet bay, true laurel. One species, and nearly every leaf that reaches a European kitchen is wild; what varies is where it grew, how it was dried, and whether it is bay at all. Here is how to choose it, why it tastes of what it tastes of, and how to keep it alive through a charter.

Read more — where it comes from

Bay is a relic of the subtropical laurel forests that ringed the Tethys Sea. The genus has not changed its climate needs in three million years; the ice ages squeezed it into refuges around the Mediterranean and on the Atlantic islands, where the laurisilva of Madeira and the Canaries still stands (Rodríguez-Sánchez & Arroyo 2008, Global Ecology and Biogeography).

The leaf in your dry store outlived the ice ages. Its climate requirements have not shifted in three million years — which is why it grows wild on every warm, wet Mediterranean coast and sulks inland.

The Greeks put it on heads before it went into pots; Pliny counted thirteen kinds, and the laurel wreath crowned emperors for six centuries. Two thousand years later it had become the most invisible ingredient in the French kitchen: the parsley-thyme-bay bundle Escoffier fixed as the bouquet garni in Le Guide Culinaire (1903). Today it reaches your dock as a Turkish forest product: cut by villagers under a state permit, graded by leaf length, shade-dried and sacked for export before any jar is printed (KUZKA 2020).

Why It Works

The Science: A Slow Leaf That Wants Fat, Time, and Never the Freezer

Get the mechanism and you stop wasting fresh bay, and stop under-extracting dried.

Fresh is not the better leaf, and freezing it to keep it fresh is the one move that wrecks it. Air-dried at room temperature, bay carries more essential oil and more of its main aroma compounds than the fresh leaf (Sellami et al. 2011, Food Chemistry); air-drying or a 45 °C oven lost “hardly any” volatiles, while freezing and freeze-drying brought “substantial losses in bay leaf aroma” (Díaz-Maroto et al. 2002, J. Agric. Food Chem.). The Turkish trade agrees: a fresh leaf does not reach full flavour until weeks after cutting and drying (KUZKA 2020).

Here is the chemistry that proves it:

The Chemistry
What is in the leaf, and why it comes out slowly
  • 1,8-Cineole — 26–63% of the oil across origins (Ordoudi et al. 2022). It boils near 176 °C (NIST), so it rides out a simmer and leaves the pot slowly, on the steam.
  • Sabinene, linalool, α-terpinyl acetate — the resinous-floral middle (sabinene 12.2%, linalool 10.2%; Caputo et al. 2017).
  • Eugenol and methyl eugenol — the clove warmth, among the compounds that rose with air-drying (Sellami et al. 2011): the reason the dried leaf reads rounder than the fresh.
  • Oil cells, not a coating — the oil sits in idioblasts inside a leathery evergreen leaf (Malaspina et al. 2022): a whole leaf gives slowly, a crushed one fast.

Bay is a slow-release aromatic: oil locked in cells inside a tough leaf, and, like every essential oil, hydrophobic. That is why it shows more in a béchamel or a confit than in a thin stock, and why a whole leaf needs twenty to thirty minutes at a simmer. Put the dried leaf in early, with the fat, and give it twenty minutes; if you must keep fresh branches, hang them in the shade to dry — never in the freezer.

How to Buy: Three Leaves Sell Under the Name

Only one belongs in a Mediterranean pot — and most of its quality is decided before it reaches your knife.

The label says “Mediterranean” or laurier; the leaf is almost certainly Turkish. Türkiye meets about 90% of world demand (Ünal 2025, BioResources) from wild maquis between Hatay and the Black Sea. In 2019 it exported 13,879 t for $39.9 million — about $2.90 a kilo at the border; hand-selected grades fetched $3.8–4.8/kg in Germany, the US and Britain. In a European shop, ten leaves (6 g) cost about €3: roughly €500 a kilo, over 150 times the border price (KUZKA 2020). A 5 g jar through a ship's agent pays for the jar.

  • What the trade may do to it: dried herbs and spices are the one food class the EU lets be irradiated for microbial control, up to 10 kGy (Directive 1999/3/EC) — and the law makes it visible: irradiated or treated with ionising radiation must be on the label, on a notice by the bin if sold loose, and in the ingredient list of a blend (Directive 1999/2/EC, art. 6). The US allows 30 kGy (21 CFR 179.26). Read the small print.
  • The most natural route: Turkish bay is not farmed: it is cut from wild forest under permit and shade-dried, never in the sun, which drives the oil out (KUZKA 2020); exporters test for pesticide residues and microbes to meet buyers' standards. Certified organic wild collection means the forest received nothing but organic-permitted treatments for three years before harvest (Regulation (EU) 2018/848, Annex II). Ask for the certificate, and buy whole leaves — ground bay cannot be inspected and its oil is half gone.
  • The chef's own supply: a bay tree in a pot lives for years on a sunny deck and gives more leaves than a galley uses; hang the cut branches in the shade for two to three weeks and they become the better leaf.
  • Whole dried leaves, the export grade — what Turkish packers call Hand Select: whole, colour intact, no insect marks; the state standard's top grade runs 2.5–10 cm long (TSE 1017, in KUZKA 2020). Olive-green, matte, a slightly wavy edge, a red stalk. Snap test: it cracks clean and, crushed, smells of eucalyptus-clove at once.
  • Fresh branches from a Mediterranean market — softer, greener, harsher. Use within the week or hang them to dry; the dried leaf will be the better one.
  • The catering pack — 100–250 g from a Turkish exporter is the same leaf as the 5 g jar at a fraction per gram. Buy for the season, not for the boat's life.

And the specimen you hand straight back:

Grey or brown, crumbling to dust, no smell when crushed: the oil has gone. And hand back any “bay” you cannot name. Cherry laurel, Prunus laurocerasus, the hedge that looks like it, has a green stalk, a serrated edge, small glands on the underside by the stalk, and smells of bitter almond when crushed; its leaves carry 1–2.5% cyanogenic glycosides (Malaspina et al. 2022, Toxins). Bay has a red stalk, a wavy edge, no glands, and smells of camphor.

What Every Cuisine Found

The Pairings: What Every Coast Figured Out

Serve the first four blind — the pot proved them. The fifth is the one guests remember.

Bay + dried beans, lentils, chickpeasTürkiye · Greece · Portugal

The slow leaf meets the slow pot: forty minutes at a simmer is exactly the extraction a whole leaf needs, and cineole's cool edge lifts the flat sweetness of starch. The same leaf goes into the jar of dry beans to keep the weevils out (KUZKA 2020).

Bay + milk: béchamel, rice pudding, custardFrance · Britain

Milk fat is the solvent a hydrophobic oil wants. The onion piqué — onion, clove, bay — infuses in twenty minutes off the heat and carries bay where a water-based stock cannot.

Bay + fish in liquorAtlantic France · Turkish Aegean

In a court-bouillon or a buğulama the volatile top note travels with the steam and lands on the flesh. One whole leaf in the liquor for twenty minutes, then out, and the fish tastes of the sea it came from, not of a spice rack.

Bay + slow pork or lamb: daube, cozidoProvence · Portugal

Fat-rich braises are where a fat-soluble oil ends up; the wine's acid keeps cineole's camphor edge reading fresh rather than medicinal, and the long cook is the leaf's own timescale.

The littoral wild card: bay + dried figsDalmatian islands · the Aegean fig trade

Reads wrong on paper, lands on the plate: the leaf that keeps the moth out of the fig is also its best aromatic partner. Turkish packers lay bay leaves among dried figs and raisins to hold freshness and flavour (KUZKA 2020); the Dalmatian fig cake smokvenjak is wrapped in fresh bay leaves and dried in the sun (Croatian National Tourist Board); the same oil is repellent and toxic to stored-grain beetles (Mediouni Ben Jemâa et al. 2012). Poach dried figs in red wine with two leaves; the clove-eucalyptus edge cuts the sugar.

Galley Intel: Ways to Use It, and How It Survives a Charter

One rule runs every idea below: the oil lives in fat, leaves on steam, and hates the freezer.

  • Laurel butter — 250 g butter, six dried leaves cracked in half, 60 °C for 40 minutes, strain, roll in film, freeze. Fat is the solvent cineole wants; a coin finishes fish, a beurre blanc or a purée without a leaf in sight.
  • Bay oil — 200 ml olive oil, eight leaves, 60 °C for 30 minutes, cool overnight, strain. Same logic, for grilled fish, white beans and roast vegetables; a week in the fridge.
  • Bay salt — ten dried leaves blitzed with 100 g flaky salt. On roast potatoes, lamb, a tomato salad. Ground leaf loses its oil fast: make it small and often.
  • Bay and lemon syrup — 200 g sugar, 200 ml water, four leaves, the peel of a lemon: simmer ten minutes, steep an hour. The leaf's floral linalool sits beside the citrus: a bay lemonade for the crew, a gin sour for the guests.
  • Bay smoke — a handful of dried branches on the embers for the last minute under fish or lamb, lid down. Cineole boils near 176 °C (NIST), so at grill temperature it rides the smoke onto the flesh.
  • Bay ice cream — 500 ml of the bay milk below, five yolks, 90 g sugar, cooked to 82 °C, chilled, churned: the old English custard, made for the poached figs above.
  • Not in the brine, in the fat — a water brine takes almost none of the oil; save the leaves for the roasting tin, under the joint, where the rendered fat does the extracting.
  • Storage: whole dried leaves in an airtight tub, dark, in the coolest locker. Fresh branches: a week upright in water in the fridge, or hung in the shade to dry. Never the freezer (Díaz-Maroto et al. 2002).
  • Dose: a dried leaf weighs 0.1–0.3 g (Karataş et al. 2023). Two leaves per 4 L crew pot, in at the start; one leaf per litre of guest sauce for twenty minutes, then out. Double neither — the camphor turns medicinal.
  • Count in, count out: the leaf never softens; it leaves before the plate.
  • Provisioning: in any Turkish or Greek port the pazar or laiki sells hand-picked leaves by weight; a 250 g bag is a season's supply for a fraction of jar prices.

We take it out because it is poison, the dock says. It is not — bay is non-toxic; the poisonous look-alike is cherry laurel (Malaspina et al. 2022). It comes out because it stays rigid: leaves have lodged in throats (Buto et al. 1990), blocked a duodenum (Tsang et al. 1999) and perforated a bowel (Bell & Mustard 1997). Count them back.

Of all that, one move is worth pinning above the prep bench:

Bay milk, for ten covers. 1 L whole milk, four dried leaves cracked in half, half an onion, four cloves. Bring to 80 °C (176 °F) — steaming, never boiling — then lid on, off the heat, 30 minutes. Strain, label, chill fast; fridge at 4 °C or below, three days. It is the base of the béchamel, the mash, the rice pudding and the ice cream, with no infusing at service.

Quick Reference

  • Best season — evergreen, so buy by the crop: the Turkish harvest comes off in late summer, and a new-crop pack in autumn is the freshest you will see (KUZKA 2020).
  • Best with — pulses, milk, fish in liquor, slow pork and lamb, dried figs.
  • Avoid with — anything raw or fast; ground bay; the freezer.
  • Storage — dried whole: airtight, dark, cool, one season. Fresh: fridge a week, or shade-dry two to three weeks.

Use the dried leaf, never a frozen one; put it in early with the fat and give it twenty minutes; count every leaf back out.
