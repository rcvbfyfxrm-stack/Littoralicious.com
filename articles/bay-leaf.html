Bay Leaf: Better Dried, Never Frozen, Not Poison

Nine leaves in ten are picked by hand from Turkish maquis and cross the border at three dollars a kilo. The leaf rewards drying, punishes the freezer, and was never the poison you were told.

What you'll know before you buy it
  • The two-second buying signal that separates a live leaf from a dead one, and the grade name the Turkish trade uses for it.
  • Why the dried leaf beats the fresh one, why the freezer is the one place it must never go, and the papers that measured it.
  • A thirty-minute infusion base for ten covers, and the count-in, count-out rule with the medical record behind it.

The name is the berry, not the leaf: bay comes from Latin baca, a berry, and until the 1520s it meant only the fruit (Online Etymology Dictionary). Elsewhere the tree carries the myth — laurel from laurus, Greek dáfni and Turkish defne from Daphne. The family is Lauraceae: cinnamon and avocado are cousins, which is why Cinnamomum tamala sells as “Indian bay” and tastes of cassia. Cherry laurel, the leaf that actually poisons people, is no laurel at all but a Prunus (Malaspina et al. 2022).

0540, the dry store, the night before guests come aboard: a 250 g export bag from İzmir, matte olive-green leaves the length of a finger, not a brown one among them. Snap one and the galley smells of eucalyptus and clove.

A Portuguese avó drops one leaf into the refogado before the onions have taken colour; a grandmother in Hatay keeps a branch on the kitchen wall for the bean pot and the lamb. The leaf costs nothing and makes the cheapest pot — pulses, potatoes, bones — taste of somewhere. That is why it is in every larder on this coast, and why the 0300 watch-keeper's stew tastes like someone cared.

Now the vital signs — the four numbers this whole profile hangs on:

Laurus nobilis L. — sweet bay, true laurel. One species, and nearly every leaf that reaches a European kitchen is wild; what varies is where it grew, how it was dried, and whether it is bay at all. Here is how to choose it, why it tastes of what it tastes of, and how to keep it alive through a charter.

Where It Comes From

Origin: A Survivor of the Forest That Once Covered the Mediterranean

Know its age and you stop treating it like a garnish.

Bay is a relic of the subtropical laurel forests that ringed the ancient Tethys Sea. The genus Laurus has not changed its climate needs in three million years; the ice ages squeezed it into refugia around the Mediterranean and on the Atlantic islands, where the laurisilva of Madeira and the Canaries still stands (Rodríguez-Sánchez & Arroyo 2008, Global Ecology and Biogeography).

The Greeks put it on heads before it went into pots. Pliny counted thirteen kinds and recorded the branches planted after a triumph; Ovid gave the tree its story — Daphne, turned to laurel to escape Apollo, who claims it for his hair, his lyre and the victors' wreath (Metamorphoses I.452–567). Her Greek name is still on the sacks: defne.

The leaf in your dry store outlived the ice ages. Its climate requirements have not shifted in three million years — which is why it grows wild on every warm, wet Mediterranean coast and sulks inland (Rodríguez-Sánchez & Arroyo 2008).

The status flip took two thousand years and ended in anonymity. The crown of Caesar — who, Suetonius says, wore his laurel wreath more gladly than any honour because it hid his baldness — became the parsley-thyme-bay bundle Escoffier fixed as the bouquet garni in Le Guide Culinaire (1903), the most invisible ingredient in the French kitchen. Today it stands on your dock as a Turkish forest product: cut by villagers under a state permit, graded by leaf length, shade-dried, and sacked for export before any jar is printed (KUZKA 2020).

The leaf that crowned emperors now goes in with the onions and comes out with the bones.

Why It Works

The Science: A Slow Leaf That Wants Fat, Time, and Never the Freezer

Get the mechanism and you stop wasting fresh bay, and stop under-extracting dried.

Fresh is not the better leaf, and freezing it to keep it fresh is the one move that wrecks it. Air-dried at room temperature, bay carries significantly more essential oil and more of its main aroma compounds than the fresh leaf (Sellami et al. 2011, Food Chemistry). Air-drying or a 45 °C oven lost “hardly any” volatiles against fresh, while freezing and freeze-drying brought “substantial losses in bay leaf aroma” (Díaz-Maroto et al. 2002, J. Agric. Food Chem.). The Turkish trade has said it for years: a fresh leaf is soft and does not reach full flavour until weeks after cutting and drying (KUZKA 2020).

Here is the chemistry that proves it:

The Chemistry
What is in the leaf, and why it comes out slowly
  • 1,8-Cineole (eucalyptol) — 26–63% of the oil across origins (Ordoudi et al. 2022; 31.9% in a Southern-Italy sample, Caputo et al. 2017): the cool eucalyptus-camphor top note. It boils near 176 °C (NIST), so it rides out a simmer and leaves the pot slowly, on the steam.
  • Sabinene, linalool, α-terpinyl acetate — the resinous-floral middle (sabinene 12.2%, linalool 10.2%; Caputo et al. 2017).
  • Eugenol and methyl eugenol — the clove warmth, among the compounds that rose with air-drying at ambient temperature (Sellami et al. 2011); the reason the dried leaf reads rounder than the fresh.
  • Oil cells, not a coating — the oil sits in secretory idioblasts inside a leathery evergreen leaf (Malaspina et al. 2022): a whole leaf gives slowly, a crushed one fast.

Bay is a slow-release aromatic: oil locked in cells inside a tough leaf, and, like every essential oil, hydrophobic — it dissolves into fat and barely into water. That is why bay shows more in a béchamel or a confit than in a thin stock, why a whole leaf needs twenty to thirty minutes at a simmer, and why the dried leaf is the cook's leaf. Put the dried leaf in early, with the fat, and give it twenty minutes at a simmer; if you must keep fresh branches, hang them in the shade to dry — never in the freezer.

One more thing the jar won't tell you: where the leaf comes from, and what it costs.

The label says “Mediterranean” or laurier; the leaf is almost certainly Turkish. Türkiye meets about 90% of world demand (Ünal 2025, BioResources) from wild maquis between Hatay and the Black Sea, cut by forest villagers who pay the state for the right and sit at the bottom of the chain: of 51 surveyed in 2025, one in ten called it profitable. In 2019 Türkiye exported 13,879 t for $39.9 million: about $2.90 a kilo at the border. Hand-selected grades fetched $3.8–4.8/kg in Germany, the US, the UK, Spain and Canada; six kilos in ten went to Vietnam at about $2.15, largely bound for China. In a European shop, ten leaves (6 g) cost about €3 — roughly €500 a kilo, over 150 times the border price (KUZKA 2020, TÜİK data). A 5 g jar through a ship's agent pays for the jar.

What Every Cuisine Found

The Pairings: What Every Coast Figured Out

Serve the first rows blind — the pot proved them. The last row is the one guests remember.

Pairing Region Why it works
Bay + dried beans, lentils, chickpeas Türkiye · Greece · Portugal The slow leaf meets the slow pot: forty minutes at a simmer is exactly the extraction a whole leaf needs, and cineole's cool edge lifts the flat sweetness of starch. The same leaf goes into the jar of dry beans to keep the weevils out (KUZKA 2020).
Bay + milk: béchamel, rice pudding, custard France · Britain Milk fat is the solvent a hydrophobic oil wants. The onion piqué — onion, clove, bay — infuses in twenty minutes off the heat and carries bay where a water-based stock cannot.
Bay + fish in liquor: court-bouillon, buğulama Atlantic France · Turkish Aegean The volatile top note travels with the steam and lands on the flesh; one whole leaf in the liquor for twenty minutes, then out, and the fish tastes of the sea it came from, not of a spice rack.
Bay + slow pork or lamb: daube, cozido Provence · Portugal Fat-rich braises are where a fat-soluble oil ends up; the wine's acid keeps cineole's camphor edge reading fresh rather than medicinal, and the long cook is the leaf's own timescale.
The littoral wild card: bay + dried figs Dalmatian islands · the Aegean fig trade Reads wrong on paper, lands on the plate: the leaf that keeps the moth out of the fig is also its best aromatic partner. Turkish packers lay bay leaves among dried figs and raisins to hold freshness and flavour (KUZKA 2020); the Dalmatian fig cake smokvenjak is wrapped in fresh bay leaves and dried in the sun (Croatian National Tourist Board); the same oil is repellent and toxic to stored-grain beetles (Mediouni Ben Jemâa et al. 2012). Poach dried figs in red wine with two leaves; the clove-eucalyptus edge cuts the sugar.
At the Dock

The Varieties: Which Leaf, and Which Is Not Bay at All

Three leaves sell under the name. Only one belongs in a Mediterranean pot.

Variety Taste profile Best for
True bay, Laurus nobilis — Türkiye, Greece, Italy, Iberia Soft eucalyptus from 1,8-cineole over resinous-floral sabinene and linalool, with a clove warmth from eugenol that deepens on drying (Caputo et al. 2017; Sellami et al. 2011). Everything above: pulses, milk, fish, braises, figs.
“California bay”, Umbellularia californica Not a Laurus. Harsh camphor-menthol; the leaf oil is 41% umbellulone (Kelsey et al. 2007), an irritant ketone that fires the TRPA1 pain receptor in rodent and cell studies — locals call it the “headache tree” (Nassini et al. 2012, Brain; not tested in humans). American recipes written for it, at a third of the quantity. Never a swap into a European braise.
“Indian bay leaf”, tej patta, Cinnamomum tamala A cinnamon relative: eugenol-, cinnamaldehyde- or linalool-dominant chemotypes by region (Sharma & Rao 2014) — clove and cassia, not eucalyptus. Biryani, garam masala, Bengali dals. Not a substitute in either direction.

How to Buy: What to Look For

Most of the eating quality is decided before it reaches your knife.

  • Whole dried leaves, the export grade — what Turkish packers call Hand Select: whole, colour intact, no insect marks; the state standard's top grade runs 2.5–10 cm long and 2–4.5 cm wide (TSE 1017, in KUZKA 2020). Olive-green, matte, a slightly wavy edge, a red stalk. Snap test: it cracks clean and, crushed, smells of eucalyptus-clove at once.
  • Fresh branches from a Mediterranean market — soft, greener, harsher. Use within the week or hang them in the shade for two to three weeks; the dried leaf will be the better one (Sellami et al. 2011).
  • The catering pack — 100–250 g from a Turkish exporter is the same leaf as the 5 g jar at a fraction per gram. Buy for the season, not for the boat's life.
  • Ground bay — never. The oil goes fast, and you cannot fish it out.

And the flip side — the specimen you hand straight back:

Grey or brown, crumbling to dust, no smell when crushed: the oil has gone — hand it back. And hand back any “bay” you cannot name. Cherry laurel, Prunus laurocerasus, the hedge that looks like it, has a green stalk, a serrated edge, small nectary glands on the underside by the stalk, and smells of bitter almond when crushed; its leaves carry 1–2.5% cyanogenic glycosides (Malaspina et al. 2022, Toxins). Bay has a red stalk, a slightly wavy edge, no glands, and smells of camphor.

Tested at Sea

Yacht Galley Intel

What survives a 4 m² galley, a swell, and a week without a market.

  • Storage: whole dried leaves in an airtight tub, dark, in the coolest locker — not the one against the engine-room bulkhead. Fresh branches: a week upright in water in the fridge, or hung in the shade to dry. Never the freezer (Díaz-Maroto et al. 2002).
  • Batch prep: a litre of bay milk (below) covers the béchamel, the mash and the rice pudding for a ten-guest week.
  • Dose: a dried leaf weighs 0.1–0.3 g (Karataş et al. 2023). Two leaves per 4 L crew pot, in at the start; one leaf per litre of guest sauce for twenty minutes, then out. Double neither — the camphor turns medicinal.
  • Count in, count out: the leaf never softens. Five patients in one hospital series had a bay leaf lodged in the throat or oesophagus (Buto et al. 1990); one perforated a Meckel's diverticulum (Bell & Mustard 1997). The leaf leaves before the plate.
  • Provisioning tip: in any Turkish or Greek port the pazar or laiki sells hand-picked leaves by weight; a 250 g bag is a season's supply for a fraction of jar prices.

Of all that, one move is worth pinning above the prep bench:

Bay milk, for ten covers. 1 L whole milk, 4 dried leaves cracked in half, half an onion, 4 cloves. Bring to 80 °C (176 °F) — steaming, never boiling — then lid on, off the heat, 30 minutes. Strain, label, chill fast; fridge at 4 °C or below, three days. It is the base of the béchamel, the mash and the rice pudding, with no infusing at service.

The Stories

The Stories

Told at the pass — each one sourced, none invented.

The emperor's thunder wreath. Pliny: of the shrubs of a Roman house, the laurel “is the only one that is never struck by lightning” (Natural History XV.40). Tiberius believed it: “Whenever the sky was lowering, he always wore a laurel wreath” (Suetonius, Tiberius 69).

Why the sack says defne. Ovid's Daphne became Apollo's tree (Metamorphoses I.452–567); Caesar wore his laurel more gladly than any honour because it hid his baldness (Suetonius, Divus Julius 45).

The myth: we take it out because it is poison. It is not — bay is non-toxic; the poisonous look-alike is cherry laurel (Malaspina et al. 2022). It comes out because it stays rigid: leaves lodged in the throat (Buto et al. 1990), the duodenum (Tsang et al. 1999) and the bowel (Bell & Mustard 1997).

Here is the card to pin above the bench.

  • Best season — evergreen, so buy by the crop, not the calendar: the Turkish harvest comes off in late summer, and a new-crop pack in autumn is the freshest you will see (KUZKA 2020).
  • Best with — pulses, milk, fish in liquor, slow pork and lamb, dried figs.
  • Avoid with — anything raw or fast; ground bay; the freezer.
  • Storage — dried whole: airtight, dark, cool, one season. Fresh: fridge a week, or shade-dry two to three weeks.
  • Chef's note: in first, out last, and count them.

And if the card washes overboard, keep this much:

Use the dried leaf, never a frozen one; put it in early with the fat and give it twenty minutes; count every leaf back out.
