Bay Leaf: Better Dried, Never Frozen, Not Poison

Nine leaves in ten are picked by hand from Turkish maquis and cross the border at three dollars a kilo. The leaf rewards drying, punishes the freezer, and was never the poison you were told.

Laurus nobilisThe nymph in the name, the cousin behind the clove, three million years of the same weather — and the state permit it is cut under. Out of the bag: red stalk, wavy edge, a midrib you can feel.

The word carries its history. In Turkish it is defne, in Greek dáfni: both are the name of Daphne, the nymph who became the tree. English bay is the Old French baie, a berry; laurel is the Roman laurus. The family is the Lauraceae, which tells a cook two things: cinnamon and cassia are relatives, so the clove-warm edge is family; and the hedge that looks like it, cherry laurel, is no relation and no food.

Bay is a relic of the subtropical laurel forests that ringed the Tethys Sea. The ice ages squeezed it into refuges around the Mediterranean and on the Atlantic islands, where the laurisilva of Madeira and the Canaries still stands (Rodríguez-Sánchez & Arroyo 2008, Global Ecology and Biogeography).

The leaf in your dry store outlived the ice ages. Its climate requirements have not shifted in three million years — which is why it grows wild on every warm, wet Mediterranean coast and sulks inland.

The Greeks put it on heads before it went into pots; Pliny counted thirteen kinds, and the laurel wreath crowned emperors for six centuries. Two thousand years later it had become the most invisible ingredient in the French kitchen: the parsley-thyme-bay bundle Escoffier fixed as the bouquet garni in Le Guide Culinaire (1903). Today it reaches your dock as a Turkish forest product: cut by villagers under a state permit, graded by leaf length, shade-dried and sacked for export before any jar is printed (KUZKA 2020).

In the mouth: a cool, almost menthol lift first — eucalyptus and camphor — then a resinous middle of pine needle and dried grass, faintly floral, and a clove-like warmth that arrives late and stays. The leaf itself is bitter and drying, like strong tea, which is why it never comes to the plate; what it leaves in a pot is rounder than any of that: a savoury depth that makes beans taste of more than beans and milk taste of a place. Fresh, the same notes run sharper and greener, with a bitterness that drying softens into the clove. Snap a good leaf and it cracks clean, and the whole galley smells of eucalyptus and clove.

Every larder on this coast keeps a leaf in the flour jar, and every grandmother has a reason for it — usually the wrong one. One of those old kitchen habits turns out to be entomology rather than superstition, and one of bay's medical reputations does not survive contact with the dose: both are settled below.

Why It Works

The Science: A Slow Leaf That Wants Fat, Time, and Never the Freezer

Four numbers, then the mechanism that makes them matter.

0.6–1.5%Of the dry leaf is essential oil
26–63%Of that oil is 1,8-cineole, the eucalyptus note
41%Umbellulone in “California bay” — a different tree
90%Of the world's supply is wild-picked in Türkiye

Fresh is not the better leaf, and freezing it to keep it fresh is the one move that wrecks it. Air-dried at room temperature, bay carries more essential oil and more of its main aroma compounds than the fresh leaf (Sellami et al. 2011, Food Chemistry); air-drying or a 45 °C oven lost “hardly any” volatiles, while freezing and freeze-drying brought “substantial losses in bay leaf aroma” (Díaz-Maroto et al. 2002, J. Agric. Food Chem.). The Turkish trade agrees: a fresh leaf does not reach full flavour until weeks after cutting and drying (KUZKA 2020).

Here is the chemistry that proves it:

The Chemistry
What is in the leaf, and why it comes out slowly
  • 1,8-Cineole — 26–63% of the oil across origins (Ordoudi et al. 2022). It boils near 176 °C (NIST), so it rides out a simmer and leaves the pot slowly, on the steam.
  • Sabinene, linalool, α-terpinyl acetate — the resinous-floral middle (sabinene 12.2%, linalool 10.2%; Caputo et al. 2017).
  • Eugenol and methyl eugenol — the clove warmth, among the compounds that rose with air-drying (Sellami et al. 2011): the reason the dried leaf reads rounder than the fresh.
  • Oil cells, not a coating — the oil sits in idioblasts inside a leathery evergreen leaf (Malaspina et al. 2022): a whole leaf gives slowly, a crushed one fast.

Bay is a slow-release aromatic: oil locked in cells inside a tough leaf, and, like every essential oil, hydrophobic. That is why it shows more in a béchamel or a confit than in a thin stock, and why a whole leaf needs twenty to thirty minutes at a simmer. Put the dried leaf in early, with the fat, and give it twenty minutes; if you must keep fresh branches, hang them in the shade to dry — never in the freezer.

Is it a remedy?

Two answers, and they point opposite ways.

  • Swallowed by the gram, it moves blood sugar — in small trials. Forty people with type 2 diabetes took capsules of 1, 2 or 3 g of ground bay daily for thirty days; fasting glucose fell 21–26% against placebo, with lipids improving too (Khan et al. 2009, Journal of Clinical Biochemistry and Nutrition 44:52–56). A later crossover in twenty healthy people found biscuits carrying 6% bay-leaf powder gave a lower glycaemic response than plain ones (Khan et al. 2017, Journal of the American College of Nutrition 36:514–519). Strength: weak — two small trials from overlapping author groups, no large replication.
  • Your pot delivers none of that. A dried leaf weighs 0.1–0.3 g, it goes in whole and it comes out whole: a stew for ten carries a fraction of a gram of leaf matter, none of it eaten. The dose that moved glucose is five to thirty leaves, ground and swallowed, every day — that is a supplement, not a seasoning, and nobody has shown it is safe or useful at that dose for long.

So the leaf is a flavouring, not a medicine. But one folk use of it holds up completely, and it is the one nobody calls medicine:

The leaf in the flour jar is not superstition. Bay's own oil is repellent and toxic to the beetles that live in stored grain and pulses (Mediouni Ben Jemâa et al. 2012, Journal of Stored Products Research) — which is why Turkish packers lay bay leaves among dried figs and raisins to hold them (KUZKA 2020).

  • Where: flour, rice, dried pulses, oats, pasta, dried fruit — every dry-store container that a moth or a weevil would want. Not the spice jars; they are already aromatic.
  • How much: one whole leaf per litre of container, two for a five-kilo sack, laid on top where you can see it. Whole, never ground: the oil is the working part and a ground leaf has already lost it.
  • How often: crush a corner every month or so — when it stops smelling of eucalyptus it has stopped working. That is roughly a season in a warm galley, longer in a cool locker.
  • Its honest limit: the kill figures in that paper come from concentrated oil in a sealed chamber. A leaf in a jar is a repellent, not a fumigation: it keeps them out, it will not clear an infestation you already have. If you find one, the sack goes over the side.
What Every Cuisine Found

The Pairings: Proven, Molecular, and One from the Shore

Two to five things on a card — the classics, the two that share bay's own molecules, and the one guests remember.

TraditionalBay + white beans + olive oil + garlicTürkiye · Greece · Portugal

The slow leaf meets the slow pot: forty minutes at a simmer is exactly the extraction a whole leaf needs, the oil is the solvent, and cineole's cool edge lifts the flat sweetness of starch. The same leaf goes into the jar of dry beans to keep the weevils out (KUZKA 2020).

TraditionalBay + milk + onion + cloveFrance · Britain

The onion piqué, and the reason it works: milk fat is the solvent a hydrophobic oil wants, and the clove doubles the eugenol the dried leaf already carries. Twenty minutes off the heat is enough; boiling it is what makes béchamel taste of nothing.

ModernBay + brown butter + hazelnut + brown crabAtlantic France, reworked

Brown butter is fat plus the same roasted-nut chemistry the crab's brown meat already has; bay's clove-warm end sits under it and its bitterness stops the whole thing turning sweet. Infuse the leaf into the butter as it foams, off the heat at 60 °C, then spoon it over the picked crab and toasted hazelnut.

CompoundBay + cardamom + rice + creamThe shared-molecule surprise

Cardamom is bay's chemical twin: its oil runs about 34% 1,8-cineole and 23% α-terpinyl acetate (Abdelgaleil et al. 2022, Plants) — the same two compounds that carry bay's eucalyptus lift and its resinous middle (Caputo et al. 2017). Use them together and neither reads as itself; they read as one deeper aromatic. A rice pudding with two bay leaves and four bruised pods, infused at 80 °C, tastes of somewhere nobody can name — the same pudding Persia builds on saffron, run on a different molecule.

CompoundBay + juniper + orange peel + coarse saltThe shared-molecule surprise

Juniper's sabinene chemotype is the commonest in the wild (Vuko et al. 2026, Plants), and sabinene is 12% of bay's own oil (Caputo et al. 2017) — the resinous, faintly peppery note both share. That is why a bay-and-juniper cure reads as one flavour rather than two: blitz four leaves with a tablespoon of berries, the peel of an orange and 200 g coarse salt, and bury a side of oily fish or a duck breast in it for six hours.

From the littoralBay + dried figs + red wineDalmatian islands · the Aegean fig trade

Reads wrong on paper, lands on the plate: the leaf that keeps the moth out of the fig is also its best aromatic partner. Turkish packers lay bay leaves among dried figs and raisins to hold freshness and flavour (KUZKA 2020); the Dalmatian fig cake smokvenjak is wrapped in fresh bay leaves and dried in the sun (Croatian National Tourist Board); the same oil is repellent and toxic to stored-grain beetles (Mediouni Ben Jemâa et al. 2012). Poach the figs in red wine with two leaves; the clove-eucalyptus edge cuts the sugar.

Every card above was found the same way: by the molecule two ingredients share. That map is becoming the Pairing Wheel in the Spoon Lab — bay in the middle, everything that shares a compound with it around the rim. It is not ready. It is simmering.

Tested at Sea

Galley Intel: What to Do With It, and How It Keeps

One rule runs the whole field: the oil lives in fat, leaves on steam, and hates the freezer.

Laurel butter250 g butter, six leaves cracked in half, 60 °C for 40 minutes, strain, roll in film, freeze. A coin finishes fish, a beurre blanc or a purée with no leaf in sight.
Bay oil200 ml olive oil, eight leaves, 60 °C for 30 minutes, cool overnight, strain. A week in the fridge.
Bay saltTen leaves blitzed with 100 g flaky salt. Ground leaf loses its oil fast — make it small and often.
Bay smokeDried branches on the embers for the last minute, lid down. Cineole boils near 176 °C, so it rides the smoke onto the flesh.
Bay and lemon syrup200 g sugar, 200 ml water, four leaves, a lemon's peel: ten minutes, then an hour off the heat. Lemonade for the crew, a sour for the guests.
Bay ice creamInfuse a litre of milk with four leaves at 80 °C for 30 minutes; five yolks, 90 g sugar, cooked to 82 °C, chilled, churned. The old English custard — and the plate for the poached figs above.
Not the brine — the fatA water brine takes almost none of the oil. Save the leaves for the roasting tin, under the joint.
DoseA dried leaf weighs 0.1–0.3 g. Two per 4 L crew pot, in at the start; one per litre of guest sauce for twenty minutes, then out.
Storage & buying at seaAirtight, dark, the coolest locker; never the freezer. In any Turkish or Greek port the pazar or laiki sells hand-picked leaves by weight — a season's supply for a fraction of jar prices.

We take it out because it is poison, the dock says. It is not: bay is non-toxic, and the poisonous look-alike is a hedge you can learn to refuse in thirty seconds — below. The leaf comes out because it stays rigid: leaves have lodged in throats (Buto et al. 1990), blocked a duodenum (Tsang et al. 1999) and perforated a bowel (Bell & Mustard 1997). Count them in, count them out.

At the Dock

How to Buy: Three Leaves Sell Under the Name

Only one belongs in a Mediterranean pot — and most of the quality is decided before your knife.

The real oneTrue bay — Laurus nobilisTürkiye · Greece · Italy · Iberia

Flavour profile: soft eucalyptus from cineole over a resinous-floral middle of sabinene and linalool, with a clove warmth that deepens on drying.

Best for: everything above — pulses, milk, fish, braises, figs. The only one of the three that belongs in a Mediterranean pot.

Half the trees will never give you a berry. Bay is dioecious, its star-shaped male and female flowers carried on separate plants (Paparella et al. 2022), so the olive-like black fruit on a Greek market table comes off the female trees alone. It makes no difference to the leaf — but the tree you keep in a pot on deck is a coin toss.

Not a Laurus“California bay” — Umbellularia californicaThe Pacific coast, Oregon to Baja

Flavour profile: harsh camphor-menthol with nothing soft in it. The oil is 41% umbellulone (Kelsey et al. 2007), an irritant that fires the TRPA1 pain receptor in rodent and cell studies (Nassini et al. 2012, Brain).

Best for: American recipes written for it, at a third of the quantity. Never a swap into a European braise.

It earned its other name honestly. Neurologists call it the headache tree because breathing the vapour of a crushed branch can bring on a severe headache — that is the opening line of the 2012 Brain paper, which then traced the crisis to umbellulone and the receptor it shares with mustard oil and tear gas.

A cinnamon relative“Indian bay leaf”, tej patta — Cinnamomum tamalaHimalayan foothills · Bengal

Flavour profile: clove and cassia, never eucalyptus — a true cinnamon relative with several chemotypes, eugenol, cinnamaldehyde or linalool by region (Sharma & Rao 2014).

Best for: biryani, garam masala, Bengali dals. Not a substitute in either direction.

Rome bought this leaf by the shipload. The first-century Greek merchants' handbook, the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea, describes a leaf carried down from the interior, packed into balls and sold in three grades — large-ball, medium-ball, small-ball, sorted by the size of the leaf (§65). That cargo, malabathrum, is generally identified as this leaf: graded by the leaf, two thousand years before Türkiye graded bay the same way.

The label says “Mediterranean” or laurier; the leaf is almost certainly Turkish. Türkiye meets about 90% of world demand (Ünal 2025, BioResources) from wild maquis between Hatay and the Black Sea. In 2019 it exported 13,879 t for $39.9 million — about $2.90 a kilo at the border; hand-selected grades fetched $3.8–4.8/kg in Germany, the US and Britain. In a European shop, ten leaves (6 g) cost about €3: roughly €500 a kilo, over 150 times the border price (KUZKA 2020). A 5 g jar through a ship's agent pays for the jar.

  • What the trade may do to it: dried herbs and spices are the one food class the EU lets be irradiated for microbial control, up to 10 kGy (Directive 1999/3/EC) — and the law makes it visible: irradiated or treated with ionising radiation must be on the label, on a notice by the bin if sold loose, and in the ingredient list of a blend (Directive 1999/2/EC, art. 6). The US allows 30 kGy (21 CFR 179.26). Read the small print.
  • The most natural route: Turkish bay is not farmed: it is cut from wild forest under permit and shade-dried, never in the sun, which drives the oil out (KUZKA 2020); exporters test for pesticide residues and microbes to meet buyers' standards. Certified organic wild collection means the forest received nothing but organic-permitted treatments for three years before harvest (Regulation (EU) 2018/848, Annex II). Ask for the certificate, and buy whole leaves — ground bay cannot be inspected and its oil is half gone.
  • The chef's own supply: a bay tree in a pot lives for years on a sunny deck and gives more leaves than a galley uses; hang the cut branches in the shade for two to three weeks and they become the better leaf.
  • Whole dried leaves, the export grade — what Turkish packers call Hand Select: whole, colour intact, no insect marks; the state standard's top grade runs 2.5–10 cm long (TSE 1017, in KUZKA 2020). Olive-green, matte, a slightly wavy edge, a red stalk. It should snap, not bend.
  • Fresh branches from a Mediterranean market — softer, greener, harsher. Use within the week or hang them to dry; the dried leaf will be the better one.
  • The catering pack — 100–250 g from a Turkish exporter is the same leaf as the 5 g jar at a fraction per gram. Buy for the season, not for the boat's life.

And the specimen you hand straight back:

Grey or brown, crumbling to dust, no smell when crushed: the oil has gone. And hand back any “bay” you cannot name. Cherry laurel, Prunus laurocerasus, the hedge that looks like it, has a green stalk, a serrated edge, small glands on the underside by the stalk, and smells of bitter almond when crushed; its leaves carry 1–2.5% cyanogenic glycosides (Malaspina et al. 2022, Toxins). Bay has a red stalk, a wavy edge, no glands, and smells of camphor.

Use the dried leaf, never a frozen one; put it in early with the fat and give it twenty minutes; count every leaf back out.
