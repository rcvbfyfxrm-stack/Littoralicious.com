Biltong: The Vinegar Is the Safety Step, Not the Seasoning

Snap a good stick and it breaks dark and dry at the edge, ruby at the heart: salt first, then toasted coriander, then the long sour finish of the vinegar. That sourness is not there for flavour. Biltong is never cooked, and every serious test of it turns on the same two things, the acid bath and the controlled hang. Get those right and the rest is seasoning. The settlers who added the vinegar did not know they were adding the safety step.

The one idea: with no heat, the vinegar bath and six days of moving air are biltong’s kill step. Take the vinegar below about 3 % of the batch, or hurry the hang, and you have seasoned raw beef.

Where it comes from

The name is Dutch and blunt: bil, the rump or buttock, and tong, a tongue, for the strips it is cut into. That is why this recipe uses silverside, the big lean muscle of the back leg the word points to. Before the name, the story is told more than it is recorded. The main review of biltong processing words it carefully: it has been said that the peoples of southern Africa salted and dried meat before the settlers came, and the settlers allegedly took up the method, adding vinegar and the spices of the Cape Colony. Their vinegar, the story goes, came from the grapes of the Huguenot vineyards.

The documented part comes later. The Voortrekkers cured and dried their meat for up to two weeks and wrapped it in cloth for the wagons before they set out. Hung in the shade in winter, when the air is cold and dry, it kept. Two weeks on a wagon was the test the old method had to pass. The lab work of the last twenty years asks whether it passes a stricter one, and the answer turns on what the settlers added, not on what they found.

Hanging it longer does not make it safe. In a US trial, biltong made from beef inoculated with four pathogens was hung for 17 to 26 days, and the process cut them by only 1.2 to 3.8 log. That is a real kill, but short of the 5-log reduction of Salmonella that the USDA’s jerky guidance is built around. Droëwors, the fattier sausage, did worse, “probably because of the higher fat content”. — Burnham, Hanson, Koshick & Ingham, Journal of Food Safety 28 (2008) 198–209. DOI: 10.1111/j.1745-4565.2008.00114.x; drying time as summarised by Jones et al., S. Afr. J. Anim. Sci. 47 (2017) 743–757

So time is not the lever. An Oklahoma State team changed one variable at a time to find out what is.

They cut beef round into pieces 1.9 cm thick, inoculated them with five strains of Salmonella, marinated them and hung them at 22–25 °C and 55 % relative humidity with a fan running. Nearly every version passed 5 log within six to eight days. Then they tested the levers. Salt at 1.7, 2.2 or 2.7 % made no significant difference. Vinegar did: at 2 % of the formulation it failed to reach 5 log in eight days; at 3 and 4 % it passed. An overnight marinade with 10 % vinegar reached 5 log after six days on the hook. The salt seasons the meat; the acid, then the air, does the killing. — Karolenko, Bhusal, Nelson & Muriana, Microorganisms 8 (2020) 791. DOI: 10.3390/microorganisms8050791

Method

0/0 steps done

  1. Bil means the rump: the name tells you the cut.

    Cut with the grain. Firm the silverside in the freezer for 30–45 min, then take off every scrap of silverskin. Slice along the grain into strips 2–2.5 cm thick, 4–5 cm wide and 20–30 cm long. Keep every strip the same thickness: the thickest one sets the date the whole batch is done.

    Why
    Cut across the grain and the dried strip crumbles; cut along it and it slices clean across the fibres when you serve it. Thickness matters more than anything else because water leaves from the surface, so the centre of a thick strip is always the last part to dry. The validated process used pieces 1.9 cm thick; nothing thicker than about 2.5 cm was tested. — Karolenko et al., Microorganisms 2020

  2. Coriander is the taste of biltong, and it only comes out once the seed is toasted.

    Toast, crack, mix. Dry-toast the coriander in a pan until it smells strongly, then crack it coarsely in a mortar; don’t grind it to dust. Mix it with the salt, sugar, pepper and bicarb. If you use curing salt, weigh it to the hundredth of a gram: 1.9 g per kg of meat is the EU ceiling, not the 2.5 g printed on most packets.

    Why
    Since 9 October 2025, EU law caps nitrite in non-heat-treated meat products at 80 mg/kg going in, measured as the nitrite ion (sodium nitrite × 0.67). At the US dose of 2.5 g of a 6.25 % cure per kg, you add 156 mg/kg of sodium nitrite, which is 105 mg/kg as nitrite ion: over the limit. 1.9 g per kg gives 79. Nitrite is optional here. It brings the cured colour and acts as an antioxidant, which slows fat turning rancid; the safety work is the vinegar’s. If your cure is not 6.25 %, recalculate to 80 mg of nitrite per kg. — Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/2108, as tabled in FSAI Guidance Note 43 (2025); Honikel, Meat Science 78 (2008) 68–76. DOI: 10.1016/j.meatsci.2007.05.030

  3. Season and rest. Lay the strips in a tray and press the cure into every face, ends included. Cover and rest for 1 hour at 5 °C or below, until the salt has pulled a film of liquid to the surface.

  4. This is the step the settlers added, and the one the lab says matters.

    The vinegar bath, overnight. Pour the vinegar and Worcestershire over the strips, turn them to coat, cover and refrigerate for 12–18 hours at 5 °C or below, turning them once halfway through. Malt vinegar gives the classic South African sourness; cider gives a cleaner one.

    Why
    The closest validated process to this one marinated the beef for 16–20 hours at 5 °C in a marinade that was 10 % white vinegar, then reached a 5-log Salmonella kill after six days of drying. A 2 % vinegar dose failed to reach it in eight days. The 450 ml here is about 12 % of the whole batch, comfortably past the floor. — Karolenko et al., Microorganisms 2020

  5. Drain, weigh, hang. Lift the strips out and pat them lightly; don’t rinse them. Dust on a little more cracked coriander for the crust. Mark one strip of average thickness as the test strip and write its weight down: this is day 0. Hang the strips on stainless S-hooks, 3–4 cm apart, touching neither each other nor the walls.

  6. Biltong dries in moving air, not in heat.

    Dry for at least six days. Keep the box at 20–26 °C and 45–60 % relative humidity, with a small fan moving air past the strips the whole time. Never take a batch down before day 6, whatever the scale says.

    Why
    The kill was measured over time on the hook, not by weight lost, so six days is the floor. Too much heat, too fast an airflow or too dry a room forms a crust before the centre can shed its water, a fault called case-hardening. Even under good conditions the gradient is steep: at the end of the validated process the outer surface read a water activity of 0.80–0.81 and the inside face 0.89–0.90. — Jones et al., S. Afr. J. Anim. Sci. 2017; Karolenko et al., Microorganisms 2020

  7. Read it by weight. Weigh the test strip every day and work out how much it has lost: (day-0 weight − today’s weight) ÷ day-0 weight. About 35 % is wet, soft and red in the middle. About 50 % is the classic medium stick. 60 % or more is dry and snaps. The validated process finished at up to 60 %. Wet biltong sits outside what was tested: keep it in the fridge and eat it within the week.

One cure, three batches — wagyu, crew, dock

Same ratio, three jobs. What changes is the meat and how strictly the process is held.

Crew
3 kg → ≈ 1.5 kg		The recipe above · cure 5.7 g, optional · bath 12–18 h · down at ≥ 6 days, 50 % loss
Dock sale
10 kg → ≈ 5 kg		Silverside · salt 220 g · coriander 150 g · sugar 100 g · pepper 50 g · bicarb 10 g · cure 19 g, every batch · 1.5 L vinegar, 300 ml Worcestershire · bath 16 h, fixed · released at ≥ 6 days and ≥ 50 %, logged · 50 bags of 100 g

Wagyu. Fat carries little water, so a marbled strip never reaches 50 % loss; stop when the lean is firm right through. In the droëwors trial, fat probably shielded the pathogens, so the wagyu gets the full vinegar dose and the full six days, and it lives in the fridge: vacuum-pack it, eat it within two weeks, or freeze it. Above BMS 6–7 the centre stays greasy and wet. Here the cure’s antioxidant work earns its place, holding back the rancid note in the fat.

Crew. One biltong box with a fan, or about 1.5 m of rail. A batch every two weeks keeps a stick in the mess between services.

Dock sale. Every bag has to be the same, so nothing is adjusted to taste: fixed bath time, cure in every batch, and a batch log (lot number, day-0 weight, the test strip daily, temperature and humidity) with release only at day 6 or later and 50 % loss. The label carries the name, the ingredients with fish (Worcestershire) and barley (malt vinegar) emphasised, net weight, lot, best-before, storage and your name and address. To price it, divide the meat cost plus about 10 % for spices, vinegar and bags by the yield, because you buy 10 kg and sell 5.

Selling is a different job from cooking, and the law treats it that way.

Before the first bag. In the EU every food business must register with its competent authority and run a hazard-analysis plan based on HACCP principles (Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, Articles 5 and 6). Regulation (EC) No 853/2004, which adds establishment approval for products of animal origin, does not apply to retail sold direct to the final consumer except where it says so, and generally does once you supply other businesses. The finished product must also meet the nitrite limit of 45 mg/kg residual, and drying concentrates whatever nitrite remains, so only a lab test proves a batch compliant. Whether a yacht galley can be registered at all is the first question: ask the local authority (in Barcelona, the Agència de Salut Pública de Barcelona) before you sell anything.

Weight loss stalls at 40 %The room is too humid → dehumidifier or air conditioning; a strip cannot dry in air wetter than itself

Charter prep & storage

At sea, humidity is the enemy. A drying product and the air around it trade water until they meet: air more humid than the product puts water back in. A stick near water activity 0.85 cannot finish drying in a cabin at 85 % relative humidity. Dry it in an air-conditioned space or beside a dehumidifier, with the fan running; never in the engine room or a warm oven. — Jones et al., S. Afr. J. Anim. Sci. 2017

Storage. Keep finished sticks whole, in paper bags rather than plastic, somewhere cool and dry, and slice to order. Vacuum-pack and freeze anything you won’t eat within a few weeks. Wet biltong and wagyu always go in the fridge.

Once you own the ratio — 2.2 % salt, vinegar well past 3 %, six days of moving air — the rest is seasoning: chilli, smoke, game for beef. Cook once, serve two tiers: the guests get the wagyu, sliced paper-thin across the grain and laid out as charcuterie; the crew get silverside sticks from the same cure, the same bath and the same box, cut thick for the pocket. The vinegar the settlers poured in for taste is still the step that keeps it safe.

From the box to the people eating it, the only decision left is how thin to slice.

Biltong is a hand food. The crew take a stick on watch and cut it with a pocket knife; guests take it thin, off a board, with a drink. Slice it to order, across the grain, and leave the sticks whole until then.
