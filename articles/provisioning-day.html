Provisioning Day: Twenty-Two Mouths, Seven Days, One Map

The quay delivery lands in one go and the cold chain is measured in minutes; the protocol that gets a charter week's food from the pallet to the right shelf before anything warms — with the Station Map to pin above the fridges and an A5 card to print.

Probe at the tailgate, not at the fridge — and write the number on the crate before it crosses the passerelle. Every other line of the protocol exists to make that one reading count.

The Problem

A charter week's food arrives once, as a wall of crates, on a quay that is above 30 °C.

Twelve guests and ten crew, three meals a day, seven days: 462 covers, and they do not arrive over the week. They arrive in one hour. The USDA puts the constraint in two sentences: between 4 °C and 60 °C bacteria can double in as little as twenty minutes, and food should never sit out of refrigeration for more than two hours — above 32 °C, no more than one. The van door opens and the hour starts, for the chicken at the bottom of the last crate as much as for the box on top.

And the obvious fix — get it all inside quickly, sort it later — is the one that fails, because the hour is not yours alone. The FDA Food Code gives a chilled food that has left 5 °C a total of four hours before it is discarded (six if it never climbs above 21 °C, and only where someone is writing the times down). The supplier's cold room, the loading bay, the van, the wait behind the other boat's delivery: all of it was spent before the tailgate came down, and none of it is written on the crate. Provisioning day is the day the cold chain is either handed over intact or quietly broken, and nobody finds out which until Thursday.

The Fix

Receive before you stow — five stations, three numbers, sixty seconds a crate.

Three numbers run the day, all three from the rulebooks rather than anyone's habit: 5 °C for chilled food on receipt (Food Code §3-202.11, §3-501.16 — the CDC's Vessel Sanitation Program applies the same figure on ships), −18 °C for frozen at every point, and the hour on the quay. Everything else is choreography.

  1. Probe at the tailgate, before a crate moves. Between packs, in the middle of the crate — not stabbed through the top pack, which reads the pack nearest the door. Not in the galley after the carry, when refusing something means carrying it back.
  2. Write the number on the crate. Marker, on the cardboard, with the time. The reading is the receipt: a reading nobody wrote down was never taken.
  3. Refuse on paper, before the driver leaves. With the reading, on the delivery note. The supplier who argues with a written 9 °C is rare; the one who argues with a phone call the next day is not.
  4. Then sort, in shade, into three lanes — freezer, fridge, dry. Nothing is opened on deck and nothing waits there; the deck is not a store.

Three climates, not eight, because nobody has eight rooms: the UC Davis chart collapses the storage groups exactly that way (Thompson, Kader & Sylva, 1996), on the assumption of seven days or less — which is the length of a charter. Every pack is dated twice, received and opened; a chilled ready-to-eat item carries a use-by no more than seven days out at 5 °C, and the day it was opened counts as day one (§3-501.17). Thursday's lamb comes out of the freezer on Tuesday, into the fridge or under running water at 21 °C or below — never onto the counter overnight (§3-501.13).

Before coffee, probe the warmest shelf of every cold unit and write the number down — the probe's number, not the door display's. Any unit above 5 °C: move the contents, find the cause, write both down. Five minutes for four units. The Maritime Labour Convention requires frequent, documented inspections of the food and the galley under the master's authority (Standard A3.2), and your log is what that inspection reads.

Why It Works

The mechanism is a clock you inherited, and a thermometer that is lying to you.

The USDA's twenty-minute doubling time is why the hour matters; the FDA's four-hour total is why it is not your hour alone. European law allows a frozen product short upward swings of no more than 3 °C in transport — so a frozen crate reading −15 °C at the tailgate is inside tolerance, and one reading −10 °C is not, which is a judgement you can only make with a probe in your hand. And the sensor belongs in the warmest part of the unit (§4-204.112), because that is the part that decides. On most galley fridges that is the top of the door shelf — which is also where nothing that matters should live.

This is the trade's own habit turned the right way round. Every chef probes; most probe in the galley, once the crates are in. The mechanism says the reading is worth almost nothing there, because by then the inherited clock has run and refusal has stopped being a decision and become a negotiation.

What It Costs

Sixty seconds a crate, and the one place this makes things worse.

The whole cost is a minute a crate at the tailgate and a marker in your pocket. What it buys is the only reading anyone can act on. Honestly stated: we have no counted tally for this protocol and will not invent one — the A5 card carries a line for yours, services run against cold units ever found above 5 °C at the morning reading. Fill it for a season and the Weekly Brief will print what comes back, boat unnamed.

Where it makes things worse: a driver on a tight round, in the rain, with the tender waiting. Probing every crate on the quay is the right call and it is also the slowest one, and the temptation is to do half of it and call it done — which produces a delivery note that looks checked and a fridge that is not. Either probe every chilled and frozen crate or write on the note that you did not, and say which.
