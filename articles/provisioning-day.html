The Problem: 462 Covers Land in One Hour

Twelve guests and ten crew, three meals a day, seven days: 462 covers. A charter week's food does not arrive over the week; it arrives once, as a wall of crates on a quay, and a Mediterranean quay in August is routinely above 30 °C. That is the whole problem, and it is a parameter, not a complaint. The United States Department of Agriculture puts it in two sentences: between 4 °C and 60 °C bacteria can double in number in as little as twenty minutes, and food should never sit out of refrigeration for more than two hours — above 32 °C, no more than one hour. The van door opens and the hour starts, for the chicken at the bottom of the last crate as much as for the box on top.

The rule: a galley fridge exists to hold 5 °C, and the law's clock was already running when the van left the depot. Receive as if half the hour is gone — because it usually is.

It is worse than an hour, because you inherit time you cannot see. The FDA Food Code gives a chilled food that leaves 5 °C a total of four hours before it is discarded (six if it never climbs above 21 °C, and only when someone is writing the times down). The supplier's cold room, the loading bay, the van, the wait behind the other boat's delivery: all of that was spent before the tailgate came down, and none of it is written on the crate. Provisioning day is not the day the food is bought. It is the day the cold chain is either handed over intact or quietly broken, and nobody will know which until Thursday.

The System: Five Stations, Three Numbers — with the Station Map

Three numbers run provisioning day, and all three come from the rulebooks rather than from anyone's habit. Five degrees: chilled food must be at 5 °C or below when it is received, and it stays there (FDA Food Code, §3-202.11 and §3-501.16; the CDC's Vessel Sanitation Program applies the same figure on ships). Minus eighteen: frozen food is frozen at −18 °C or lower at every point, by European definition, and a food shipped frozen must arrive frozen. And the hour on the quay. Everything else is choreography: who stands where so that those three numbers survive the walk from the van to the shelf.

The cold chain in numbers: the USDA's doubling time of twenty minutes is why the hour matters, and the FDA's four-hour total is why it is not your hour alone. European law allows a frozen product short upward swings of no more than 3 °C in transport — a frozen crate reading −15 °C at the tailgate is inside that tolerance; one reading −10 °C is not. The thermometer that decides all of this is the probe in your hand, not the display on the fridge door: the code puts the sensor in the warmest part of the unit for the same reason.

The Quay: Receive Before You Stow

The single habit that separates a galley that runs from a galley that hopes is where the thermometer comes out. Not in the galley, after the carry, when the crates are already inside and refusing anything means carrying it back. At the tailgate, before a crate has moved, while the driver is still holding the delivery note.

  • Probe between packs, not through film. A probe slipped between two packs in the middle of a crate reads the crate; a probe stabbed through the top pack reads the pack that has been nearest the door.
  • Write the number on the crate. Marker, on the cardboard, with the time. The reading is the receipt; a reading nobody wrote down was never taken.
  • Frozen means frozen. The Food Code and the Vessel Sanitation Program say the same thing: a food shipped frozen is received frozen, and chilled food is received free of any evidence of previous temperature abuse. The pack that has thawed and refrozen carries its own confession — ice crystals inside the film, packs frozen into a single block.
  • Eggs have their own number. Raw eggs are received from a unit holding an ambient 7 °C or less (FDA Food Code, §3-202.11).
  • Refuse on paper. A refused crate is written on the delivery note before the driver leaves, with the reading. The supplier who argues with a written 9 °C is rare; the one who argues with a phone call the next day is not.
Do not get it all inside and sort it later. Once the crates are aboard, refusal stops being a decision and becomes a negotiation, and the four-hour clock you inherited from the van does not pause while you negotiate.

The Cold Ladder: Where Each Thing Goes

The map above puts every category on a shelf; the ladder is the logic that lets a stewardess stow a crate correctly without asking. Raw animal foods sit below anything that will be eaten without cooking, and the species are kept apart from one another — beef, fish, lamb, pork, poultry — which is the FDA's own list in §3-302.11. Inside that, the European temperatures decide the order: poultry at 4 °C or less, minced meat at 2 °C, offal at 3 °C, other meat at 7 °C, and fresh fish held at a temperature approaching that of melting ice, which on a yacht means a drawer of flaked ice with a drain under it.

  • Produce is three climates, not one. The UC Davis chart (Thompson, Kader and Sylva, 1996) collapses eight storage groups into three for exactly the reason a boat needs: nobody has eight rooms. Group 1 at 0–2 °C is the leaves, the cole crops, temperate fruit and berries. Group 2 at 7–10 °C is citrus, subtropical fruit and the fruit-type vegetables. Group 3 at 13–18 °C is roots, hard-rind squash, melons and most tropical fruit — bananas go in the dry store, not the fridge. The chart assumes seven days or less and ethylene under 1 ppm, which is why the ripening fruit lives away from the salad.
  • Every pack is dated on receipt. Received date on the way in; opened date when the film comes off. A chilled ready-to-eat item held more than 24 hours after opening carries a use-by no more than seven days out at 5 °C, and the day it was opened counts as day one (FDA Food Code, §3-501.17).
  • Thaw in the fridge, or under running water. Under refrigeration at 5 °C or less, or fully submerged under running water at 21 °C or below; never on the counter overnight (§3-501.13). Thursday's lamb comes out of the freezer on Tuesday.
  • A thermometer in every unit, in the warmest place. Each cold-holding unit carries its own thermometer, with the sensor where the unit is warmest (§4-204.112). On most galley fridges that is the door shelf, top, which is also where nothing that matters should live.
Name, received, opened. Three words on every pack, in marker, before it goes on the shelf. Sixty seconds a crate at the tailgate is the whole cost; an unlabelled fridge on day five is the whole price.
The fridge door is the finish line, and on an August quay it is one hour away.

The Habit: The Morning Reading

Provisioning day hands over a cold chain; the morning reading is what keeps it. It is the least glamorous five minutes in the galley and the only ones an inspector will ask to see.

  1. Before coffee, probe the warmest shelf of every cold unit and write the number in the log — the probe's number, not the door display's.
  2. Any unit above 5 °C: move the contents, find the cause, write both down. The reading that was fixed and not written is the reading that is missing.
  3. The freezer reads −18 °C or lower, or it is a fridge with ambitions and its contents are on the clock.
  4. Sign the line. The Maritime Labour Convention requires frequent, documented inspections of the food, the stores and the galley, carried out by or under the authority of the master (Standard A3.2). Your log is what that inspection reads.
One reading per cold unit, warmest shelf, written down, before service. Budget five minutes for four units. Miss it once and the next probe you can trust is the inspector's.

Proof It Scales: The Same Numbers at Three Thousand Covers

The test of a galley protocol is whether it survives a bigger boat, and this one was borrowed from the biggest. The CDC's Vessel Sanitation Program inspects every ship with a foreign itinerary carrying thirteen or more passengers, unannounced, twice a year; its 2018 Operations Manual receives chilled food at 5 °C or below, hot food at 57 °C or above, frozen food frozen, and everything free of evidence of previous temperature abuse — the same lines, almost word for word, as the FDA Food Code, and the same lines as the Station Map above. A 3,000-passenger ship and a 45-metre yacht are running the same three numbers; what changes is the number of thermometers.

What this article does not have is a tally of its own, and it will not invent one. The card carries a line for yours: services run, and cold units ever found above 5 °C at the morning reading. Fill it for a season. If you send it in, the Weekly Brief prints what comes back — counts and conditions, boat unnamed.

Setup Checklist

Before the van arrives.
  • A probe thermometer in your pocket, checked in a glass of iced water — it should read 0 °C — and a marker with it.
  • Three crates or bags marked FREEZER, FRIDGE, DRY on the shaded side of the deck: the sort lanes.
  • A thermometer in every cold unit, sensor on the warmest shelf, and the freezer already reading −18 °C or lower before a single pack arrives.
  • The Station Map laminated above Fridge 1, the log sheet beside it, and the A5 card in the crew mess.
  • Two shelves cleared in Fridge 1 (raw, bottom) and one in Fridge 2 (leaves, coldest corner) — a full fridge on provisioning day means the last crate sits on the deck while someone rearranges.
  • The refusal sentence rehearsed, in the supplier's language: “This crate reads nine degrees; it goes back, and I am writing it on the note.”
The One Thing: probe at the tailgate. Every other line of this protocol exists to make that one reading count.
