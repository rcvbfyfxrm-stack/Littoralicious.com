Butter at Four Euros a Kilo: Fill the Freezer Before the Curve Turns

EU butter was quoted at €398 per 100 kg in the week to 16 August — 45% under last year — and the first tick up is in. Cream is tightening and the futures curve has turned for the autumn. Four to six weeks to buy the season's butter and freeze it.

EU butter was quoted at €398 per 100 kg in the week ending 16 August 2026 — 45% below the same week last year, and up 0.9% on the week before, the first tick upward in the series (European Commission Milk Market Observatory, updated 19 August). Western European 82% butter traded at $4,425–4,750 a tonne in the same fortnight, unchanged at the bottom of the range and up slightly at the top (USDA Dairy Market News, 10–14 August). The window is four to six weeks: the cream is tightening and the futures curve has turned.

Butter is the heaviest dairy line a charter galley buys, and every sauce, pastry and crew lunch of the autumn runs on it — this is the week the season's butter costs half what it did last summer, and the last week it will be cheap.

That window is the whole story, so here is what to do with it before it shuts.

The Move

Order the autumn's butter this week, by the case, and put it in the freezer.

Order 10 kg of unsalted AOP butter — Isigny or Charentes-Poitou — in 250 g or 500 g blocks through a Rungis wholesaler or your provisioner, this week. Vacuum-bag it in 1 kg lots and freeze at −18 °C (0 °F); the USDA FoodKeeper gives frozen butter six to nine months at best quality, so date every bag and rotate unsalted first. Keep 2 kg in the fridge at 4 °C (39 °F) for the working week and thaw the next kilo in the fridge overnight, never on the bench. Ten kilos is a six-week autumn season at 1.5 kg a week for a ten-guest boat.

Here is why that call is right this week and would be wrong in three.

Why It's Happening

Read the cause and you can see the next move coming, not just react to this week's price.

The EU weighted average butter quotation stood at €398 per 100 kg for week 33, ending 16 August 2026, 45% below the same period of 2025 (European Commission, Milk Market Observatory, EU averages of main dairy commodities, updated 19 August 2026). Evidence Grade: official price index · coverage = EU-27, Member State quotations weighted by production under Regulation (EU) 2017/1185 · Strength: firm.

Last summer's butter was the dearest on record; it fell by roughly a fifth in the last quarter of 2025 and again in the first of 2026 as milk output grew in every big exporting region at once, cold stores filled and buyers stepped back. Through early summer the churns ran busy on abundant cream (USDA Dairy Market News). The spot price you see today is the bottom of that slide.

The driver now is cream. European contacts report cream availability tightening and butter production lightening in August, with demand steady (USDA, 10–14 August); deliveries fall from the early-summer peak into the autumn, and every litre that goes to cheese takes its fat with it. The market has priced the turn: on 12 August the nearby EEX butter futures closed with clear upward momentum, August to October strongest, November and December firming behind them (USDA, reporting EEX).

And the cream is not only scarcer from here; the butter it made this summer is different.

The cause changes the butter itself, not only its price. The blocks in the chain now were churned from summer milk, and summer milk off pasture carries more β-carotene and a different fat than winter silage milk: in a controlled Irish comparison, butter from pasture-fed cows held 5.16 mg/kg of trans-β-carotene against 2.27 in butter from indoor-fed cows, and scored differently on sensory panels (O'Callaghan et al. 2016, Journal of Dairy Science); across a Sicilian year the pigment in milk fat ran from 9.7 µg/g in spring to 0.8 in autumn (Marino et al. 2012). Freeze it now and you freeze the yellow half of the year.

That is the product; now the money.

The €398 is the EU average for an anonymous 82% block; the butter worth freezing carries a name. At the Rungis market a wholesaler lists Beurre d'Isigny AOP doux at €30.40 excluding VAT for ten 250 g blocks — €12.16 a kilo (Au Fromager de Rungis, 26 August 2026); a Paris delivery grocer lists the same 250 g block at €3.79, €15.16 a kilo (Mon Marché, 26 August 2026). Between the cow and the quay the price triples once for the appellation — Normandy pasture, cream ripened under the PDO rules — and again for the wholesaler and the provisioner. Buy at the wholesale step; the appellation premium is real, the quay premium is not.

Strip the label off and the signal reads the same: it is the cream, not the brand, that decides what you pay.

The cow's price halved since last summer; the shelf price did not, and the curve has turned.

Read the table back in one sentence: the block price is at the bottom, the named butter is at its yearly best, and the futures market has already stopped waiting — which is exactly what The Move says.

What it means for the people you feed: the beurre monté stays under the lobster and the puff pastry stays on the card through the autumn at last summer's half-price; the quiet swap is the commodity block into the sofrito and the roasting tin, with the AOP kept for the pass, the pastry and the Tuesday crew lunch that gets a proper butter sauce.

Make that swap badly, though, and the saving turns into waste.

The trap is the freezer itself: butter frozen in its paper picks up every odour in a galley freezer and dries at the edges, salted and unsalted get muddled in pastry, and a block thawed on the bench weeps. Vacuum-bag, label salt and date, thaw in the fridge, and do not buy past nine months of use — frozen butter is safe after that, but it is no longer the butter you paid for (USDA FoodKeeper).

No supplier holds, so carry a fallback before the window closes.

The hedge is clarified butter: a kilo of AOP clarified to ghee keeps for months in a cool locker without a freezer, and any French wholesale market on the route sells the same appellation blocks at the Rungis step. The honest uncertainty: a wet, mild autumn can loosen the cream again and flatten the curve — the futures are a market's bet, not a forecast — so buy the season, not the year.

Ten kilos of named butter, this week, vacuum-bagged and frozen. Tested at sea.

The one thing

Buy the autumn's butter now at the wholesale step, freeze it in kilo lots at −18 °C, and use it within six months — the block price is at its bottom and the curve has turned.
