Butter at Four Euros a Kilo: Fill the Freezer Before the Curve Turns

EU butter was quoted at €398 per 100 kg in the week to 16 August — 45% under last year — and the first tick up is in. Cream is tightening and the futures curve has turned for the autumn. Four to six weeks to buy the season's butter and freeze it.

Order ten kilos of the autumn butter this week at the wholesale step, vacuum it in kilo lots and freeze it at −18 °C. The block price is at its bottom and the curve has already turned.

The Alert

The cheapest butter in two years, for about a month, and it is the good half of the year's milk.

What's happening. EU butter was quoted at €398 per 100 kg in the week ending 16 August 2026, 45% below the same week last year — and up 0.9% on the week before, the first tick upward in the series (European Commission Milk Market Observatory, updated 19 August; official price index, EU-27, Member State quotations weighted by production under Regulation (EU) 2017/1185. Strength: firm). Western European 82% butter traded at $4,425–4,750 a tonne in the same fortnight (USDA Dairy Market News, 10–14 August). That is the bottom of a slide that began last autumn, when milk output grew in every big exporting region at once and cold stores filled.

The context. The driver now is cream, and it is tightening: European contacts report butter production lightening through August with demand steady, and on 12 August the nearby EEX butter futures closed with clear upward momentum, August to October strongest (USDA, reporting EEX). Deliveries fall from the early-summer peak into autumn, and every litre that goes to cheese takes its fat with it. Note also what the €398 is: an anonymous 82% block, €3.98 a kilo. The butter worth freezing carries a name — Beurre d'Isigny AOP at a Rungis wholesaler runs €12.16 a kilo against €15.16 for the same block from a Paris delivery grocer. The appellation premium is real; the quay premium is not. Buy at the wholesale step.

The cow’s price halved since last summer; the shelf price did not, and the curve has turned.

How it hits us. Butter is the heaviest dairy line a charter galley buys, and every sauce, pastry and crew lunch of the autumn runs on it: the beurre monté stays under the lobster and the puff pastry stays on the card at half of last summer's cost. And the cause changes the product, not just the price — these blocks were churned from summer pasture milk, which carries 5.16 mg/kg of trans-β-carotene against 2.27 in butter from indoor-fed cows (O'Callaghan et al. 2016, Journal of Dairy Science); across a Sicilian year the pigment runs from 9.7 µg/g in spring to 0.8 in autumn (Marino et al. 2012). Freeze it now and you freeze the yellow half of the year.

Order 10 kg of unsalted AOP butter — Isigny or Charentes-Poitou, in 250 g or 500 g blocks — through a Rungis wholesaler or your provisioner, this week. Vacuum-bag it in 1 kg lots and freeze at −18 °C (0 °F); the USDA FoodKeeper gives frozen butter six to nine months at best quality, so label salt and date, and rotate unsalted first. Keep 2 kg in the fridge at 4 °C for the working week and thaw the next kilo in the fridge overnight, never on the bench. Ten kilos is a six-week autumn at 1.5 kg a week for a ten-guest boat. The trap is the freezer itself: butter frozen in its paper picks up every odour in a galley freezer and dries at the edges. If you cannot spare the freezer space, clarify a kilo — ghee keeps for months in a cool locker.
