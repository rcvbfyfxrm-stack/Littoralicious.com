The Ship's Cook Certificate: The Ten-Crew Line That Decides Whether You Are Legal

Under MLC 2006 a yacht with a prescribed manning of ten or more must carry a trained and qualified cook — and the certificate that proves it depends on the flag, not the chef. The named rule, the three cases, where to train, what it pays, and the trap at the port-state inspection.

What changed, and what it costs you
  • The dockside line is “yacht chefs don't need a ship's cook ticket.” Under MLC 2006 that is true only below a line the flag draws at ten crew — and most 45-metre boats sit on it.
  • The rule: a ship ordinarily operating with ten or more seafarers must carry a trained and qualified cook (MLC 2006, Standard A3.2); the UK adds a 60-mile test, Cayman adds three days or 36 hours from a safe port.
  • The move: get the certificate or the flag's recognition before the next contract, and get everyone else in the galley a Level 2 food-safety qualification — that part applies below ten too.

The Situation

A merchant-ship rule now decides who may cook for the crew on a 50-metre yacht.

The dock says the ship's cook certificate is a merchant-navy thing: yacht chefs are hired on their food, and a Level 2 hygiene card covers the rest. The Maritime Labour Convention does not distinguish a bulk carrier from a commercially registered yacht: seafarers employed as ships' cooks must be trained and qualified for their position, and a ship ordinarily operating with ten or more seafarers must carry one (MLC 2006, Regulation 3.2 and Standard A3.2).

It persisted because most yachts sat under the line and inspectors had bigger fish. Two things changed: a 50-metre's crew list now runs to ten or twelve, and the UK rewrote its route in MSN 1846, cutting the sea-service requirement from five years to one month — a ticket built for career cooks became one a yacht chef can hold by the end of a season.

The line is a headcount, not a length: “ships which ordinarily operate with fewer than 10 seafarers on board” are exempt from the ship's cook requirement under the UK notice (MSN 1846 (M) Amendment 1, para 2.2) — the tenth crew member, not the tenth metre, is what makes your certificate mandatory.

The Rules and Reality

Two definitions decide everything: who counts as the ship's cook, and how many seafarers the ship ordinarily carries.

The Convention applies to ships ordinarily engaged in commercial activities; a private yacht outside charter is outside it, a commercially registered one is inside (MLC 2006, Article II). Within it, everything turns on the prescribed manning — the crew the flag's safe-manning document and the vessel's normal operation put on board — and on the definition of the cook.

Key Concept One — the ship's cook

The ship's cook is the person responsible for the food and catering provided to the crew. Cayman's guidance draws the line that matters on a yacht: “A visiting chef who is on board who only prepares food for guests and other non-crew members is not considered as a ship's cook for the purposes of MLC 2006” (Cayman Islands Shipping Registry, Guidance Note on Recognizing Ship's Cooks, 2018). The flag state then decides what “trained and qualified” means.

The mechanism is a threshold plus a syllabus. The threshold: on ships operating with a prescribed manning of less than ten, a fully qualified cook may not be required (ILO, MLC 2006 Standard A3.2, as restated in the Special Tripartite Committee's Technical Note 12, 2021). The syllabus: practical cookery, food and personal hygiene, food storage, stock control, environmental protection and catering health and safety — the list the flags copy into their own notices (Marshall Islands MI-118; UK MSN 1846 Annexes 1 and 2). The floor for everyone else in the galley is separate and lower: any person processing food must be trained or instructed in food and personal hygiene and safe handling (MSN 1846, para 6.2).

What that means for the chef reading this: the certificate is about the crew's food, and it is triggered by the crew's number.

If the vessel is commercially registered and ordinarily carries ten or more seafarers, someone on board must hold what the flag accepts as a ship's cook qualification — and if that someone is you, the ticket goes on the crew list next to the ENG1.

Key Concept Two — what the flag accepts

Three flags, three answers to the same convention. The UK issues a Ship's Cook Certificate of Competency; Cayman accepts shore qualifications and offers a voluntary recognition statement; the Marshall Islands sets its own requirements on the MLC syllabus. The thresholds differ by a day: the UK exempts ships operating only within 60 miles of a safe haven on UK-only voyages, Cayman applies the ten-seafarer rule to voyages of more than three days or more than 36 hours from a safe port.

  1. UK Ship's Cook Certificate — at least 18 years old; a minimum of one month's sea service in any capacity, proven by discharge-book entries; training to the learning outcomes of MSN 1846 Annexes 1 and 2, or a recognised qualification from the list in MIN 671 with prior learning accredited by IAMI; the STCW basic safety courses; an ENG1; application on form MSF 4395 (MSN 1846 (M) Amendment 1; PYA).
  2. Cayman Islands — a recognised cook's qualification such as City & Guilds, an NVQ Level II in catering and hospitality or a diploma from a shore-based catering school, provided the syllabus meets Standard A3.2 and includes advanced training in food hygiene and preparation, or separate certificates covering it; a seafarer medical; optional Recognition of Qualification as a Ship's Cook from the registry (CISR Guidance Note, 2018).
  3. Everyone else in the galley, any flag — food safety at Level 2 as the minimum, assessed in person: the UK does not accept online certificates (MIN 583/689).

Those are the rules on paper. Here is what they mean the day they bite.

Nobody checks the cook's ticket until the day they check the cook's ticket — and the day is a port-state inspection with guests due aboard.

By Flag

The rule isn't one rule; here is the version that applies to you.

Case One — Red Ensign, commercially registered, ten crew or more

The most common case in the Mediterranean charter fleet: a UK, Cayman or other Red Ensign yacht on a commercial registration with a crew of ten or more. Under the UK notice the ship must carry a certificated ship's cook unless it works only within 60 miles of a safe haven on UK-only voyages, which no charter yacht does.

  • Trigger — the vessel ordinarily operates with ten or more seafarers on board (MSN 1846, para 2.2(b)).
  • Document — a UK Ship's Cook Certificate of Competency, or a flag-recognised equivalent; the certificate is inspectable alongside the Declaration of Maritime Labour Compliance.
  • The gap that voids it — a chef who cooks only for guests while a steward feeds the crew: the steward is then the ship's cook, and needs the qualification.

A 45-metre with nine crew is exempt; add a daywork deckhand for the season and it ordinarily carries ten — the exemption is gone, and the chef who never needed a certificate now does, mid-charter.

The Lighter Comparison Set

Cayman Islands (CISN 07/2014; Guidance Note 2018)

Ten or more seafarers on voyages of more than three days or more than 36 hours from a safe port: a fully qualified cook. Shore qualifications accepted if they carry advanced food hygiene; a voluntary registry recognition statement exists for large and passenger yachts.

Marshall Islands (MI-118)

Requirements taken directly from the MLC syllabus — practical cookery, hygiene, sanitation, nutrition, storage, stock control, environmental protection, catering health and safety — plus a current seafarer medical.

Under ten, or private

No fully qualified cook required, but anyone processing food must be trained or instructed in hygiene and safe handling — the Level 2 card; a private yacht outside commercial activity sits outside the Convention altogether (MLC 2006, Article II).

Those cases send chefs to a short list of places to qualify — the training centres the flags recognise.

Where the Training Lives

United Kingdom

MCA-approved assessment centres for working chefs: Leiths (London) runs a three-day Assessment in Marine Cookery; Tante Marie (Woking) an approved assessment; Entier an MLC ship's cook competency course. Fastest route for a chef already qualified ashore.

Cape Town

The Superyacht Culinary Academy runs a 20-day ship's cook course for chefs coming to the fleet without a shore qualification, in the season the Mediterranean boats are laid up.

The registries

The certificate is issued by the flag, not the school: the UK's Registry of Shipping and Seamen in Cardiff (form MSF 4395), the Cayman Islands Shipping Registry (recognition on request), the Marshall Islands under MI-118.

What the ticket buys is access to the boats above the line — and those are the boats that pay.

The Practical Path

Substance over paperwork: hold the qualification the flag will actually recognise, and hold it before the crew list crosses ten.

The principle is that the certificate follows the flag, not the chef: qualify against the registry your boat — or your next boat — flies, and keep the supporting documents (discharge book, food-safety certificate, medical) in one folder the captain can hand to an inspector.

  1. Read the crew list and the registry. Commercial or private, which flag, and how many seafarers the vessel ordinarily carries — the three facts that decide whether the requirement exists.
  2. Get the Level 2 food-safety qualification first, in person. It is the floor for anyone in the galley on any flag and it feeds into every route; online certificates are not accepted by the UK (MIN 583/689).
  3. Choose the route for your flag. Red Ensign: the UK certificate via an approved assessment plus one month of logged sea service, the STCW basics and an ENG1, then MSF 4395 to Cardiff; Cayman: assemble your shore qualification with its advanced-hygiene component and ask the registry for the recognition statement.
  4. Log the sea service now. One month in any capacity, proven by the discharge book or certificates of discharge — the cheapest item on the list and the one most chefs cannot evidence.
  5. Don't take advice at the dock bar. The rule that applies to the 38-metre alongside you is not the rule that applies to your 52-metre; read the notice for your flag.

This week: count the seafarers your boat ordinarily carries, check the registration type, and if the answer is ten and commercial, book the assessment — three days for a chef already qualified ashore.

The Cost of Getting It Wrong

A missing certificate is a port-state deficiency against the ship; a missing chef is a lost season.

The requirement is the shipowner's, so the first cost lands on the boat: an inspector who finds no qualified cook on a ship that needs one raises a deficiency under MLC Title 3, and the ship does not sail until it is closed. The second cost is the chef's: the 40-metre-plus boats that carry ten crew increasingly write the certificate into the job, and a chef without it is invisible to that band of the market. Against that, the fix is a Level 2 card, a three-day assessment and a month of sea service most working chefs already have.

The worst case is not a fine; it is the boat held alongside with guests due, a chef hired in a hurry with the right ticket, and the one without it flying home mid-season on the wrong side of a reference.

That bill is not paid by the owner; it is paid by whoever was feeding the crew.

The person it lands on is the chef who fed nine people well for two seasons, learned the boat's every fridge, and was replaced in a week because a tenth crew member joined and nobody read the notice. The certificate is three days; the season is five months.

The One Thing

Count the crew, not the metres: at ten seafarers on a commercial registration the flag requires a qualified ship's cook — hold the ticket your flag recognises before the list gets there.
