The American Pancake: Whole Grain, Tall, Browned Like a Diner's

Break the stack with a fork and the edge should give like a sponge, the underside an even mahogany, not blotchy tan, the crumb open enough to take butter without turning to paste. That is a whole-grain pancake, and it is not what most people mean by one. The healthy version usually lands pale, flat and faintly wet in the middle — the pleasure removed, the fibre added, and nobody asks for it twice. Two moves fix it, and neither one is more white flour or more sugar. The first happens in a bowl, hours before the pan. The second takes ninety seconds, right at the end. And because neither one belongs to the pan, the same bowl will go just as happily into a waffle iron — which wants the opposite things from it.

The one idea: whole grain has to drink in the bowl, not in the pan. Bran and oat β-glucan take up water slowly; hand them to a hot griddle still thirsty and they go on swelling while the cake sets around them. That is the dense, gummy, half-raw middle every wholemeal pancake stands accused of. Give them the time first and the accusation goes away.

Where it comes from

European pancakes are thin because nothing was lifting them. The crêpe, the Dutch pannenkoek, the Russian blin — each leans on eggs, on gluten, or on yeast and a long wait. The American pancake stands up for a reason that begins outside the kitchen: a continent being cleared for farmland made wood ash by the shipload, and wood ash leaches into pearlash — potassium carbonate. Amelia Simmons put it into print in American Cookery (Hartford, 1796), the first cookbook written by an American and printed in America, where the alkali meets the acid in sour milk and molasses and makes its gas in the bowl, before the pan ever gets involved.

Everything since is refinement of that one trick. Pearlash gave way to saleratus in the 1840s; Alfred Bird mixed the first single-acting powder in England in 1843; and in 1856 Eben Norton Horsford patented calcium acid phosphate in Rhode Island, pairing the alkali with a dry acid so the cook no longer had to supply the sourness herself. What never changed is that the American griddle cake is an alkali dish — and an alkali quietly does two jobs, not one. Everybody remembers the first. Step five is about the second.

“Wholemeal makes a tough pancake — barely stir it.” The opposite holds. Wheat bran is abrasive: it shears gluten strands as they form, so the network in a whole-wheat batter comes out shorter and weaker than in a white-flour one, and the cake is more tender, not less — America's Test Kitchen stir theirs deliberately smooth for exactly that reason. The heaviness people pin on gluten is not gluten. It is water that arrived late and heat that arrived too fast. — 100 Percent Whole-Wheat Pancakes, Cook's Illustrated / America's Test Kitchen

Which leaves a better question. If the grain is going to sit in acid dairy for hours anyway, what else is that soak doing while it waits?

More than softening bran. Whole grain locks its iron, zinc and magnesium into phytate, a storage molecule the small intestine barely opens — which is how wholemeal can be richer in minerals on the label and poorer in the body. The grain also carries the key: phytase, its own enzyme for cutting phytate apart, and one that only works in acid. Buttermilk runs pH 4.4–4.5 alone; stirred into flour it buffers up into the exact window where wheat phytase works best. Hold a wholemeal dough at pH 5.5 and phytate breakdown climbs from 40% to 70% of the flour's starting content, with the acid as the only variable changed. It is the lever a sourdough pulls, borrowed for breakfast — and like a sourdough it wants warmth and hours, not a cold twenty minutes. — Leenhardt, Levrat-Verny, Chanliaud & Rémésy, J. Agric. The ratio — memorise, rescale anything
Flour 100% (½ oat · ½ white whole wheat) · Buttermilk 110% · Cottage cheese 67% · Egg 50% · Brown butter 15% · Maple 7% · Salt 1.5% · Baking powder 2.5% · Bicarbonate 0.8% — two numbers carry the dish. Total dairy at 177% looks reckless against a diner pancake's 120%, and it is the whole point: whole grain and oat β-glucan hold far more water than refined flour, so the batter has to be built wet enough to still pour after it has finished drinking. And bicarbonate is held down at 0.8% because that is about all the acid in the bowl can spend. Cultured buttermilk must carry at least 0.5% lactic acid to be sold as such, and usually runs nearer 0.8%; add the acid in 400 g of curd and 5 g of soda is comfortably inside the budget at either end of that range. Spending it is the point — turning lactic acid into lactate walks the batter from about pH 4.5 up towards neutral, and step five collects on every tenth of that climb. Dose past what the acid can absorb and the surplus tastes of soap.

For the iron, three numbers move: Brown butter 15% → 35% · Cornflour 0 → 10% · Buttermilk 110% → 135% — richer to fry and release, starch to go glassy, thinner to reach the corners of the grid. Everything else, including the whole soak, is unchanged. — 21 CFR §131.112, Cultured milk (US standard of identity)

Method

0/0 steps done

  1. This is where an American breakfast borrows a European bread baker's patience.

    Soak the grain. Blend the 400 g cottage cheese with the 660 g buttermilk until it is completely smooth — a surviving curd becomes a rubbery fleck in the finished cake, so run the blender longer than feels necessary. Whisk in both flours and the salt. Cover and leave: 30 min at room temperature is the floor, overnight in the fridge is the recipe. It will stiffen to something like wet plaster. That is correct.

    Why
    Two clocks run at once. The fast one is hydration: bran and β-glucan pull water in over tens of minutes, and whatever they have not taken by the time they hit the pan, they take from the crumb while it sets. The slow one is phytase, and it is honest to say the fridge nearly stops it — the 40% → 70% phytate figure comes from a dough held warm at pH 5.5, not from a cold night. A room-temperature soak of 2–4 hours does real work on the minerals; a cold overnight one is mostly about texture, which is reason enough. — Leenhardt et al., J. Agric. Food Chem. 53 (2005) 98–102

  2. The French name for the smell is noisette — hazelnut — and the smell is the entire reason for the step.

    Brown the butter. 100 g unsalted butter in a pale-bottomed pan over medium heat. It foams, the foam falls, and the milk solids on the base turn from cream to hazelnut. Pull it off the moment it smells of nuts, not when it looks right — the colour lags the flavour by about twenty seconds. Scrape every solid in; they carry it. Cool to warm.

    Why
    Butter is around 15% water and 2% milk solids. Boil off the water and the solids — lactose and milk protein together — sit in hot fat and run the Maillard reaction, which is why browned butter tastes of far more than butter. It matters here because the fat budget is lean: 90 g across twelve people is about 7 g a head, and lean fat has to be loud.

  3. From here everything is on a clock.

    Wake the batter. Stir the yolks, the warm brown butter and the maple into the soak. Sift the baking powder and bicarbonate over the top and fold them through — sift, do not tip, or you get alkaline pockets that taste of metal. Loosen with extra buttermilk until a ladleful falls in a thick ribbon and the ribbon sinks back in about three seconds. Cold soaks want more; warm soaks want less.

    Why
    The soda has two employers. Against the buttermilk's lactic acid it makes carbon dioxide immediately — 5 g of bicarbonate is roughly 1.4 litres of gas at room temperature. Spending that acid is also what raises the batter's pH, and the higher pH is what step five collects. The baking powder is the reserve: double-acting, so a second release comes from heat in the pan, which is what keeps the last ladle of a long service as tall as the first.

  4. This is Bill Granger's move, out of a Sydney breakfast room, and it is what makes the whole thing possible.

    Whip and fold. Whites to soft peak — the tip should flop over, not stand up. Fold into the batter in three additions, cutting down and turning over, and stop while a few streaks still show. Streaks cook out. Broken foam does not.

    Why
    Every other route to a tall pancake runs through gluten or through gas trapped by gluten, and this batter is half oat flour with no gluten at all. Whipped whites go around the problem: ovalbumin and ovotransferrin unfold at the air-water interface, turn from helix to sheet and set into a film that holds bubbles on their own. That is lift a whole-grain batter cannot generate chemically. Whipped past soft peak the same proteins over-coagulate, the foam goes grainy, and it shatters when you fold instead of stretching.

  5. And here is the alkali's second job — the one nobody remembers.

    Griddle low and long. 175 °C (350 °F) — lower than you would run a white-flour pancake. Butter the surface and wipe it almost dry. 60 g a cake, spread to about 10 cm. Give it 2½–3 min on the first side and 1½–2 min after the flip. Flip once.

    Why
    The Maillard reaction starts when an amino group attacks a sugar's carbonyl, and it can only attack while it is unprotonated. Acid keeps it protonated; alkali frees it. Browning rate therefore climbs steeply with pH — across glucose–amino acid systems held at 100 °C and titrated from pH 4.0 to 12.0, colour development rises the whole way. That is why a batter carrying 3 g of sugar per serving can still come off the griddle mahogany: the colour is bought with pH, not sugar. It is also why the heat comes down. An alkaline batter browns early, and a thick whole-grain cake needs the extra minute to finish its middle before the face goes dark. — Ajandouz & Puigserver, J. Agric. Food Chem. 47 (1999) 1786–1793. Cook within 25 minutes of folding. A batter with whipped whites in it is a countdown, not a mise en place. Held longer it slumps, and the pancakes come out flat with a dense band along the base. If service is staggered, hold back a third of the soak and fold fresh whites into it later — never re-whip into a slumped batter.

PannenkoekNetherlands — plate-wide and thin, carried to New Amsterdam. Nothing to lift it, so it never learned to stand.

CrumpetEngland — yeast plus soda in a ring mould. The same carbon-dioxide story as this recipe, except the bubbles are trapped standing up instead of spread flat.
Ricotta hotcakeSydney — Bill Granger's, fresh curd plus folded whites. The direct parent of steps one and four.
Modern variations
Buckwheat thirdSwap 100 g of the oat flour for buckwheat. Darker, mineral, closer to a blin; brown 10 °C cooler, it scorches early.
Barley for oatBarley flour carries β-glucan too and EFSA treats the two together. Slightly sweeter, slightly stickier — add 5% more buttermilk.
Ricotta instead of cottage cheeseRicher, softer, about 3 g less protein a serving. No blending needed — ricotta breaks up on its own.
Kefir instead of buttermilkMore acid, so the soda goes further; drop the bicarbonate to 4 g or the batter tips soapy.
SavouryDrop the maple, add spring onion and a little cured chilli. Accept that they come out paler — the sugar was doing browning work.
BlueberriesFrozen, straight from the freezer, dropped onto the wet face after the batter hits the griddle. Stirred into the bowl they bleed and their acid knocks the pH back down.
What it actually buys you

Per serving of three — roughly 15 g protein, 5.5 g fibre and 3 g added sugar, against about 8 g, 1 g and 3 g for the same stack in white flour. The oat half carries around 1 g of β-glucan, which is the per-portion floor EFSA sets for the oat-β-glucan cholesterol claim — but the effect belongs to 3 g a day, so one breakfast is a third of it, not the whole thing. Be sceptical of the exact figure, too: across 95 oat cultivars β-glucan ranged from 2.8% to 6.6% of dry matter, so the honest range for this plate is 0.7–1.6 g, and the sack in your dry store does not say which. The larger claim is the plainer one — this is 50 g of whole grain a head — 100% of the flour as a pancake, about 91% as a waffle, once the cornflour goes in — and whole-grain intake tracks lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality in dose-response meta-analysis, at around 90 g a day for most of the benefit. — Lo, Lampoglou et al., npj Sci. Food 10 (2026) 145. DOI: 10.1038/s41538-026-00800-z · EFSA NDA Panel, EFSA Journal 8(12) (2010) 1885. DOI: 10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1885 · Aune et al., BMJ 353 (2016) i2716. DOI: 10.1136/bmj.i2716

What it does not buy: a licence to say it fills you up longer. High-protein breakfasts do raise reported satiety in trials, but they have repeatedly failed to cut total daily energy intake, and the honest grade on that claim is B at best. Eat it because it is a better breakfast, not because it is a diet.

Troubleshooting
Gummy, damp middleBatter reached the pan still thirsty, or the griddle ran hot. Extend the soak; drop to 165 °C and add a minute.
Soapy or metallicMore bicarbonate than the acid can spend, or it went in unsifted. Drop to 4 g, or add 60 g more buttermilk.
Flat, dense band at the baseWhites whipped to stiff and broken on folding, or the batter sat too long. Soft peak, three additions, cook within 25 min.
Pale and dullGriddle too cool, or the soda under-dosed. Fix the pH before you reach for sugar — sugar past 7% starts tasting like a cake.
Bitter, aggressively bran-yRed wholemeal instead of white whole wheat. Red wheat bran carries the tannins and phenolics that read as bitter; hard white wheat is the same grain without them.
Rubbery flecks through the crumbCottage cheese curd that survived the blender. Blend longer, or push it through a sieve.
Spreading to a crêpeOver-loosened at step 3. The ribbon should sink back in three seconds, not one.

The bubbles that open at the rim stay open — a batter that reseals its own holes is still too wet to turn. The rim goes matte from the outside in, about 5 mm deep. The underside is even mahogany rather than blotched, and the cake springs back slowly when pressed at the centre, without a wet give underneath.

The other machine — the waffle

The soak does not care what cooks it. Everything that took the overnight — the bran swelling, the β-glucan drinking, the phytase working in the acid — is machine-agnostic, which is why one container in the fridge can go two ways at eight in the morning. What is not machine-agnostic is everything after it. A griddle asks a batter to stay soft and absorbent. An iron asks it to dry out and go brittle, from both faces at once, in five minutes. Those are opposite requests, and pouring pancake batter into a waffle iron is how you get a soft, pale, gridded pancake that welds itself to the plates.

Whole grain fights a waffle in a way it does not fight a pancake. Oat β-glucan is a hydrocolloid — its job in the body is to hold water, and it does that job just as well in a crust, where held water is exactly what stops a shell going brittle. So this makes a very good whole-grain waffle: firm, crisp-edged, deeply browned, moist in the middle. It does not make the shatter-crisp yeasted Brussels waffle, and nothing you do to this batter will get it there. That is a different build, and it has its own Blueprint. Choose by which one you actually want.

Within that limit, three additions at the wake stage close most of the gap. None of them touches the soak.

Extra buttermilk 150 g
beyond the usual loosening		A waffle batter has to flow, into the corners of the grid and up against the top plate. A batter that ribbons and holds — correct for a pancake — sits in the middle of the iron and cooks as a thick disc with a pattern pressed on it.
Longer, not hotter
iron on its highest setting, 5–7 min		The iron's real job is drying, and this batter is carrying more water than a white-flour one. Turning the heat up only darkens the outside before the inside has given up its moisture. More time on the same high setting is the fix — the shell cannot brown until it has dried.

Which leaves the part nobody writes down: the two things are not interchangeable at the table either, and the difference is more useful than it looks.

A pancake is a sponge and a waffle is a plate. The pancake is soft, open and absorbent by design — it drinks the butter and the syrup, which is why it is stacked, and why it has to be eaten within minutes of leaving the pan. The waffle's grid does the opposite: the ridges dry hard and the pockets hold liquid on top rather than drawing it in, so it carries wet things a pancake would dissolve under — a poached egg and its yolk, creamed mushrooms, something cold and melting. Same batter, opposite relationship with anything wet.

On a boat that distinction decides the service, not the menu.

PancakesA live dish. Someone stands at the griddle while people eat, and they are past their best in ten minutes. Right for crew breakfast, where the galley is already occupied and everybody arrives at once.
WafflesThe only one of the two that survives being made in advance. Cook the batch cold, freeze flat, and toast to order straight from frozen — they come back crisper than they were. Right for charter service, where guests come up one at a time across two hours and nobody can be standing at a griddle for all of it.

Different senses from the pancake, because you cannot see it. The steam is the gauge: a hard plume at first, then a thin wisp — while it is still steaming hard the crust is wet and capped at 100 °C, and it cannot brown until it has dried. The lid should lift with no resistance and the waffle release itself; if it grips, it is not finished, and opening early is what tears it. Deep chestnut, and it sounds hollow tapped on the rack. Then: wire rack, single layer, uncovered — stacked or covered, its own trapped steam turns it to leather in about a minute.

Provisioning at sea
White whole wheat flourHard white wheat, not red. Nutritionally the two are near-identical, but red bran carries the tannins and phenolic acids that make wholemeal taste bitter — white wheat lacks them, and it is the single provisioning decision that decides whether guests notice this is a health recipe. If only red wholemeal is aboard, cut it to a third and raise the oat flour.
Cottage cheese4% fat, plain, no gums or stabilisers on the label — stabilised versions blend gluey. Skyr or thick yogurt substitutes at 1:1 but adds acid, so drop the bicarbonate to 4 g.

The soak holds 48 hours covered in the fridge and improves for the first 24. The batter, once leavened and folded, holds nothing — that is the whole reason the recipe is split in two. Cooked pancakes hold 40 minutes on a rack at 70 °C, single layer and uncovered; cover them and the trapped steam turns the crust to flannel inside five minutes.

Then the two part company. Pancakes are a same-morning dish: they freeze acceptably, interleaved with paper, and come back in 4 minutes at 180 °C, but they are never as good as they were. Waffles genuinely improve on the round trip — the freezer drives more water out of the shell, and a toaster from frozen returns them crisper than the iron did. So for a charter breakfast spread across two hours, cook waffles the day before and freeze them flat, and keep the pancakes for the meal where everyone sits down at once.

Once you own the ratio — flour 100, dairy 177, egg 50, soda 0.8 — the dish is yours: swap a third of the flour for buckwheat or barley, run it savoury by dropping the maple, or send half of it to the iron with the three numbers changed. Cook once, serve two tiers: the same soak feeds both ends of the boat — guests get waffles, made yesterday and toasted to order as they surface, under a poached egg or roasted stone fruit and thick yogurt; crew get pancakes off the griddle at 120 g, spread wide and folded over bacon with a scrape of maple, two to a plate and eaten standing up. One soak, two machines, and nobody waiting on the same pan. And the alkali that Amelia Simmons leached out of a woodpile in 1796 is still doing both of its jobs, whichever machine you put it in.

Which leaves only the part that has nothing to do with chemistry: what a stack of pancakes is actually for.

Nobody eats pancakes alone, and nobody eats them in a hurry. They come to the table in a pile, in the middle, and are dealt out — which is why they belong to Sunday and to the morning after a long passage, when the crew are slow and there is finally nothing to do at nine o'clock. Put the yogurt and the fruit in the middle too, and let the syrup be somebody's decision rather than yours. The stack is the point: it says there is more than enough, and that nobody has to be anywhere.
