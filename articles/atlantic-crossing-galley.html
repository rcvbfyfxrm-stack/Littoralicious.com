The Atlantic Crossing: 2,700 Miles, Twenty Crew, One Freezer Plan

Twenty crew, 2,700 miles, three weeks without a shop and a galley that pitches: the crossing is cooked at the dock — the freezer by week, the one-pot rough-weather meals, the bake that lifts morale.

Cook the crossing at the dock, in thirty-millimetre slabs, and stow the boat in eating order — fridge for week one, freezer for week two, dry store for week three. At sea you are a line cook pulling from your own past self.

The Problem

1,260 covers, 2,700 miles, and no shop after the first morning.

Las Palmas to Rodney Bay is 2,700 nautical miles, and the World Cruising Club puts the average yacht on that track at 18 to 21 days. Plan on 21. Twenty crew, three meals: 1,260 covers, not one of them buyable once the lines are off, with somebody awake and hungry at every hour of the twenty-four. That is the design parameter, not a complaint.

The obvious approach — cook at sea, as you would in any galley — fails on day four, when the wind comes round and the person with their head over a bucket is the chef. And count the litres before the covers: a wet braise packs at near enough a kilo to the litre, so a dinner for twenty at 250 g a head is five litres of slab, and fourteen frozen dinners about seventy. The freezer's litres, not the menu, decide how many nights it carries.

The Fix

Two flat days at the dock do the work of three rolling weeks.

Stow in eating order — fridge for week one, freezer for week two, dry store for week three — plus a locker and a shelf that never depend on the weather.

  1. Brown the mince plain, by the kilo, and direct it three ways later: ragù, chilli, cottage pie.
  2. Braises, curries, soups and purées go down flat, 30 mm thick — trays in the fridge to 21 °C inside two hours, then the freezer for the rest of the six. Probe the centre of one slab; the number goes in the log.
  3. Lasagne and baked pasta freeze in the service dish, foil-lidded: frozen to oven, no handling on a heeling boat.
  4. Portion raw proteins by dinner. The breakfast meats are the shortest lines on the FDA chart — bacon one month, raw sausage one to two — so buy them last, freeze them flat, eat them first.

Week one, eat the fridge: salads, herbs, soft fruit and the last chilled proteins, in the order they die — which happens to fall on the days the crew is least able to eat. The largest survey run at sea, 20,029 passengers across 114 ferry voyages, found 7% vomited and 29% rated themselves unwell (Lawther & Griffin, 1988; questionnaire survey, ferries rather than ocean passages · Grade B). The countermeasure a galley can plan around is habituation: susceptibility falls with continued exposure (Koch et al., 2018). So week one is simple food, small portions, and the locker's one-pot meals for anyone who cannot face a fork. Week two, draw the freezer down: the evening before, tomorrow's dinner moves to the bottom shelf of the fridge. A 30 mm slab thaws in a night; the brick you did not flatten does not.

Two probe readings, one freezer opening, tomorrow's thaw moved down. Open the freezer once a day and take everything you need in that opening — the cold you spill is the cold you cannot buy back at 2,700 miles from a shop.

Why It Works

One clock governs the whole plan, and it is not the freezer's.

Cooked food must fall from 57 °C to 21 °C within two hours and to 5 °C within six hours in total (FDA Food Code 2022, §3-501.14); the Code's annex calls 21–52 °C the ideal incubation band and names slow cooling a leading contributor to foodborne illness. Its own remedies are shallow pans and thinner portions (§3-501.15) — which is the entire argument for the 30 mm slab over the ten-kilo tub. Once down, at −18 °C the food is safe indefinitely and only quality ages: the FDA's chart gives soups, stews and cooked meat dishes 2–3 months, frozen casseroles 3–4, chops 4–6, steaks 6–12. On the way back up, 74 °C for 15 seconds before anything reheated reaches the pass (§3-403.11).

The eggs are the one place the plan runs against the instinct to chill everything, and the mechanism is worth knowing. Buy them unchilled: a European Class A egg is never chilled below 5 °C anywhere in the chain, and its best-before is fixed at 28 days from laying (Regulation 589/2008, Articles 2 and 13), so an egg laid the week you leave Las Palmas is in date at Rodney Bay. A cold egg brought back into warm air sweats, and condensation on the shell feeds bacteria and helps them through it (recital 7). Once chilled, keep chilled; otherwise cool, dark and dry.

What It Costs

Two days of your life at the dock, and the failure that hides itself.

The cost is the last 48 hours alongside, spent cooking rather than resting, on the two days everyone else is stowing sails. What it buys is a chef who is still standing on day four. The common failure is the opposite of laziness: the chef who cooks from the dry store on day three to save the fresh, and composts a fridge of herbs on day nine.

The one that hides itself: do not put a deep tub of hot braise into the freezer to cool it faster. The surface freezes while the centre lags through the incubation band, and the crust hides it — you will not know, and neither will the probe unless you push it to the middle. Shallow trays, thin slabs, and read the centre.

Tested at sea.
