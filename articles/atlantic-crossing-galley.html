The Atlantic Crossing: 2,700 Miles, Twenty Crew, One Freezer Plan

The Problem: 1,260 Covers and No Shop for 2,700 Miles

Twenty crew, twenty-one days, three meals: the passage is bought, cooked and stowed before the first line comes off the cleat.

Las Palmas de Gran Canaria to Rodney Bay, Saint Lucia, is 2,700 nautical miles; the World Cruising Club, which sends the ARC fleet down that track every November, puts the average yacht at 18 to 21 days. Plan on 21. Twenty crew, three meals, 21 days: 1,260 covers, not one of them buyable after the first morning. Someone is awake and hungry at every hour of the 24 — four on, eight off. That is the design parameter. The complaint arrives on day four, when the wind comes round and the person with their head over a bucket is the chef.

The rule: an Atlantic passage is not cooked at sea. It is cooked at the dock, while the boat sits flat, and eaten slowly over 2,700 miles. At sea you are a line cook pulling from your own past self: pull, thaw, heat to 74 °C, serve.

Count the litres before the covers: a wet braise packs at near enough a kilo to the litre, so a dinner for twenty at 250 g a head is five litres of slab, and fourteen frozen dinners about 70 litres. The freezer's litres, not the menu, decide how many nights it carries and how many the dry store must.

The System: Cook It at the Dock — with the Station Map

Six zones, one clock: cook flat, cool by the FDA's numbers, freeze in slabs, draw down week by week.

The last 48 hours at the dock are the kitchen; the three weeks at sea are the service. Stow the boat in eating order — fridge for week one, freezer for week two, dry store for week three — plus a locker and a shelf that never depend on the weather. Here is the map.

The row that carries the crossing is the freezer, and the rule the rest of this piece defends is nothing goes in warm — because the clock that governs the dock day is the cooling clock, not the freezer's.

The number that governs the plan: cooked food must fall from 57 °C to 21 °C within two hours and to 5 °C within six hours in total (FDA Food Code 2022, §3-501.14); the Code's annex calls 21–52 °C the ideal incubation band and slow cooling a leading contributor to foodborne illness. Its own methods are shallow pans and thinner portions (§3-501.15): a 30 mm slab on a tray, never a ten-kilo tub. At −18 °C the food is safe indefinitely and only quality ages — the FDA's storage chart gives soups, stews and cooked meat dishes 2–3 months, frozen casseroles 3–4, chops 4–6, steaks 6–12. On the way back up: 74 °C (165 °F) for 15 seconds before anything cooked, cooled and reheated goes into the pass (§3-403.11).

The Last 48 Hours: The Dock Is the Kitchen

Two flat days do the work of three rolling weeks.

The delivery has its own protocol — probe at the tailgate, refuse on paper, the map above the fridges in Provisioning Day — and it lands two days out, into a freezer already reading −18 °C. Then the cook.

  • Brown the mince plain, by the kilo; direct it three ways later — ragù, chilli, cottage pie.
  • Braises, curries, soups and purées go down in bags, flat, 30 mm thick: trays in the fridge to 21 °C inside two hours, then the freezer for the rest of the six. Probe the centre of one slab; the number goes in the log.
  • Lasagne and baked pasta freeze in the service dish, foil-lidded: frozen to oven, no handling on a heeling boat.
  • Raw proteins portion by dinner. The breakfast meats are the shortest lines on the FDA chart — bacon one month, raw sausage one to two — so buy them last, freeze them flat, eat them first.
Do not put a deep tub of hot braise in the freezer to cool it faster: the surface freezes while the centre lags through the incubation band, and the crust hides it. Shallow trays, thin slabs, the probe.

Three Weeks, Three Stores

Stowed in eating order; the menu follows the thermometer, not the other way round.

The failure is the chef who cooks from the dry store on day three to save the fresh, and composts a fridge of herbs on day nine.

“Do the heavy lifting when the seas are calm, so at sea you only pull, heat, season and serve.” — one chef, passage-prep thread, anonymised

Week one: eat the fridge

Salads, herbs, soft fruit, the fish and the last chilled proteins, in the order they die — on the days the crew is least able to eat. The largest survey run at sea, 20,029 passengers on 114 ferry voyages, found 7% vomited and 29% rated themselves unwell (Lawther and Griffin, 1988). The countermeasure a galley can plan around is habituation: susceptibility falls with continued exposure (Koch and colleagues, 2018). So week one is simple food, small portions, and the locker's one-pot meals for anyone who cannot face a fork.

Eggs are the exception to the fridge. Buy them unchilled: a European Class A egg is never chilled below 5 °C in the chain, and its best-before is fixed at 28 days from laying (Regulation 589/2008, Articles 2 and 13); an egg laid the week you leave Las Palmas is inside date at Rodney Bay. The reason is a mechanism a chef can use: a cold egg brought back into warm air sweats, and condensation on the shell feeds bacteria and helps them in (recital 7). Once chilled, keep chilled; otherwise cool, dark, dry. Breakfast for twenty without a pan at a moving stove is the two-temperature method in Two Eggs, Two Temperatures, run on a calm morning.

Week two: draw the freezer down

The evening before, tomorrow's dinner goes from the freezer to the bottom shelf of the fridge: thawing under refrigeration at 5 °C or less is the Code's first method, running water at 21 °C or below the second; the counter overnight is not on the list (§3-501.13). A 30 mm slab thaws in a night; the brick you did not flatten does not. Reheat to 74 °C, season, serve from the pot.

Week three: cook from the dry store

Rice, pasta, pulses, tinned tomatoes and fish, the confit, the roots and squash that kept in the dark, UHT and powdered milk. This is the week the starter earns its passage — a loaf every other day from the same sack of flour, with the timings for a moving galley in Sourdough at Sea. Week three is not the poor week if it was designed in Las Palmas.

Rough Weather: One Pot, One Hand

No knife, no second hand, no plate — and decided at the dock.

Nobody is browning mince at a heeling stove on day four; someone — not necessarily you — pulls a labelled slab from the locker and puts it in the oven. That is why the locker is one reach from the stove and its list is taped to the door.

  • Soups and braises from slab to pot: one pan, one hand on the rail, served in a mug or a deep bowl, never a plate.
  • Baked pasta in its dish: frozen to oven, the fiddles up, nobody at the stove until it is done.
  • The salt-and-crunch tin: crackers, pretzels, salted nuts, crisps. One chef's verdict from a hard leg, anonymised: “dry, salty snacks saved most of the crew on their worst days.” Testimony, not a trial — and the cheapest thing aboard.
Stock it before departure and list it on the door. Six one-pot dinners for twenty, the snack tins, instant stock, tea and cocoa. Two hands, a knife at a moving board or a plate that slides: the wrong meal for the conditions.

The Bake: Morale, Timed

Mid-Atlantic, the same horizon for the fifth day, a tray of warm biscuits does more than any plated dinner — so it is scheduled, and its flour is untouchable.

Morale is quieter than seasickness and just as load-bearing. Every grandmother knew it and never called it a technique: the smell of baking reaches the bunks before the plate does, and it says somebody is looking after the boat. One chef put it plainly — baking matters most “when morale gets low mid-Atlantic.” The timing is the 1500 watch change, so the off-going watch eats it warm before they sleep and the on-coming watch takes the rest up with the flask; the trigger is the log, not the calendar. A cake on day twelve costs 400 g of flour and an hour of a flat afternoon.

The Daily Non-Negotiable: Ten Minutes

Two readings, one freezer opening, tomorrow's thaw and the night shelf: the discipline that turns three weeks of cold into three weeks of food.

  1. 0700 — read every cold unit and write it down. Probe on the warmest shelf, not the door display: fridges at 5 °C or less, the freezer at −18 °C or lower. The Maritime Labour Convention requires frequent documented inspections of food, stores and galley (Standard A3.2, paragraph 7); your log is that inspection.
  2. 0705 — the freezer opens once. Today's parcels and tomorrow's thaw out in a single lift, lid down. Opened once a day it holds its cold; opened by every watch it is a fridge with ambitions.
  3. 1900 — tomorrow's dinner to the fridge's bottom shelf, the flask filled, the watch shelf restocked. Before the first night watch goes up.
  4. Your own rest is on the same sheet. The Convention gives every seafarer, the cook included, ten hours' rest in any 24, in no more than two blocks, one at least six hours (Standard A2.3). A 0600-to-2100 galley day with no designed afternoon block is not stamina; it is a breach — and the slab system exists so someone else can heat dinner while you take it.
0700 readings written, freezer opened once, 1900 thaw-and-flask. Ten minutes a day for 21 days. The reading you skipped is the one the freezer failed on.

Proof It Scales: The Law's Own Test, and Your Tally

The same map at four crew is 252 covers; at twenty it is 1,260. The rules do not change, and the count is yours to keep.

The design parameters at the top are the compliance test, not a preference: the Convention's food standard is written having regard to the number of seafarers on board and the duration and nature of the voyage (Standard A3.2, paragraph 2), so 20 crew × 21 days × 3 meals is the calculation the inspection exists to check. The ARC's average yacht runs the same 2,700 miles in 18 to 21 days; the zones, the clock and the −18 °C are identical. What changes is the litres.

This article has no crossing count of its own, and it will not invent one. Keep yours: crossings, days at sea, covers served, 0700 freezer readings ever above −18 °C, dinners abandoned to weather. Fill it for a season. If you send it in, the Weekly Brief prints what comes back — counts and conditions, boat unnamed.

Setup Checklist

Most of it two days out, the last of it the night before.

  • The freezer measured in litres and reading −18 °C before the first slab; the dinners it can carry written on its lid.
  • A probe thermometer checked in iced water (it reads 0 °C), a marker, bags and flat trays for the whole cook.
  • The delivery landed two days out and received by the Provisioning Day map; the last market lunch eaten flat — The One-Hour Lunch is the dock-day meal.
  • Every parcel marked and stowed by week, week two on top; every dry-store case labelled with its stow position.
  • The locker stocked and listed on its door; the bake reserve sealed; the watch shelf laid out; the log ruled for 21 days; the crew told which slab is theirs to heat when the chef is the one over the bucket.
At the Table

Somewhere past day fifteen, if the trade wind has held, the last lasagne comes out of its dish in the cockpit with the sun going down behind the boat, and nobody at that table is thinking about the cooling clock. That is the point. The owner who announced “I'll just eat crew food” for the passage — one chef's line, anonymised — lasted two days on the idea.

The One Thing
Nothing goes in warm, and nothing is cooked at sea that could have been cooked at the dock. Cool on the clock, freeze in slabs, stow in eating order; three weeks of ocean become pull, thaw, heat, serve — even on the day the chef is the one over the bucket.

An Atlantic crossing is not a feat of cooking under fire. It is a feat of planning under calm, eaten slowly over 2,700 miles.
