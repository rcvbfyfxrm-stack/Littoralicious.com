Modernist Mise en Place at Sea: What Holds, for How Long, and What Stays Ashore

Charge the siphon and seal the pouches the day before, set the gels with agar rather than gelatine, make the spheres reverse and hold them in oil, and leave the nitrogen on the dock. One table of hold times, the temperature behind each one, and the arithmetic that keeps a dewar off the boat.

The Problem

The new techniques are taught as one block; a galley can carry four of them, each for a different number of hours.

Foams, gels, spheres, sous-vide pouches and liquid nitrogen are sold as one toolbox, and in a lab with a blast chiller and a thirty-litre dewar they are one. At sea the question is never whether you can make them. It is what you can make in the quiet afternoon before guests board, how long it holds, and at what temperature.

A pasteurised pouch in a galley fridge at 5 °C is a ten-day product; the same pouch held below 3.3 °C keeps for thirty-one days. A gelatine foam slumps on a warm plate; an agar one rides a 70 °C pass. What holds is decided by a temperature, not by talent.

The obvious fix is to make everything on the day. On a charter there is no such day: lunch runs into tea and the crew eat at four. The techniques that earn a locker are the ones that hold.

The Fix

Sort the five families by what holds, make them in that order, and write the clock on each one.

Run this once on the dossier or the book you came home with, and again at the start of every season.

  1. Pouches first, the day before. Cook, chill in iced water to below 5 °C, hold on the coldest shelf you own, write the day on the bag. At 5 °C the clock is ten days; at 3.3 °C it is thirty-one; frozen, it stops.
  2. Charge the siphons cold. Fat, gelatine, xanthan and fluid-gel bases keep in the sealed, pressurised siphon at 5 °C or below, date-marked seven days from preparation with the day of making counted as day one. A hot foam is one service: an egg-white base in a bain-marie no hotter than 61 °C, a starch or agar base to the ceiling printed in the siphon's manual, and never cooled and reheated.
  3. Set the gels with agar, kappa or gellan, not gelatine. Agar sets between 32 and 45 °C and does not melt until 85 °C, so a sheet cut in the morning survives the sun deck. Gelatine melts at body heat: fridge to table, nothing in between.
  4. Make the spheres reverse, and hold them under oil. With the alginate in the bath and the calcium in the liquid, gelling stops at the skin the moment the sphere leaves the bath, and a tray of them waits under oil for the service. Basic spheres keep gelling through and are made in the minute they are served. Frozen in a hemisphere mould, a reverse portion waits weeks for the night it is wanted.

The whole fix fits on one card above the fridge.

Why It Works

Three kinds of hold: a gel with a fifty-degree gap, a reaction you can stop, and a cold chain with a legal floor.

Agar sets when its chains pair into double helices and the helices bundle; the bundles only come apart again at 85 to 95 °C, so the gel forms between 32 and 45 °C and melts fifty degrees higher. That gap is hysteresis. Alginate sets another way: calcium ions lock neighbouring chains into the egg-box junction, for as long as calcium can diffuse in. In a measured test the skin hardened for about 100 seconds in calcium chloride and about 2,000 in calcium gluconate before it plateaued. Put the alginate in the bath, and nothing inside the sphere can gel.

The pouch holds for a legal reason. Non-proteolytic Clostridium botulinum makes toxin from 3.3 °C; the FDA keeps vacuum-packed fish below that line when cold is the only barrier, and a 5 °C fridge buys ten days, not the three weeks a cook was taught.

The 61 °C ceiling on an egg-white foam is not folklore. Conalbumin, the first egg-white protein to set, denatures at 65 °C in the calorimeter; the four degrees are the margin.

The grandmother's rule was the cold room and the covered terrine. The pouch is the same rule with a thermometer and a date.

What It Costs

A siphon and a sealer, a thermometer you trust, and the honesty to leave one technique ashore.

The kit is modest: a half-litre siphon with a metal head, a sealer and a circulator, agar and alginate in the dry store, a probe thermometer. The discipline is not. Every hold time above assumes the fridge is really at the number on its dial, measured on the shelf and not on the door, and a date on every bag and siphon. No counted trial at sea backs this table yet; the numbers are the regulators' and the physics, and the first season that counts them reports back in the Weekly Brief.

Liquid nitrogen fails on arithmetic, not on skill. One litre of the liquid becomes about 0.69 cubic metres of gas at galley temperature, and a four-square-metre galley with 2.1 m of headroom holds about 8.4 cubic metres of air. Vaporise that one litre with the door shut and nothing leaving, and the oxygen share falls from 20.9 to 19.3 per cent, under the 19.5 per cent that OSHA and the gas industry define as oxygen-deficient. A dewar vents whether you cook or not. On a boat, the dewar stays on the dock.

Tested at sea is owed, and the first counted season will print it.
