Chamber or Edge: The Vacuum Sealer Verdict for a Galley

Two Anova machines stand in for the two kinds of vacuum sealer; the physics that decides liquids, the FDA line that decides raw fish, and a counted study that says what a vacuum actually buys — with the verdict a galley can act on.

Two Anova machines — the Precision Chamber Vacuum Sealer at $374 and the Precision Vacuum Sealer Pro at $155 — stand in here for the two kinds of vacuum sealer a chef can buy, and the question this review settles is whether the extra $219 and the extra bench space buy anything a galley needs.

The Job It Has to Do

Judge the kit against the task, not the brochure — here's the task.

A charter week's fish and meat arrive on one day and are eaten across seven; the galley has one fridge that holds 5 °C on a good day, a freezer at −18 °C, and a bench the width of a chopping board. Without a sealer the chef wraps in film and loses the fish first: oxidation and drip in the fridge, freezer burn in the freezer. The tool competes with cling film, with the freezer bag, and with the other kind of vacuum sealer.

The job: put a portion of protein — or a litre of stock — into a bag with almost no air in it, sealed so that it survives a fridge shelf, a freezer and a water bath, on a bench that moves.

The number that decides the purchase is the one only the chamber publishes: a final pressure. Anova gives the chamber −0.95 bar and a 70 L/min pump; for the edge machine it gives an extraction rate and no pressure at all, which is the brochure telling you, politely, that the two are not the same tool. Neither sheet gives a weight.

Quick check: 280 W is about 1.2 A at 230 V, 2.3 A at 120 V, for the seconds a cycle runs — inside any galley's power budget. The chamber's constraint is the 22.5 cm of height with the lid up and the 30 cm seal bar, not the amps.

What They Claim

The makers' promises, and the physics that would have to hold for them to be true.

Anova's chamber page says the machine seals “even liquids” and keeps them “in the bag, not on your counter”; its edge-sealer page promises an end to “sucking up excess liquid into the vacuum port” by way of a moist setting. Henkelman, whose Boxer machines are the professional version of the same idea, says a chamber machine “removes up to 99.8% of the air from the package before sealing”. Whether liquids stay in the bag is not a matter of settings. It is a matter of where the pressure difference sits.

The mechanism: an edge sealer pumps air out through the bag's open mouth, so the pressure inside the bag falls below the room and the room pushes the contents — liquid first — toward the port. In a chamber the bag lies open inside the box and the whole box is evacuated, so bag and surroundings fall together and nothing is pushed anywhere; the bag is sealed and the air let back in only then, and it is the returning air that squeezes the bag tight. The limit is the liquid's own vapour pressure: water at 20 °C boils below 23 mbar absolute, at 37 °C below 63 mbar (NIST Chemistry WebBook, Antoine parameters). Anova's −0.95 bar is about 63 mbar absolute at sea level: a room-temperature marinade sits still, a warm stock boils. Henkelman's 99.8% is about 2 mbar, where anything wetter than a cold cheese boils — which is why the professional machines sell a Liquid Control option that stops the pump when the bag starts to foam.

The proof

How It Has Been Tested

Counted trials, by other people, in a published study — and the honest limit of what that proves.

We have not run a counted trial of these two machines and this review will not pretend to. The counted test exists elsewhere: in 2026 a Polish team packed carp fish balls with a Multivac C200 tabletop chamber machine and, for comparison, in air, and held both at +4 °C for fourteen days — 28 sets, two packaging methods by seven sampling days by two replicates, sixteen samples a set (Kaliniak-Dziura et al., Molecules, 2026).

Twelve days against nine: three days of shelf life bought by taking the air out, on a product that was already a processed one.

What Worked

  • Sensory life at 4 °C ran to 12 days under vacuum against 9 days in air, and fat oxidation markers (peroxide value, TBARS) were lower in the vacuum-packed samples at days 7, 9, 12 and 14 — the chamber's job, done, with the numbers attached.
  • Both sets stayed inside the microbiological limits the authors used (a total viable count below 6 log CFU/g) for the whole fourteen days: the vacuum bought quality, not just safety.

Where It Struggled

  • The study is fish balls, cooked and formed, not raw fillets, and it used a chamber machine; it says nothing about an edge sealer, and it does not measure what a vacuum does to raw fish held at 5 °C for a week, which is the galley case.
  • The galley case is the FDA's: Clostridium botulinum type E grows and makes toxin from 3.3 °C, and for vacuum-packed refrigerated raw fish where refrigeration is the only barrier the guide says the temperature should be kept below 38 °F (3.3 °C) from packing to consumption. A galley fridge at 5 °C is inside the growth range, and a vacuum bag is the anaerobic package the organism wants.

Watch for: the bag of raw fish that goes into a 5 °C fridge for a week. Vacuum-packed raw fish is held below 3.3 °C or frozen — on ice in the fish drawer, or in the freezer — and the bag is opened before it goes back into ordinary refrigeration. That is the FDA's line for processors, and there is no reason a galley's fridge is safer than theirs.

The Verdict

The unambiguous call — buy, skip, or buy-only-if — and the chef it's right for.

Buy if

Buy the chamber machine if the galley portions fish and meat for multi-day charters or cooks sous-vide, and if it can hold the bags below 3.3 °C or freeze them; skip the edge sealer for anything wet, and skip both if the plan was to keep bagged raw fish in a 5 °C fridge.

Verdict: the chamber, for the chef who seals stock, marinades and portions every week and has 34 × 29 cm of bench and a freezer to put the fish in. The edge sealer is a dry-goods and freezer-bag tool at a third of the price, and it is honest about that in its own specifications.

Alternatives & When Not To

When the cheaper, smaller, or simpler kit quietly wins.

The trade-off is bench against pressure: the edge machine is a 40 × 18 cm bar you can stow in a drawer; the chamber is a box with a lid; the professional chamber is a piece of furniture.

  • Cheaper option: the edge sealer at $155 (Anova's, $114 on sale in August 2026), 110 W, 12 L/min. You lose liquids and a published final pressure; that loss is acceptable for rice, flour, coffee, dry-cured meats and anything going straight to the freezer.
  • Smaller-footprint option: the same edge sealer, which fits a drawer the chamber never will. When stowage matters more than stock, it wins.
  • The professional step up: Henkelman's Boxer 42 — 53 × 49 × 44 cm, 64 kg, a 21 m³/h pump, 0.8–1.0 kW, a 410 mm seal bar, 99.8% air removal — is the machine for a crew galley that seals all day and has a locker built for it. It is dealer-priced, and it weighs more than the chef.
  • Skip it entirely if: the fridge holds 5 °C, the freezer is full, and the fish is eaten within two days of landing. Ice in a drawer and film do that job, and the FDA's line does not care what bag the fish is in.

Bottom line: seal wet things in a chamber, dry things in whatever you have, and never keep a vacuum bag of raw fish above 3.3 °C. Tested at sea — the rule, not these two machines.

Whatever you decide at the till, leave with this:

The one thing: the bag is not the preservative; the cold is. A vacuum buys three days of quality on a product held at 4 °C in a counted study, and it buys nothing, and risks something, on raw fish held above 3.3 °C.
