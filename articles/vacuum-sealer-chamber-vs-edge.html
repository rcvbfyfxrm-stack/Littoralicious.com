Chamber or Edge: The Vacuum Sealer Verdict for a Galley

Two Anova machines stand in for the two kinds of vacuum sealer; the physics that decides liquids, the FDA line that decides raw fish, and a counted study that says what a vacuum actually buys — with the verdict a galley can act on.

The bag is not the preservative, the cold is. Vacuum what is already at 4 °C, never bag raw fish held warmer than 3.3, and chill anything wet before it goes in the chamber.

What you need, and why:

  • Above 60 m — a full chamber machine on the bench: Henkelman Boxer 42 or Sammic, because only a chamber holds bag and room at the same pressure, which is what keeps stock in the bag instead of in the pump.
  • Under 60 m — a compact chamber that hides in a cupboard: Anova Precision Chamber, $374, 34.5 × 28.5 cm, because it is a real chamber at −0.95 bar and it stows.
  • Either boat — a proper circulator, not a sealer accessory: Breville | PolyScience HydroPro Plus or Sammic SmartVide, because a bag is only worth sealing if what you do with it afterwards holds a temperature.
  • Skip: the edge sealer as your only machine. It is a dry-goods tool and its own spec sheet says so by publishing no final pressure.
Buy if

Buy the chamber your bench can actually carry, and buy the circulator at the same time — the two are one purchase, and a galley that seals without holding temperature has bought half a system.

Why It Works

Whether liquids stay in the bag is not a setting. It is where the pressure difference sits.

The mechanism: an edge sealer pumps air out through the bag's open mouth, so pressure inside falls below the room and the room pushes the contents — liquid first — toward the port. In a chamber the bag lies open inside the box and the whole box is evacuated, so bag and surroundings fall together and nothing is pushed anywhere; the bag is sealed first and the air let back in only then, and it is the returning air that squeezes it tight.

The real ceiling is your own food's vapour pressure. Water at 20 °C boils below 23 mbar absolute, at 37 °C below 63 mbar (NIST Chemistry WebBook, Antoine parameters). The compact chamber's −0.95 bar is about 63 mbar at sea level: a room-temperature marinade sits still, a warm stock boils. A professional machine at 99.8% is near 2 mbar, where anything wetter than a cold cheese boils — which is exactly why those makers sell a liquid-control option that stops the pump when the bag begins to foam, and why the deeper machine is not automatically the better one. Chill what you are about to seal and the ceiling stops mattering on either.

What the Test Actually Shows

One published counted trial — and the honest limit of what it proves.

We have not run a counted trial of any machine here and will not pretend to. The counted test exists elsewhere: a Polish team packed carp fish balls with a Multivac C200 chamber machine and, for comparison, in air, holding both at +4 °C for fourteen days — 28 sets, two packaging methods across seven sampling days, in duplicate (Kaliniak-Dziura et al., Molecules, 2026).

Twelve days against nine: three days of shelf life bought by taking the air out.

Sensory life at 4 °C ran to 12 days under vacuum against 9 in air, with peroxide value and TBARS lower in the vacuum-packed samples at days 7, 9, 12 and 14. Both sets stayed under the authors' microbiological limit of 6 log CFU/g for the whole fourteen days — so the vacuum bought quality, not safety. What it does not cover is the galley case: those were cooked, formed fish balls, not raw fillets, and the study says nothing about raw fish held at 5 °C for a week.

Watch for: the temperature, not the bag. Clostridium botulinum type E grows and makes toxin from 3.3 °C, and where refrigeration is the only barrier the FDA keeps vacuum-packed refrigerated raw fish below that from packing to consumption (Fish and Fishery Products Hazards and Controls, June 2022, Ch. 13 and Table A-1). A galley fridge at 5 °C is inside the growth range, and a vacuum bag is precisely the anaerobic package the organism wants. The sealer makes a good fridge better and a warm fridge worse.

The Field, by Boat Size

The right machine changes with the bench you have — and twice here, two makers are level.

Above 60 mHenkelman Boxer 42Dealer-priced · 53 × 49 × 44 cm, 64 kg

What you give up: the locker, and then some — 0.8–1.0 kW and a machine that weighs more than the chef. It needs a built place, not a corner.

Best for: a galley that seals all day. A 21 m³/h pump, a 410 mm seal bar, 99.8% air removal, and CombiVAC programmes for marinating, tenderising and soft-air sealing sensitive product.

The detail that matters at sea is maintenance: rounded chamber corners and removable seal bars, so the machine can actually be cleaned by the person who used it rather than by a technician in Palma.

Above 60 m — level with itSammic chamber machinesDealer-priced · sensor-controlled

What you give up: Henkelman's programme range, and its dealer network is the one to check on your cruising ground before you commit.

Best for: a galley that cooks sous vide seriously. Sammic's machines are sensor-controlled — the maker's claim is that they reach the exact vacuum percentage asked for, every cycle, rather than running the pump for a set time.

That is the honest split between these two, and why both are here: Henkelman for the build and the programmes, Sammic for repeatability. If every bag has to behave the same way on Thursday as it did on Monday, the sensor is the argument. Neither claim has a published head-to-head behind it.

Under 60 mAnova Precision Chamber$374 · 34.5 × 28.5 cm, 280 W

What you give up: depth — −0.95 bar against a professional 2 mbar — and cycle speed. Not the principle: it is a true chamber.

Best for: the boat where the machine has to disappear between services. It goes in a cupboard, and 280 W is about 1.2 A at 230 V for the seconds a cycle runs, so the generator is never the constraint. The 22.5 cm of height with the lid up is.

This is the compromise worth making. A small chamber does the thing an expensive edge sealer still cannot — hold stock in the bag — and it stows.

The circulatorBreville | PolyScience HydroPro Plus~1,485 W · NSF and cUL certified

What you give up: price, and a touchscreen to keep dry in a galley that moves.

Best for: precision on a small bath. A digital needle probe allows Delta-T cooking, and one independent test had it at 132 °F in fifteen minutes — among the fastest measured (Reviewed, 2026; a consumer-test figure, not a maker spec).

The certification is the part a yacht should care about: NSF and cUL for commercial use is what an auditor reads, and most circulators sold to chefs do not carry it.

The circulator — level with itSammic SmartVideContainers to 56 L

What you give up: the probe and the Delta-T toolbox.

Best for: volume. The maker rates it for containers up to 56 litres — on the order of 200 bags at 200 g regenerated at once, which is crew lunch on a big boat rather than four steaks.

The second pair fighting for the top: HydroPro Plus for precision on a small bath, SmartVide for volume on a large one — and if the sealer is already Sammic, one dealer and one service call is a real argument on a boat.

Skip itThe edge sealer as your only machine$114–155

What you give up: every wet thing you cook. It pumps through the bag's mouth, so stock, marinade and anything with a sauce goes to the port.

Best for: rice, flour, coffee, dry-cured meat, and anything going straight to the freezer. As a second machine in a drawer it earns its place.

Its own specification is the honest one: no final pressure appears because it cannot hold bag and room at the same pressure, and the maker does not claim it can.

Tested at sea — the rule, not these machines.
