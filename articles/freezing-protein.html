Freezing Protein: Fast, Flat, Sealed, and Colder Than −18 °C

Frozen well, fish and meat come back close to fresh; frozen slowly in air, they lose water, colour and flavour. The speed, the seal, the steady cold, the parasite law, and the six ways galleys get it wrong.

Freeze it the day it lands: dry, portioned, sealed flat, on metal, into the coldest box on board, and hold it steady at −25 °C. Anything served raw does −20 °C for a full 24 hours first.

The Problem

Frozen well, protein comes back close to fresh. Frozen the usual way, it pays for the freezer in drip.

Below −10 °C bacteria stop growing, and food held at a constant −18 °C stays safe indefinitely. The limit is quality, and it is decided on the way in. The usual way wastes it: shop tray, air in the film, stacked on warm packs, in a box that warms every time the door opens. Out comes frost in the wrap, grey patches, and a pink pool of drip on the board.

The Move

Three controls: fast on the way down, sealed from air, still once frozen.

Take the protein through the crystal zone fast, seal it, and hold it at one steady temperature. Speed sets the size of the crystals. The seal stops the surface drying and the fat going rancid. Steady cold stops the crystals growing afterwards: the control almost everyone forgets.

A fast freeze buys the first month, not the year. Fast-frozen pork dripped no more than fresh, but after four weeks in storage the gap between fast and slow had gone. In beef held between −5 and −20 °C, crystals grew to the same limit size whatever the freezing rate. The freeze decides the first weeks. The freezer decides the rest.

A fur trader learned this on the ice.

In Labrador between 1912 and 1915, Clarence Birdseye saw fish from Inuit ice holes freeze almost at once in air near −40 °C and eat close to fresh months later. The plate freezer and the blast cell come from that lesson.

The Steps

Dock to freezer. Step three is where quality is won.

  1. Freeze on day one, never on day four.

    Cold stops the clock. It does not wind it back.

    day of purchase

  2. Dry it, portion it, seal it flat.

    Air means freezer burn and rancid fat; thickness means time. A 5 cm pack freezes solid in about two hours. Whole fish get a glaze.

    ≤ 5 cm · vacuum

  3. Pre-chill, then one layer, on metal.

    Metal pulls heat far faster than air. A big warm load drags the whole box back through the crystal zone.

    ≤ 1 kg per 28 L per day

  4. Stack once solid, and label.

    Species, cut, weight, date. Oldest to the front.

    first in, first out

  5. Hold it cold and still, and thaw it cold.

    −25 to −30 °C doubles or triples the life of anything fatty. Trust a max/min thermometer in the box, not the display. Thaw in the fridge or a cold brine, never on the counter.

    −25 °C · max/min thermometer

The Numbers

The cold ladder, warmest first.

−1 to −5 °CThe crystal zone. About 70 % of a fish's water turns to ice here. Cross it in under two hours.
−6 °CRaw yolk starts to set for good.
−10 °CBacteria stop growing. They do not die.
−18 °CThe legal storage floor for frozen food. Held here unbroken, food stays safe; only quality falls.
−25 to −30 °CWhere fatty fish belongs. Its life doubles at −25 and triples at −30 °C, against −18.
−55 °CThe tuna trade's cold. At −20 °C tuna browns as the enzyme that keeps it red fails; at −55 °C bluefin held its colour over six months.

Cold pauses bacteria; it does not clean anything. In fish and prawns held three months at −20 °C, Listeria fell by less than one log, so more than a tenth of it was still alive. Histamine in tuna left warm on deck survives both freezing and cooking. Fridge-thawed protein may be refrozen, worse each round; counter-thawed is cooked or binned. The one hazard cold kills is parasites, on the law's terms:

The hard line · fish served raw

−20 °C × 24 h

Every part of the fish, core included, for the full time, or −35 °C for 15 hours (EU Reg. 1276/2011). The US FDA asks for 7 days at −20 °C. A domestic freezer at −18 °C meets neither.

A thick loin in a crowded box can take most of a day to reach it at the core. For crudo, buy it certified, or time it on a probe.

Frozen Against Fresh

Well frozen comes close to fresh. Tired "fresh" loses to it.

The FAO calls properly sea-frozen fish, thawed, almost indistinguishable from fresh fish a few days on ice. A trained panel on ten cod products found storage method made only a minor difference early on, more over time. Beef held 90 days at −20 or −80 °C showed no difference and tasted no less juicy. Done carelessly, it shows: ostrich frozen a month at −20 °C lost 5 % of its weight on thawing and came out tougher.

Best-frozen does not beat truly fresh; it beats tired. This morning's fish wins. Four-day-old "fresh" loses to the loin frozen at −30 °C the day it landed.

When It Breaks

The six bad habits, and the save for each.

Frost inside the shop wrap, grey leathery patches

Air in the pack: ice evaporates from the surface and the dried flesh oxidises.

Safe to eat: trim the patches. Vacuum-pack everything else today.

A red puddle under the thawing loin, soft grainy flesh

Slow freezing, in a thick block or stacked on warm packs: big crystals tore the cells.

Braise, poach or mince it rather than serving it as a steak. Next time: 5 cm packs, one layer, on metal.

Snow in the bag, spongy texture after a few months

The box swings: door, defrost cycle, generator off. Small crystals feed the big ones.

Cook it wet. Put a max/min thermometer in the box and stop the swings.

A sharp, paint-like smell off salmon or mackerel

The fat has oxidised: too long at −18 °C, with air in the pack.

Bin it; no sauce hides rancid fat. Keep fatty fish under four months at −18 °C, longer only in a colder box.

Seasoned mince tasting stale after six weeks

The salt sped up oxidation of the fat.

Use it in a heavily spiced dish. From now on, freeze mince plain.

Refrozen fish that weeps and flakes to mush

Every thaw and refreeze grows the crystals again.

Cook it and serve it cooked. Freeze in service portions so you only ever thaw what you will use.

At sea, the freezer itself is the risk:

At sea

Generator off at anchor, the door open thirty times on provisioning day, a box too full for air to move. A full, closed freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours without power, a half-full one about 24. Pull the day's stock in one opening.

On charter

Guests get the centre loins, frozen at −30 °C and logged for raw service. Crew get the tails, collars and long-held lean fish, cooked through in the curry, where a little lost water does not matter.

The Proof

You know it worked when: a 5 cm pack is solid in about two hours, the bag is still clear of snow after two months, and the thawed fillet leaves the tray barely damp and holds its shape in the pan.

That is the whole reason for the precision.

Freezing well is how the crew still eats good fish a week out from the last port, and how a guest gets proper tuna in the middle of an ocean.
