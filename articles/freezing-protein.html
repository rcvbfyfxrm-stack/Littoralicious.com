Freezing Protein: Fast, Flat, Sealed, and Colder Than −18 °C

Frozen well, fish and meat come back close to fresh; frozen slowly in air, they lose water, colour and flavour. The speed, the seal, the steady cold, the parasite law, and the six ways galleys get it wrong.

Freeze it the day it lands: dry, portioned, sealed flat, on metal, into the coldest box on board, and hold it steady at −25 °C or below. Anything going out raw has to have done −20 °C for a full 24 hours first.

The Problem

The freezer is the best tool on board, and most galleys use it as a cupboard.

Frozen well, protein is the most flexible stock you carry: buy where the fish is good, cook it three weeks later mid-crossing, never bin the second half of a case. Bacterial growth stops below about −10 °C (FAO, 1994), and the USDA says food held constantly at −18 °C is always safe. Only the quality suffers. That quality is decided by how the food went in.

The usual way throws it away. The loin goes in still in its shop tray, air in the film, on top of last week's packs, in a box that warms every time the door opens. Weeks later it comes out with frost inside the wrap, grey patches on the flesh, and sheds a pool of pink liquid on the board.

That pool is drip loss: water, soluble protein and colour the muscle could no longer hold. Slow freezing puts it there, and a freezer whose temperature swings adds to it every week.

The Technique

The Move

Three controls do the whole job: speed on the way down, a seal against air, and cold that stays still.

Push the protein through the zone of maximum crystal formation as fast as the galley allows. Seal it so no air touches the surface. Then hold it at one cold, steady temperature. Speed decides how big the ice crystals are when they form. The seal stops the surface drying out and the fat going rancid. Steady cold stops the crystals growing afterwards, which is the part almost everyone forgets.

A fast freeze buys you a head start, not a guarantee. In pork frozen fast, drip on thawing matched fresh meat; after four weeks in storage the gap between fast and slow freezing had disappeared (Ngapo et al., 1999). The freeze decides the first month and the freezer decides every month after that.

None of this came out of a laboratory. A fur trader learned it on the ice.

In Labrador between 1912 and 1915, Clarence Birdseye saw fish pulled from Inuit ice holes freeze solid almost at once in air near −40 °C, and taste close to fresh when cooked months later. The lesson is the plate freezer, the blast cell and the vacuum bag: speed and contact with cold metal. Japan's tuna trade took it to the extreme, freezing at sea far below −50 °C and keeping it there.

The Steps

Five moves, from the dock to the freezer; the third is where the quality is won.

  1. Freeze on day one, never on day four. Cold stops the clock; it does not wind it back. Whatever state the protein goes in, it comes out in that state or worse.
  2. Dry, portion and seal flat. Pat the surface dry and cut to service portions no thicker than 5 cm (2 in). The USDA puts a 5 cm pack at about two hours to freeze solid. Vacuum-seal it; if you have no sealer, press out the air and double-wrap. Whole fish get a glaze.
  3. Pre-chill, then lay single on metal. Start from fridge-cold, never warm. Spread the packs in one layer on a steel tray or straight on the evaporator plate, in the blast chiller if you have one. Load no more than roughly 1 kg per 28 litres of freezer volume in a day (NCHFP); a bigger warm load drags everything already in there back up through the crystal zone.
  4. Stack once solid, and label. Species, cut, weight, date. Oldest to the front.
  5. Hold cold and still. −18 °C is the legal floor for storage; −25 to −30 °C doubles or triples the life of anything fatty. Keep the door shut and a thermometer inside the box, not on the display. When it is time, thaw in the fridge or in a cold brine, never on the counter.

Put a max/min thermometer inside every freezer and read it at every provisioning. A box that shows −18 °C at 0800 but touched −9 °C overnight is doing more damage to your stock than anything you do at the bench.

Each protein carries its own weak point into the cold: its fat, its salt, or its water.

  • Lean white fish (cod, hake, sole, bass): freezes well, but its proteins stiffen slowly into a dry, spongy texture. Some white fish break down TMAO into formaldehyde, which cross-links the flesh (Sikorski et al., 1976). Colder is slower.
  • Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel, sardine, tuna belly): the fat oxidises and turns rancid first. Four months at −18 °C against twelve at −30 °C (IIR, via FAO 1994). Vacuum packing measurably slows the rancid taste and smell (Sone et al., 2020).
  • Shellfish: freeze prawns, squid and cooked crab or lobster meat. Never freeze live bivalves; shuck first, and keep the meat three to four months at most.
  • Red meat, whole cuts: the most forgiving protein. Steaks and roasts keep a year at −18 °C.
  • Poultry: keeps a year whole. Dark bones after thawing young birds are pigment from the marrow, harmless (USDA FSIS).
  • Mince, sausage, bacon, anything salted: the shortest life. Salt speeds fat oxidation in the cold (Lee et al., 1997): freeze mince plain and season it after thawing. Bacon: one to two months.
  • Eggs: never in the shell. Raw yolk sets into an irreversible paste below about −6 °C; beat yolks with 10 % salt or sugar first (Primacella et al., 2018), or beat whole eggs together. Whites freeze fine alone.
Why It Works

Crystals. Slow freezing grows large ice crystals between the muscle cells, pulling water out of them; fast freezing makes many small crystals inside the cells (FAO 1991, after the IIR). Big crystals tear the cells, and on thawing they cannot take their water back. Storage. Every temperature swing melts small crystals onto big ones. In beef stored between −5 and −20 °C, crystals grew to a limit size that did not depend on how fast the meat was first frozen (Martino & Zaritzky, 1989). Surface. Freezer burn is ice turning straight to vapour where air meets the flesh. The dried patch then oxidises. Evidence: FAO, IIR and USDA guidance plus controlled studies on beef, pork and fish · strong for the mechanism, moderate for the size of the effect.

The Safety Line

Cold stops bacteria growing; it does not clean anything.

Freezing pauses bacteria. In fish and prawns held three months at −20 °C, Listeria fell by less than one log, so more than a tenth of it was still alive (Harrison et al., 1991). Thawed, they start again. The same goes for histamine in tuna or mackerel that sat warm on the deck: once formed, it is not removed by freezing or by cooking (FDA, 2022). Cold keeps only what was already sound.

Parasites are the one hazard that freezing actually kills, and only on the regulation's terms.

For fish served raw, lightly marinated or cold-smoked, EU law requires every part of the fish to reach −20 °C for at least 24 hours, or −35 °C for 15 hours (Reg. (EU) 1276/2011). The US FDA asks for 7 days at −20 °C in an ordinary freezer. A domestic freezer at −18 °C meets neither. A thick loin in a crowded box can take a day just to reach its core temperature. For crudo, buy fish certified as frozen for parasites, or time it on a probe in a freezer you have measured.

Refreezing is about quality more than safety. Protein thawed in the fridge may go back uncooked (USDA FSIS), but every cycle grows the crystals again. Anything thawed on the counter, or out over two hours, is cooked or binned, never refrozen.

Frozen Against Fresh

Well-frozen comes close to fresh, and the evidence says how close.

The FAO's fisheries manual says fish frozen properly at sea is, once thawed, almost indistinguishable from fresh fish kept a few days on ice. A trained panel testing ten cod products found fresh, frozen or gas-packed storage made only a minor difference after short storage, growing with time (Sveinsdóttir et al., 2010). In beef stored up to 90 days, freezing at −20 or −80 °C made no measurable difference. The meat held a little less water by day 90, but tasters found it no less juicy (Vieira et al., 2009).

Best-frozen does not beat truly fresh; it beats tired. Fish on ice from a good dock this morning wins. Four-day-old "fresh" from a market that doesn't say when it landed loses to the loin frozen at −30 °C the day it came off the boat.

The counter-case is industrial freezing done without care. Ostrich meat frozen one month at −20 °C lost 5 % of its weight on thawing and came out tougher than fresh (Leygonie et al., 2012). Colour is where tuna shows the cold. At −20 °C the enzyme that keeps the flesh red fails and it browns, while −30 and −40 °C held the colour (Yamanaka et al., 1973). In bluefin, −55 °C beat −18 °C clearly over six months (Wang et al., 2022).

At sea, the difference is made by the freezer more than the freeze.

Galley freezers have their own failures: the generator switched off overnight at anchor, the door opened thirty times during provisioning, the box packed so full that the air cannot move. Each swing grows the crystals again. A full, closed freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours without power, a half-full one about 24 (USDA FSIS). Keep the fatty fish and the fish meant for raw service at the back of the coldest box, and do the day's pulls in one opening.

Freeze with care and one purchase covers two services.

Guests get the centre loins, frozen at −30 °C and certified or logged for raw service, for the crudo and the bare sear. The crew get the tails, the collars and the lean white fish from the long-hold box, cooked through in the curry or the fish pie, where a little lost moisture does not matter.

The Proof

You know it worked when: a 5 cm pack is solid in about two hours; the bag is clear of snow after two months; and the thawed fillet leaves the tray barely damp, glossy, holding its shape in the pan the way it did the day it was bought.

That gloss is the point, because freezing on a boat is a promise made weeks ahead.

Freezing is how the crew still eats fish a week out from the last port, and how a guest gets the good tuna in the middle of the ocean. Done properly, the freezer is how the far end of a passage still gets fed well.
