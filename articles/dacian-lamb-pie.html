Dacian Lamb Pie, Rebuilt From What the Ground Kept

No Dacian recipe survives. This pie uses only what their excavations and their neighbours prove they had — bread wheat, butter, sheep's cheese, garlic, millet and lamb — baked under a lid, with the millet there to drink the juice.

Rest it twenty minutes and cut: the wedge should stand on the board — a butter crust that snaps, lamb and sheep’s cheese held by millet that drank every drop the meat let go — and no puddle in the pan. That is the whole problem of a closed meat pie, and a grain the Dacians cooked into porridge solves it. No Dacian wrote this recipe down; every ingredient in it was dug out of a Dacian site or recorded by someone who met them.

The one idea: the millet is the sponge. Chopped lamb gives up its juice long before the pie is done; scalded millet, mixed through the raw filling, drinks that juice inside the crust, so the base bakes crisp instead of stewing and the slice holds its shape.

Where it comes from

The Dacians left almost nothing in writing, so their kitchen is read from the ground. At Sarmizegetusa Regia, the capital Trajan took in 106 AD, the burnt granaries held mostly bread wheat — the species in your flour bag, not an “ancient grain”. One house there kept a few cloves of garlic, the only garlic yet reported from that age in Romania. Broad beans, lentils, peas and millet recur from site to site. And the one cooked dish anyone has recovered, two charred lumps from Șimleu Silvaniei of the first century BC or AD, is millet cooked wet: porridge, not bread.

The neighbours fill in the rest. Strabo records that the Greeks counted the Getae as Thracians who shared one language with the Dacians, and quotes Posidonius on Danube Thracians who kept flocks, would not eat them, and lived on honey, milk and cheese. The bones say most people did eat meat, and at the earlier Getic hill forts of Moldavia it was mostly beef — so lamb is here as the animal of the cheese, not the everyday roast. A Greek comedy mocks a Thracian royal wedding for its crowds of butter-eaters: hence butter, never olive oil. What nobody recorded is what the Dacians called a pie. That word came later, with Rome.

Both Romanian words for this dish and its oven are Latin. Plăcintă, pie, comes from placenta — the etymological dictionary finds it surviving in Romanian alone — and țest, the clay bell still set over bread on a hot hearth, comes from testum. Cato wrote both down around 160 BC: his placenta is sheets of dough layered with fourteen pounds of sheep’s cheese and honey, set on oiled bay leaves on a hot hearth, covered with a heated testum and heaped with coals. His savoury version, scriblita, is the same build without the honey. — Cato, De Agri Cultura 76 and 78; Cioranescu, Dicționarul etimologic al limbii române, s.v. plăcintă; DEX, s.v. țest

The borrowed words describe a method. The Dacian larder brings a problem Cato’s cheese cake never had: raw meat, and the water it lets go of as it cooks.

Lamb gives up its juice before millet can take it. The collagen in the shoulder denatures and shrinks between 53 and 63 °C, wringing water from the meat. Millet only drinks in earnest once its starch gelatinises, and across 95 varieties of proso millet that happens between 71.5 and 79.0 °C; soaked below that, even at 70 °C, the grain holds less than half its dry weight in water. The juice arrives first and the sponge switches on ten to fifteen degrees later. Hence the scald before the millet goes in, and a centre that must pass 80 °C (176 °F), not the 71 °C that makes minced lamb safe. — Li et al., Food Chemistry 324 (2020) 126863, DOI: 10.1016/j.foodchem.2020.126863; Balkrishna & Visvanathan, Journal of Food Science and Technology 56 (2019) 3534–3539, DOI: 10.1007/s13197-019-03789-4; Tornberg, Meat Science 70 (2005) 493–508, DOI: 10.1016/j.meatsci.2004.11.021

The ratio — memorise, rescale anything
Filling: lamb 100% · soaked sheep’s cheese 25% · millet 10% dry, scalded 1:1 in water · garlic 2% · salt 1.25%  |  Crust: wholemeal flour 100% · boiling water 45% · butter 30% · salt 1.5% — the millet is sized to the meat, one part dry grain to ten of lamb, because the meat is where the juice comes from; the lamb gets only 1.25% salt because the cheese brings its own. The crust runs wetter than a white-flour hot-water crust because wholemeal drinks more.

Method

0/0 steps done

  1. Cato soaked his sheep’s cheese and changed the water three times. A brined cheese needs it more than his fresh one did.

    Soak the cheese. Cut it into 3 cm pieces, cover with three times its volume of cold water and refrigerate 2 hr, changing the water twice. Taste a crumb: milky and lightly salted, no longer sharp. Drain, squeeze hard in a clean cloth, and crumble to 1 cm.

    Why
    Salt leaves the cheese for fresh water the way it went in, by diffusion, and it moves fastest while the difference is steep: small pieces, cold water, changed often. The squeeze matters as much as the soak — water left in the crumb is water the millet has to drink. — Cato, De Agri Cultura 76

  2. No grinder in a Dacian house, and none needed: a knife, and an hour with the salt.

    Chop and salt the lamb. Take out the sinew and the thickest seams of fat, keep the rest. Cut into 1 cm strips, then across into 5–8 mm pieces. Work in the 20 g salt and the garlic with your hands for two minutes, until the mass turns tacky and clings to itself. Cover and refrigerate 1 hr, or up to 24 h. This is the bind the burger refuses: here you want it.

    Why
    Salt swells the muscle fibres and draws out myosin, and on heating that protein sets into a gel that holds the chopped pieces and their water together — the reason a sausage slices. In a burger it is a fault. In a pie filling that must stand up on the board, it is the point. — Offer & Trinick, Meat Science 8 (1983) 245–281, DOI: 10.1016/0309-1740(83)90013-X; Ruusunen & Puolanne, Meat Science 70 (2005) 531–541, DOI: 10.1016/j.meatsci.2004.07.016

  3. Romanian kept a word for hulled millet boiled and eaten with butter and cheese: păsat.

    Scald the millet. Melt the 40 g butter in a wide pan, add the drained millet and toast it over medium heat for 3–4 min, stirring, until it smells of nuts and the first grains pop. Pour on the 160 g boiling water — it spits — stir once, lid on, off the heat for 10 min. The water should be gone and the grains swollen but firm at the centre. Spread on a tray to cool. It should taste underdone.

    Why
    Boiling water starts the grain’s gelatinisation with too little water to finish it, so the millet goes into the filling still thirsty and completes its cooking on the lamb’s juice. Raw millet would stall below its gelatinisation range and stay gritty; fully cooked millet would have nothing left to drink. — Li et al. 2020; Balkrishna & Visvanathan 2019

  4. The Greeks laughed at the Thracians for eating butter. The butter goes in the crust.

    Make the hot-water butter crust. Flour and salt in a bowl. Bring the 340 g water and the 225 g butter to a full boil together, pour over the flour, mix with a spoon, then knead for a minute while hot to a smooth, soft dough. Cover and rest 15 min, still warm. Divide it into one 660 g piece per pie; from each, roll 380 g to a 34 cm circle for the base and 280 g to a 29 cm circle for the lid, about 4 mm thick. Roll it warm; cold, it cracks.

    Why
    Boiling water and melted fat wet the flour hot, which leaves the dough soft and plastic rather than springy: it rolls without shrinking back, pinches shut into a seam and holds a wet filling. That is the logic of the English hot-water pie crust, made here with butter and wholemeal. Wholemeal stiffens quickly as it cools, so keep it covered, work fast, and patch any tear with a pinch of dough.

  5. Taste now. Once the lid is on, there is no second chance.

    Fill and close. Mix the lamb, the cold millet and the cheese by hand until just even. Fry a walnut-sized piece in a dry pan, taste, and adjust the salt. Brush two heavy 28 cm pans — cast iron or deep cake tins, 4 cm deep — with melted butter and line with the bases, pressed into the corners, overhang left on. Divide the filling, about 1.2 kg a pie, and press it level and firm, no air pockets. Lid on, fold the overhang over its edge and crimp tight. Cut a 1 cm steam hole in the centre and brush the lids with butter.

    Why
    An air pocket becomes a steam pocket that lifts the lid off the filling and leaves a hollow under the crust. Pressed firm, the filling bakes into one slice. The hole lets steam out through the top instead of forcing juice out through the crimp.

  6. Cato: cover it with a hot testum, heap coals on top and around, and uncover it two or three times to look.

    Bake under the bell, then open it. Heat the oven to 220 °C (425 °F), fan 200 °C, with a heavy tray or baking steel on the lowest shelf for at least 20 min. Set the pies on the hot steel and cover each with an upturned pan of the same size or a tight foil dome: that is the bell. Bake 30 min. Uncover, turn the oven down to 200 °C (400 °F), fan 180 °C, and bake 25–35 min more, until the lid is deep brown and dry and a probe through the steam hole reads 80 °C (176 °F) in the centre.

    Why
    Covered, the pie bakes in its own steam: the lid stays pale and supple while heat climbs in from the hot steel beneath — the hearth — and the filling comes up to temperature. Uncovered, the surface dries and browns. The colour tells you about the crust; only the probe tells you the millet has finished. — Cato, De Agri Cultura 76; USDA FSIS, Lamb from Farm to Table (71 °C for minced lamb)

  7. The rest is part of the cooking.

    Rest, then cut. Leave the pies in their pans on a rack for 20 min. The juice at the steam hole should have gone thick and glossy. Run a knife round the edge and cut each pie into six wedges.

    Why
    During the rest the millet keeps drinking and the filling firms as the heat evens out from edge to centre. Cut straight from the oven and the slice slumps into its own juice; after twenty minutes it stands.

Troubleshooting
A puddle under the slice, a soggy baseToo little millet, cheese not squeezed, or cut before the rest → millet at 10% of the lamb, squeeze the cheese dry, rest 20 min
Gritty milletIt went in raw, or the centre never reached 80 °C → scald it first; bake on until the probe reads 80
Filling crumbles out of the sliceThe lamb was not salted ahead, or was trimmed too lean → an hour with the salt, keep the fat
Too saltyCheese not soaked, or seasoning not tested → two hours, three waters; fry a spoonful before you close the pie
Dough cracks as you rollIt cooled → roll it warm, keep it covered, patch tears with a pinch of dough
Lid pale, base darkCovered too long, or the steel too hot → uncover at 30 min; move up a shelf for the last 10

The lid is deep brown, dry to the touch and sounds hollow when tapped · the probe reads 80 °C (176 °F) through the steam hole · the juice bubbling at the vent is thick and glossy, not thin and pink.

Charter prep & storage

Nearly everything happens before the oven: soak and salt the day before, and the pie is an hour of work on the day.

The day beforeSoak and squeeze the cheese; chop and salt the lamb; keep both covered at 4 °C or below
The crustMake and roll it on the day — a cold hot-water crust cracks. Line the pans and chill them, covered, up to 24 h if you must get ahead
Raw piesAssemble, wrap and freeze in the pans up to a month; thaw overnight in the fridge and give them 10 min longer under the bell
Baked piesCool, refrigerate within 2 h, eat within 3 days; reheat wedges covered at 180 °C (350 °F) until the centre reads 74 °C (165 °F)
ProvisioningHulled millet keeps months in a sealed tin, and brined cheese keeps in its brine — buy both before the long passage, the lamb at the last port
In a swellCast iron does not slide; bake on the lowest shelf, where the upturned pan on top also catches a spill

Once you own the ratio — ten parts lamb to one of millet, a quarter of soaked cheese, butter boiled into the crust — the pie is yours: dried plums through the filling, honey on the lid, dill and lovage for a modern Romanian table. Cook once, serve two tiers: the guests get the two round pies as written; the crew get the same filling made with lamb neck or mutton, a little more millet, and baked as a slab under a lid of crust only — half the pastry, the same slice. What the Dacians called it is lost. What they had in the store is not.

A pie that has to rest twenty minutes is a pie that is ready before the table is, which is the easiest kind to serve.

Guests get eight wedges from a pie, a sharp green salad beside and a knife and fork. Crew get six from a pie and nothing else: it is a whole meal in the hand, and it goes up to the wheelhouse wrapped in a napkin for whoever is on watch.
