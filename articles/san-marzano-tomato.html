San Marzano: The Sauce Tomato Grown to Be Tinned

The seal names two plants and forty towns, the savour hides in the seeds you scoop out, and the tin beats the vine for lycopene by nearly four to one.

Solanum lycopersicum ‘San Marzano’The golden apple that turned red, the nightshade that clears its own poison, a plain built of Vesuvius’s fallout — and the two plants the law actually names. Out of the tin: whole, 60–80 mm, pointed, more than twice as long as it is wide.

Italian kept the first colour: pomodoro is pomo d’oro, because the fruit Mattioli described in 1544 ripened to gold (Gentilcore 2010); English took Nahuatl tomatl through Spanish. The trade names are shapes and places: pelati is the peeled fruit in the tin, Roma the long plum type that shares the silhouette, San Marzano a town on the Sarno plain under Vesuvius. The family is the Solanaceae, and the kinship tells a cook one thing: vine and green fruit carry the bitter alkaloid tomatine, and ripening clears it from the flesh (Friedman 2002, J. Agric. Food Chem.). The red fruit is dinner; the plant is not.

The wild tomato is Andean, the cherry-sized Solanum pimpinellifolium of Peru and Ecuador, half-tamed there and finished in Mexico before Spain shipped it east (Razifard et al. 2020, Mol. Biol. Evol.); Italy grew it as a curiosity from the 1540s and had it in a sauce by the 1690s (Gentilcore 2010). The San Marzano is young and its parentage argued — a sport of the local Corbarino, or a chance cross on the plain — but its first season is on record: 1903–04, in the Agro Sarnese-Nocerino and straight to the peeling lines (Ercolano et al. 2014, BMC Genomics, after Zago 1929).

The San Marzano the law protects is not the plant of 1904. The DOP is reserved by name to two later selections — San Marzano 2 and Kiros, the former Cirio Selection 3 — and to lines bred from them inside the zone (disciplinare, art. 2); Kiros, an improved ecotype of the old line, is what the plots grow today (Di Mola et al. 2023, Plants).

A preserving tomato before it was a famous one — peeled, jarred and boiled so a summer glut fed a family till spring, then tinned on the plain and shipped after the emigrants (Gentilcore 2010) — it got its seal in July 1996, when Brussels registered Pomodoro S. Marzano dell’Agro Sarnese-Nocerino as a PDO (Reg. (EC) 1263/96); the growers’ consortium followed in 1999, and the rule the tin lives under is the disciplinare of 26 August 2019. On your dock it holds one form: the whole peeled fruit, or fillets of it, in its own juice, from forty named towns.

In the mouth: from the tin, a soft ripe attack with less bite than a round tomato, a dense, low-water middle and a finish savoury before it is sweet — the agrodolce the plain names it by. Cooked, it does the one thing a tomato is for: it makes a pot taste of more than what went into it, and thickens it without flour. Raw it is quieter and greener, with the cut-grass edge of (Z)-3-hexenal, the fresh fruit’s biggest volatile (Buttery et al. 1987), which heat takes away. Crush a whole one in your fist and it gives without spraying: the jelly slides out of its two or three chambers in one cool piece, and the smell off the seeds is the sharp green of the vine, not the sauce.

The dock says the tin is what you open when the market is shut, and every nonna scoops the seeds over the side before the sauce goes on. The science says the opposite of both, and is less kind to the tomato’s reputation as a medicine.

Why It Works

The Science: More Cooked Than Raw, and the Savour Is in the Seeds

Four numbers, then the mechanism that makes them matter.

The seeds are not the waste. Across thirteen varieties the jelly around them carried 3.6 times the glutamate of the flesh, and 3.7 times the AMP — 4.56 g/kg against 1.26, with some varieties past sixfold (Oruna-Concha et al. 2007, J. Agric. Food Chem.). Deseeding a tomato is scooping out its umami; straining a tin of pelati is pouring it down the sink.

The Chemistry
What is in the fruit, and why the tin gets more out of it
  • Glutamate and the nucleotides — the tomato is the rare fruit built on the savoury amino acid, with AMP beside it; the two classes multiply each other on the tongue (Yamaguchi 1967, J. Food Sci.), which is why a plain sugo reads as a dish and not a purée, and why the same molecule can cut a sauce’s salt.
  • Lycopene — heat breaks the cells and bends the molecule. The same 23 mg dose, eaten with 15 g of oil, gave 2.5× the peak and 3.8× the total plasma response from paste as from fresh fruit (Gärtner, Stahl & Sies 1997, Am. J. Clin. Nutr.); a sauce processed to 45% cis-lycopene was absorbed 54% better than an all-trans one (n = 12; Unlu et al. 2007, Br. J. Nutr.); thirty minutes at 88 °C (190 °F) raised extractable trans-lycopene 2.6-fold and cost 29% of the vitamin C (Dewanto et al. 2002).
  • Pectin and its two enzymes — crush a raw tomato and its own PME and PG start cutting the pectin that gives a sauce body; taken fast to 90 °C (194 °F) or more, the trade’s hot break, both stop and the juice stays thick (Anthon et al. 2002; Xu et al. 1986, J. Food Sci.). A tin has had its hot break. A blender has not.
  • Acid over sugar — the plant’s foliage shades the fruit, which pushes lycopene up and sugar down; citric acid is the main acid and the balance is the agrodolce (Dono et al. 2020, Metabolites). The tin must hold at least 5.0% soluble solids (disciplinare, art. 6).

Put together: the savour is water-soluble and sits in the jelly, the colour is fat-soluble and sits in the cell walls, and the body is pectin that the fruit’s own enzymes will eat if you let them. Crush the whole fruit with its jelly, take it fast past 90 °C in oil, then hold it at a bare simmer: the savour stays, the lycopene comes out, the body holds.

Is it a remedy?

The absorption is real; the cure is not.

  • Cooked tomato does put more lycopene in the blood. Eleven adults ate 470 g of tomato cooked with 25 ml of olive oil once a day for five days and plasma trans-lycopene rose 82%; twelve who ate the same tomato without oil did not move (Fielding et al. 2005, Asia Pac. J. Clin. Nutr.). Strength: moderate for absorption — small and short, but consistent with the paste and cis-isomer trials above.
  • Nothing downstream is shown. Cochrane found three randomised trials of lycopene against prostate cancer, 154 men in all, two at high risk of bias, and insufficient evidence to support or refute it (Ilic, Forbes & Hassed 2011). EFSA assessed every lycopene claim filed under the EU health-claims regulation — DNA, skin, heart, eyes — and substantiated none (EFSA Journal 2011;9(4):2031). Eat the sauce for the sauce.

What does survive is the grandmother’s method, not her medicine:

The oil goes in first. Tomato cooked with olive oil delivered its lycopene; the same tomato cooked without it delivered almost none (Fielding et al. 2005), and the paste that beat fresh fruit was eaten with fat too (Gärtner et al. 1997). The soffritto is a solvent.

  • Where: any sauce, soup or braise built on the tin — oil in the pan before the tomato, not drizzled on the plate.
  • How much: the trial ratio was 25 ml of extra-virgin olive oil to 470 g of tomato: about 20 ml, a generous tablespoon, per 400 g tin.
  • How often: the trial’s rhythm was one cooked portion a day, and the number had moved inside five days. There is nothing to load.
  • Its honest limit: more lycopene in the blood is a measurement, not an outcome. No trial has turned it into fewer cancers or a sounder heart, and EFSA lets nobody print that it does.
What Every Cuisine Found

The Pairings: Proven, Molecular, and One from the Bay

Two to five things on a card: the sugo and the pizza, a confit at 88 °C, the strawberry that shares its molecule, and the pot the fishermen made from sea water.

TraditionalSan Marzano + garlic + olive oil + basilNaples · the Sunday sugo · the Margherita

Oil first, because it is the solvent (Fielding et al. 2005); garlic fragrant, never brown; the tomato crushed by hand with its jelly; basil at the end, because its aroma is volatile — and because the DOP itself permits nothing green in the tin but basil leaves (disciplinare, art. 6). On a pizza it goes on raw. The Verace Pizza Napoletana rulebook puts S. Marzano of the Agro Sarnese-Nocerino first among its tomatoes, salts it lighter than any other pelati — 7–10 g a kilo against 10–12, “already a tastier product” — and lets a 430–480 °C oven do the cooking in 60–90 seconds (AVPN 2024).

ModernSan Marzano + olive oil + garlic + thyme, at 88 °CDewanto’s bench, reworked for a tray

Whole DOP fruit from the tin, drained — the juice goes to the sugo — laid in a tray under a finger of oil with split garlic and thyme, an hour at 88 °C (190 °F): the temperature at which extractable lycopene rose 2.6-fold in half an hour (Dewanto et al. 2002). The fruit keeps its shape because its hot break is behind it and its pectin intact; the oil takes the colour and the aroma. Under grilled fish, or blitzed with its oil into a dressing.

CompoundSan Marzano + strawberry + olive oil + black pepper + basilThe shared-molecule surprise

The strawberry’s signature is already in the tomato: furaneol, the caramel-fruit note, is among the compounds Buttery’s group found important to tomato aroma (1995, J. Agric. Food Chem.), and it is the strawberry’s defining aroma (Schwab 2013, Molecules). Both fruits run on citric acid. Raw and halved, salted, oiled and peppered, a plate of the two reads as one savoury fruit, not a dessert.

From the littoralSan Marzano + a whole white fish + sea water + parsleyThe Bay of Naples · acqua pazza

Reads wrong on paper, lands on the plate: acqua pazza, a bream poached in water with two crushed tomatoes, a clove of garlic, parsley and nothing else sounds like the infirmary. It lands because the fish brings the inosinate and the tomato the glutamate, the pair Yamaguchi measured multiplying each other in 1967, so the broth builds a stock’s savour in fifteen minutes with no stock in it. The fishermen used the sea; aboard, a litre of water and 20 g of salt does the same. 90 °C (194 °F), lid on, the fish out at 52 °C in the thickest part.

The strawberry card was found by the molecule the two fruits share, and there are more where it came from — that map is becoming the Pairing Wheel in the Spoon Lab: San Marzano in the middle, everything that shares a compound with it around the rim. It is not ready. It is simmering.

Tested at Sea

Galley Intel: What to Do With It, and How It Keeps

One rule runs every tile: the savour is in the jelly, the colour needs fat, and the enzymes need killing before they eat the body.

The base sugo, 10–12 covers120 ml olive oil, six crushed cloves, fragrant not coloured; three 400 g DOP tins crushed by hand, juice and all; up past the boil, then a bare simmer near 90 °C (194 °F) for 30–40 minutes; salt last, basil off the heat. Cool fast, bag flat, freeze: three months, a crossing’s worth of pasta, braises and pizza.
Hot break, not blenderA raw tomato blitzed and left standing thins in minutes as its own enzymes cut the pectin. Crush it into a hot pan and take it past 90 °C before it sits; a tin has had this done.
Never strain, never seedCrush by hand, and the tin’s juice goes into the pot: it is the jelly, pressed. A guest sauce that must be smooth is blended after cooking, never strained before.
Pizza sauce is rawTin, hand, salt, nothing cooked: ninety seconds in the oven does it. The same raw crush goes on a focaccia at 250 °C.
Salt late, sugar last of allA DOP tin may already carry up to 3% salt (art. 6): taste before you season. A sauce that reads sharp is over-reduced juice or a lesser tin; round it with more of the oil base or a Parmigiano rind before you reach for sugar.
Tomato waterA tin’s fruit crushed and hung in muslin overnight in the fridge gives about 250 ml of clear liquid that tastes of the jelly: the glutamate and AMP come through, the colour stays in the cloth. Cold over raw prawns, or warm over a poached fish at the pass.
DoseA 400 g tin: four crew plates of pasta, or four pizzas. Count tins as covers, not cans.
Storage & buying at seaSealed, in the coolest dry locker, to the date on the label; opened, into glass, three days in the fridge or frozen flat. The DOP’s packers sit in Sarno, Nocera, Angri, Scafati and Sant’Antonio Abate (Consorzio), under an hour from Salerno or Castellammare: carry a case, because the seal is harder to trust once you have left the Med.

Most tomatoes are grown to be eaten. This one was grown to be cooked, sealed and shipped — and it is better for it.

At the Dock

How to Buy: The Seal, the Shape, and the Seed That Left Home

Three things sell under the name; one carries the seal, and most of the eating quality is decided at the shelf.

The real onePomodoro San Marzano dell’Agro Sarnese-Nocerino DOP — San Marzano 2 · KirosForty communes · Salerno, Avellino, Naples

Flavour profile: dense, low-water flesh in two or three chambers with few seeds; acid ahead of sugar by nature and by rule, so the sauce is savoury before it is sweet.

Best for: every sauce where the fruit is crushed by hand and the texture shows, and the pizza. The swap to refuse: a “San Marzano” purée, which cannot exist under the seal.

The seal stops at the tin. The DOP covers the processed fruit only, whole or in fillets, so a crate of fresh San Marzano in a Campanian market, however true to seed, is never DOP (Consorzio). Picked by hand in passes between 15 July and 15 October, from a staked, indeterminate plant the rule will not let anyone force (art. 4–5), and every tin must print the harvest campaign it came from (art. 8): the only tomato you buy with a vintage.

The shape, not the seal“San Marzano style” · Roma-type tinned plumPuglia · Emilia · California · China

Flavour profile: honest and variable; often firmer, because a firming salt is allowed — the US standard permits calcium salts up to 0.045% calcium in the finished can (21 CFR 155.190) — and sharper, from citric acid added because tomato sits close to the pH 4.6 line the canning rules treat as the botulism boundary (NCHFP).

Best for: crew pots and long ragùs, where a tomato that holds its dice is a virtue. The swap to refuse: paying the DOP price for it.

Brussels asks less of a pizza than Naples does. The EU’s own Pizza Napoletana specification names no cultivar at all — “peeled tomatoes” or fresh pomodorini is the whole rule (Reg. (EU) 97/2010) — and it is the pizzaioli’s private mark, the AVPN’s, that writes San Marzano in by name.

The seed that left home“San Marzano” grown from seed outside the zoneCalifornia · Australia · anywhere with a summer

Flavour profile: the same shape and the same thin skin; what the plain adds is the part nobody has measured on another plain. The rule ties the seal to the zone as much as to the seed: the named varieties, grown, picked by hand and packed inside it (art. 2–4).

Best for: a dependable tin at a fair price. Refuse it only when it is sold at the DOP’s.

In the United States the seal has no force of its own, so the fight is consumer law: in July 2026 two Californians filed a class action against a New Jersey importer over tins labelled “certified San Marzano” — “false, misleading and unfair”, the complaint says — and the consortium’s president offered to stand with them (Qualivita, 15 July 2026).

Where it really comes from: the Sarno plain, “covered for the most part by pyroclastic material of volcanic origin” in the rule’s own words (art. 4) — Vesuvius’s fallout, its post-AD 79 deposits drilled and mapped right across the plain (Vogel, Märker & Seiler 2011, Geologica Carpathica) — deep, soft, rich in phosphorus and potassium, spring-fed. Pompei is on the list. Flat, irrigable ground only, capped at 80 tonnes a hectare (art. 3–4); the fresh fruit sells at close to five times the price of other varieties (Ercolano et al. 2014), and on a Mediterranean shelf the DOP tin runs roughly €3–6 a 400 g against €1–2 for a plain plum; through a ship’s agent, budget more again for the case.

  • What the trade may do to it: outside the seal, a firming salt and citric acid, purée in place of juice, and an epoxy lining that can shed BPA. EFSA re-assessed BPA in 2023 (EFSA Journal 2023;21(4):6857) and Commission Regulation (EU) 2024/3190, adopted 19 December 2024, bans it in food-contact materials, can varnishes included, after transition periods: look for BPA-NI on the tin until every lining has changed.
  • The most natural route: the DOP tin, because the rule is itself the clean list — the fruit, its own juice from the same DOP varieties grown in the zone, salt at no more than 3%, basil leaves, and citric acid only as a processing aid at no more than 0.5%; nothing else is permitted (art. 6). Glass is allowed (art. 7); take the jar when you see one.
  • The chef’s own supply: in season, the fresh fruit from a Campanian market for the raw work, and a case of the DOP bought where it is packed.
  • The marks — the DOP logo with its brown Vesuvius, the EU’s red-and-yellow PDO seal, and the words pomodori pelati interi or a filetti; beside them the packer’s name and the harvest campaign (art. 8).
  • The number — every certified tin is numbered under the control body (Consorzio). The number, not the name, is the guarantee.
  • The list — tomatoes, tomato juice, salt, basil, citric acid, and nothing more. Calcium chloride, purée or paste on a tin calling itself DOP is a tin lying.
  • The fruit — whole, 60–80 mm, twice as long as wide, deep red, skins off clean (art. 5–6).

And the tin you hand straight back:

Dented, rusted or blown: off the order, not just the boat. And any tin that says San Marzano without the PDO seal, the campaign year and the whole fruit inside is a shape wearing a name. A “San Marzano” passata, chopped tomato or paste cannot be DOP; the seal exists for two forms only (art. 6).

Buy the seal and the harvest year; crush the whole fruit by hand, jelly and all; cook it in oil, fast past 90 °C and then barely. The tin is not the compromise. It is the tomato’s best form.
