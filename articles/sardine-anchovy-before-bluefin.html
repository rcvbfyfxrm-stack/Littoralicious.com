The Cheap Fish Go First: Sardine and Anchovy Before Bluefin

The bet: through 2028 the cheap oily fish of the north-western Mediterranean get smaller and dearer while bluefin stays plentiful on a raised quota. Five named signals, the strongest case against, a falsifier you can read at the lonja, and the provisioning moves to make now.

The bet, and what would break it
  • The cheap oily fish of the north-western Mediterranean — sardine and anchovy — get scarcer, smaller and dearer at the quay through 2028, while bluefin stays plentiful on a quota that just rose a fifth.
  • For it: a shelf where sardines have been shrinking since 2008, a regional report that still calls the stock depleted, and a summer of +25% quay prices. Against it: an Atlantic sardine stock rebuilt to record catches. The falsifier is a fish length and a price.
  • The galley move: buy the crew's oily fish from the Atlantic, keep the Mediterranean sardine as a season, and plan bluefin for the guest card.

The forecast: between now and the end of ICCAT's 2026–2028 quota period, the fish that feeds the crew in the Gulf of Lions and along the Spanish Levante — sardine first, anchovy behind it — will be landed smaller and priced higher than in 2025, while the fish that feeds the guests, eastern bluefin, stays abundant on a raised quota. The cheap fish goes first. The stake for a galley is the crew-protein line: sardines on the plancha, anchovies in the salad, the pissaladière — and a provisioning list that still assumes they are the bargain.

The case for

The independent lines of evidence behind the call — each one named, so you can check it yourself.

What is moving is not one number but a shelf: the sardine itself, the assessments of its stock, and the price at the auction, read together, point the same way. Watch the basis — the gap between the origin price at the lonja and what the quay quotes a yacht.

  1. Since 2008 the sardines of the Gulf of Lions have shown a severe decrease in size and body condition and a truncation of the age structure; the fishery collapsed and has not recovered; the review that weighed the causes ruled out overfishing, predation and parasites and left bottom-up change in the plankton (Saraux et al. 2019, Deep-Sea Research II; review of a single shelf · Strength: moderate). → points to: the fish is shrinking, not just the catch.
  2. Sampled monthly across 2023–24, sardines landed at Sète averaged 118.9 mm against 137–138 mm on the Catalan coast and 152 mm at Fuengirola, and showed “the worst health status among the regions compared” (Frigola-Tepe et al. 2025, Frontiers in Marine Science). → points to: the 2008 shift is still the state of the shelf.
  3. The GFCM's State of Mediterranean and Black Sea Fisheries 2025 puts 52% of assessed stocks in overexploitation in 2023 — the lowest in its series — and still describes sardine as subject to sustained overexploitation with signs of biomass depletion (FAO/GFCM, November 2025). → points to: management is improving overall; sardine is the exception.
  4. This summer, the president of Alicante's fishing cooperatives put sardine 25% dearer than a year ago and anchovy 10%, with catches down in water that reached 33 °C in the south, about 3 °C above the historical average (COPE, 21 August 2026; a single-province, single-season report · Strength: provisional). Spanish wholesale sardine averaged €3.67/kg on 14 August across the five Mercasa markets, €5 in Valencia (Observatorio de Precios). → points to: the market has started moving before the assessments have.
  5. On the other side of the ledger, eastern bluefin is managed under a management procedure that ICCAT's scientists expect to keep the stock in the green with 60% probability over thirty years, and the TAC for 2026–2028 is 48,403 t (ICCAT SCRS, executive summary, September 2025). → points to: the expensive fish is the secure one.
The case against

The strongest reason this call is wrong — argued at full strength, not straw-manned.

Sardine is not scarce; Mediterranean sardine is. On the Atlantic side of Gibraltar the Iberian stock has rebuilt: biomass above the maximum-sustainable-yield threshold since 2020, a 2026 allowable catch of 50,294 t shared by Portugal and Spain, MSC certification regained, and a Spanish season that closed early this year because the quota was caught (ICES advice via the trade press; BOE, 2026). Portuguese and Cantabrian sardines already fill the tins and summer markets of the whole peninsula. If that fish moves into the Mediterranean auctions at scale, quay prices in Barcelona and Marseille flatten whatever the Gulf of Lions does — and the GFCM's own headline is that overfishing is at a decade low, with single-species harvest rules in force for the Adriatic since November 2024.

The counter-case in one line: an Atlantic sardine at record catch floods the Mediterranean auctions and the price signal disappears, leaving only a local length problem that a provisioner never sees.

What decides between the two cases is not opinion but mechanism — the driver underneath both readings.

The Gulf of Lions story is bottom-up: warmer, more stratified water since the late 2000s shortened the mixing that feeds the spring bloom, the plankton shifted toward smaller, poorer prey, and the sardines that eat it grew less, matured smaller and died younger (Saraux et al. 2019 · observational review · Strength: moderate). That mechanism does not switch off with a good Atlantic year, because the Atlantic fish is a different stock on a different shelf; it can mask the price for a season, not repair the fish. Bluefin, a large migratory predator managed on an ocean-basin quota, is insulated from any one shelf's plankton — which is why the two lines diverge rather than move together.

The call

Both cases heard — where the forecast lands, and how sure we are.

The call: through summer 2028, north-western Mediterranean sardine and anchovy stay at least a quarter above their 2025 summer quay prices, the Gulf of Lions fish stays small, and Mediterranean bluefin is neither scarcer nor dearer at the quay than in 2025. The Atlantic stock caps how far the price can run but does not reverse it, because the fish a coast sells as its own — the sardine of Sète, the boquerón of the Levante — is the one shrinking. Allow for the lead time: an assessment published in June reaches the provisioner's list a season later.

Confidence: likely. It moves to firm if the 2026 Gulf of Lions survey shows no recovery in mean length; it drops to watching if Atlantic sardine landings in Mediterranean auctions push the summer wholesale price back to 2025 levels.

What it means for the galley

The moves to make this season, before the shift hits your order sheet.

Treat the Mediterranean sardine as a seasonal pleasure bought at the quay when it is good, not as the crew's protein line; source the volume oily fish from the Atlantic; and stop treating bluefin as the fish you cannot get.

This week: move the crew's standing order to Atlantic product — Cantabrian salted anchovies and Portuguese tinned sardines by the case, fresh Atlantic sardine from Portugal in its May-to-October season — and price a ranched Mediterranean bluefin loin for the guest card at the same time; keep 2 kg of local sardine or boquerón a week as a market buy, priced on the day.

Then translate the wallet into the table — the part the guest actually meets.

Next season the sardines on the plancha are Portuguese and the anchovy on the pissaladière is from Santoña — nobody at the table notices; the bluefin tartare and the ventresca stay on the card at a price that no longer rises; and the one dish that gets rarer and dearer is the honest one, boquerones from the boat that morning, which you buy as a treat and say so.

And the way most galleys get this wrong:

The trap is the overreaction: crossing sardines off the list because “they are collapsing” while an MSC-certified Atlantic stock is landing record catches a day's drive away — or the opposite, paying a marina's mid-summer price for a small Mediterranean sardine as if it were still the bargain fish.

You do not need a terminal to track this. Two or three readings tell you which way it broke:

One: the Spanish wholesale sardine price (Observatorio de Precios, Mercasa average) — above €4.50/kg in July–August 2027 flips the call to firm; back under €3.00 triggers the falsifier. Two: the mean length of Gulf of Lions sardines in Ifremer's summer survey and the next GFCM assessment — above 135 mm for two summers running kills the call. Three: the June GFCM advice and the December EU fishing-opportunities regulation for the Adriatic — a raised sardine limit is a downgrade; a cut confirms. Bluefin needs no watching: the quota is fixed at 48,403 t to 2028.

The one thing

Buy the crew's oily fish from the Atlantic and the guests' bluefin from the Mediterranean, and treat the local sardine as a season, not a staple — because the cheap fish is the one shrinking.
