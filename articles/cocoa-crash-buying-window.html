Cocoa at $6,700 a Tonne, El Niño Ahead: Buy the Winter’s Couverture Before 1 October

Cocoa closed August at $6,710 a tonne, half its 2024 record, with a very strong El Niño forecast over the main crop that opens 1 October. Four weeks to buy the winter’s couverture.

Order the winter’s dark couverture before the first of October, by the bag, and hold it sealed under 18 °C. The main crop is about to be cut under a very strong El Niño, and this is the last order you place before the harvest tells the market what the weather did.

The Alert

A season signal, not a week signal — the shelf will not move by Friday, but next winter’s couverture is priced now.

What’s happening. The ICCO daily price closed at $6,709.86 a tonne on 28 August 2026 — New York $6,721.67, London £4,940.67 (ICCO, Cocoa Daily Prices, read 1 September). The front month is up about 10% on the month, gave back 3–4% on 1 September to around $6,500, and sits about 12% under a year ago: half the $12,906 record of December 2024. Evidence Grade: exchange price series · the world benchmark, both terminals · Strength: firm.

The context. Three seasons explain the level. 2023/24 closed with a 492,000-tonne deficit — 4.36 million tonnes grown against 4.81 million ground. 2024/25 rebuilt a surplus the ICCO has cut on every bulletin since: 75,000 tonnes in February, 48,000 in May, 37,000 on 31 August, leaving stocks at 28.2% of a year’s grind. For 2025/26 it has withheld the figures altogether — the referee has stopped publishing the score. Into that thin cushion comes the weather: on 13 August NOAA moved to an El Niño Advisory — a greater than 90% chance of a very strong event through exactly the months the main crop is cut. Ghana’s regulator already forecasts a 16% fall next season, and underneath sits swollen shoot: above 30% prevalence in Ghana (Ameyaw et al. 2023), currently uncontrollable across 150 Ivorian plots (Dumont et al. 2025). Trees leaving production is why last decade’s price is no longer the floor.

The farmer’s price was cut by more than half in March. Yours was not, and will not be.

How it hits us. The bag says Belgium or France; the bean is Ivorian or Ghanaian. In March 2026 Ivory Coast cut its guaranteed farm-gate price by more than half, 2,800 to 1,200 CFA francs a kilo — €1.83 to the farmer against an ICCO price of €5.79. At the other end, Callebaut 70% lists at £25 a kilo, about £4 of it bean. The bean is a sixth of what the galley pays; the rest is butter, grinder, brand and last mile — so buy at the bag step, because the bean premium is real and the pack premium is not. And the squeeze reaches couverture first: it is the most butter-hungry chocolate the law defines, which is why the trade’s answers are the 5% vegetable-fat allowance and the compound coating with no cocoa butter at all.

Order the winter’s dark couverture before 1 October: for twelve guests and crew over a six-week season, reckon 1.3 kg a week — three bags of 2.5–3 kg, two of 70% for ganache and the tart, one of 54–55% for glazing and the crew’s bakes — in callets from a pastry wholesaler, not the 400 g retail pack. Read the label: couverture must carry at least 31% cocoa butter (Directive 2000/36/EC); a bag printed contains vegetable fats in addition to cocoa butter is the substitute-fat product, not the one you priced. Hold it sealed below 18 °C, dark, away from the spice locker, and never in the fridge — cocoa butter’s working Form V crystal converts to the duller, harder Form VI faster when storage is warm or cycled, so a bag that spent August in a hot container arrives already grey (Lonchampt & Hartel 2004). Dark 70% keeps 18 months, milk 15; buy milk, white and filled per charter.

Watchingconfidence

Falsifies if: Ivorian port arrivals from 1 October to 31 December match or beat the same weeks of 2025 — the El Niño did not reach the main crop. That is the live case against, and it is strong: arrivals ran 1.999 million tonnes by 23 August, 20% ahead of last season, while Europe ground 316,366 tonnes in the second quarter, 4.6% under a year earlier. Demand still falling is the cap on any rally. But demand moves a few percent a year and one dry season can move a crop by a fifth — in Bahia the 2015–16 El Niño killed 15% of trees and 89% of yield across 31 farms (Gateau-Rey et al. 2018, PLOS ONE; one region, one event · Strength: moderate). A very strong El Niño loads the fast side.

We report back: in the Weekly Brief after the window closes (the week of 5 October 2026) — held, missed or overtaken — and again at the horizon, the ICCO’s February 2027 bulletin, or the week the arrivals run level.
