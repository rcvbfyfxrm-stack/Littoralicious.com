Cocoa at $6,700 a Tonne, El Niño Ahead: Buy the Winter’s Couverture Before 1 October

Cocoa closed August at $6,710 a tonne, half its 2024 record, with a very strong El Niño forecast over the main crop that opens 1 October. Four weeks to buy the winter’s couverture.

The ICCO daily price of cocoa closed at $6,709.86 a tonne on 28 August 2026 (ICCO, Cocoa Daily Prices). The front month is up about 10% on the month, gave 4% back on 1 September to around $6,500, and sits about 12% under a year ago: half the $12,906 record of December 2024 (ICE New York, via Trading Economics). The window is four weeks, to the 1 October opening of the West African main crop.

A season signal, not a week signal: the shelf will not move by Friday, but the crop that prices next year’s chocolate is about to be cut under a very strong El Niño, and the winter’s couverture is the last order you place before the harvest tells the market what the weather did.

The Move

Order the winter’s dark couverture this week, by the bag, and hold it below 18 °C.

Order the winter’s dark couverture before 1 October: for twelve guests and crew over a six-week season, reckon 1.3 kg a week — three bags of 2.5–3 kg, two of 70% for ganache and the tart, one of 54–55% for glazing and the crew’s bakes — in callets from a pastry wholesaler, not the 400 g retail pack. Read the label: couverture must carry at least 31% cocoa butter (Directive 2000/36/EC); a bag printed contains vegetable fats in addition to cocoa butter is the 5% substitute-fat product the law allows, not the one you priced. Hold it sealed below 18 °C (64 °F), dark, away from the spice locker — Valrhona’s own rule — never in the fridge. Dark 70% keeps 18 months, milk 15; buy milk, white and filled per charter.

Here is why the same order was cheaper a month ago and may be dearer by Christmas.

Why It’s Happening

Read the cause and you see the next move coming, not just this week’s print.

The ICCO daily price stood at $6,709.86 a tonne on 28 August 2026 — New York $6,721.67, London £4,940.67 (ICCO, Cocoa Daily Prices, read 1 September); New York alone printed $6,894 on 31 August, London shut, and the front month fell 3–4% on 1 September (ICE New York, via Trading Economics). Evidence Grade: exchange price series · coverage = the world benchmark, both terminals · Strength: firm.

Three seasons explain the level. 2023/24 closed with a 492,000-tonne deficit — 4.36 million tonnes grown against 4.81 million ground — the shortfall behind the 2024 spike. 2024/25 rebuilt a surplus the ICCO has cut on every bulletin since, 75,000 tonnes in February, 48,000 in May, 37,000 on 31 August, leaving stocks at 28.2% of a year’s grind. For 2025/26 it has withheld the figures altogether — the referee has stopped publishing the score.

On 13 August NOAA moved to an El Niño Advisory: a greater than 90% chance of a very strong El Niño through the northern autumn and winter, the July Niño-3.4 reading already +1.4 °C (Climate Prediction Center). Those are the months in which the main crop is cut and the Harmattan decides the next one. Ghana’s regulator, whose two western regions grow over half its cocoa, already forecasts a 16% fall next season on erratic weather, El Niño, swollen shoot, a low in the trees’ yield cycle and gold mining on cocoa land; Ivory Coast projects cuts too (Africanews, 31 July 2026).

Under the weather sits the disease, which no wet year reverses.

Ghana’s third country-wide survey puts swollen shoot prevalence above 30% (Ameyaw, Domfeh & Gyamera, Viruses, 2023); across 150 Ivorian plots surveyed in 2021–23 the epidemic is called currently uncontrollable, the greatest threat to cocoa in West Africa (Dumont et al. 2025); on farms replanted after cutting out, reinfection came earlier than expected, PCR finding infections the removal buffer had missed (Kouakou et al. 2026). Trees leaving production is why last decade’s price is no longer the floor.

And the cause changes the bag, not only the invoice.

Couverture is the most butter-hungry chocolate the law defines, so a bean squeeze reaches it first; the industry’s answers are the 5% vegetable-fat allowance and the compound coating with no cocoa butter at all. Heat is the other effect: cocoa butter’s working crystal, Form V, converts to the duller, harder Form VI, faster when storage is warm or cycled, so a bag that spent August in a hot container arrives already grey with fat bloom (Lonchampt & Hartel 2004, review).

That is the product; now the money, which does not run the way the label implies.

The bag says Belgium or France; the bean is Ivorian or Ghanaian — two countries growing 60% of the world’s cocoa (Africanews, June 2026). In March 2026 Ivory Coast’s Coffee-Cocoa Council cut the guaranteed farm-gate price by more than half, from 2,800 to 1,200 CFA francs a kilo (Africanews, 7 August): at the CFA’s euro peg, €1.83 to the farmer against an ICCO price of €5.79 — under a third. At the other end a UK supplier lists Callebaut 70% at £9.99 for 400 g, £25 a kilo, about £4 of it bean at London’s price (Sous Chef, 1 September). The bean is a sixth of what the galley pays; the rest is butter, grinder, brand and last mile. Buy at the bag step: the bean premium is real, the pack premium is not.

Strip the label off: the harvest decides the bean, the brand decides the rest.

The farmer’s price was cut by more than half in March. Yours was not, and will not be.

The Forecast

Where this goes after the window closes — the call, how sure we are, and the one reading that would kill it.

Watchingconfidence — a very strong El Niño against a demand line still falling

Falsifies if: Ivorian port arrivals from 1 October to 31 December 2026 match or beat the same weeks of 2025 — the El Niño did not reach the main crop.

Four lines of evidence, each named so you can check it, the one that cuts the other way included. The reading to learn is arrivals.

  1. NOAA, 13 August: El Niño Advisory, over 90% chance of a very strong event through the northern autumn and winter. → points to: a hot, dry main-crop season as the base case; next readings 10 September, 8 October, 12 November.
  2. Ghana’s regulator: output down 16% next season, Ivory Coast projecting cuts too (Africanews, 31 July — boards that also sell the crop forward). → points to: both origins warning before a pod is opened.
  3. The ICCO: the 2024/25 surplus cut on every revision to 37,000 tonnes, stocks 28.2% of grind, the 2025/26 figures withheld (bulletins of 27 February, 29 May, 31 August). → points to: a thinner cushion, and an official series gone quiet.
  4. Europe ground 316,366 tonnes in the second quarter, 4.6% under a year earlier; the half-year is down 6.3% and the twelve-month total a tenth below 2023 (European Cocoa Association, 16 July; nineteen companies). → points to: demand still falling — the cap on any rally, and the strongest line for the other side.

The other side, at full strength. Cocoa at $6,700 has priced a bad crop once already: the front month rose a tenth in a month on crop reassessments and the El Niño alone, while the beans actually reaching Abidjan and San Pedro ran at 1.999 million tonnes by 23 August, 20% ahead of last season (exporter tallies, via Trading Economics). Europe grinds 6% fewer beans than a year ago, none of that comes back at $7,000, and the aggregator’s own model puts the bean at $5,490 in a year.

The counter-case in one line: an arrivals pace 20% up that carries into October–December while Europe grinds 5% less takes the El Niño premium out, and cocoa is back near $5,500 by the horizon — with the winter’s couverture bought perhaps three percent too dear, because the bean is a sixth of the bag.

Mechanism decides between the two readings: which side of the balance moves fast.

Cocoa’s yield is water-limited: water deficit cuts it significantly, and trees in the field run hotter than the literature assumed (Lahive, Hadley & Daymond 2019, review). What a very strong El Niño does to a cocoa farm has been counted once: in Bahia the 2015–16 event cut rainfall 53% below the fourteen-year mean and, across 31 farms, killed 15% of the trees and 89% of the yield (Gateau-Rey et al. 2018, PLOS ONE; field survey · one region, one event · Strength: moderate — Bahia is not the Gulf of Guinea). Demand moves a few percent a year; one dry season can move a crop by a fifth. A very strong El Niño loads the fast side.

So the call leans up, and stays at watching until the arrivals say which way it broke.

The ICCO daily price above $7,500 by the February bulletin, and no return under $5,500 on the way. It moves to likely on 12 November if NOAA still prints a very strong event and October’s arrivals trail 2025’s; it is downgraded the week the arrivals run level. Allow for the lead time: the bean moves in a day, the arrivals inside a month, the provisioner’s list last.

At the Provisioning End

Here the signal becomes a buying decision you can make under space, heat and a locker that never quite holds 18 °C.

The bag Callebaut 70% at £9.99 per 400 g = £25/kg, about £4 of it bean Buy the sealed 2.5–3 kg bag from a pastry wholesaler; the retail-pack premium is the one you can dodge

Read the table back in one sentence: the bean is dear but not at its record, the bag is mostly not bean, and the weather ahead is the risk — so the cheap insurance is a season of dark couverture, held right, bought now.

What it means for the people you feed: the chocolate tart stays on the card through the winter at the same size and the same snap, the 70% under the guests’ ganache and the 54% into the crew’s cookies and the 200 g the banana bread folds through; the dish that gets dearer is the milk-and-caramel one, because milk couverture cannot be stocked for a season and will be bought in January at January’s price.

Make that buy badly, though, and the saving turns into grey chocolate.

The trap is the wrong bag and the wrong locker. Wrong bag: an 18%-butter chocolate or a compound marked contains vegetable fats in addition to cocoa butter, bought because the per-kilo looked kind — it will not flow or set as your recipes assume; or milk, white or filled stocked for a season. Wrong locker: the walk-in fridge, where a cold bag sweats and dries to grey sugar bloom; or the dry store against the engine-room bulkhead, where cycling past 18 °C blooms it the other way. Bloomed couverture re-tempers; a bloomed tart does not.

No supplier holds, so carry a fallback before the window closes.

The hedge is the route, not the freezer: any mainland pastry wholesaler on the winter itinerary sells the same 3 kg bags, so a boat without cool stowage buys per charter at January’s price. The honest uncertainty: El Niño explains part of the West African monsoon, not all of it (Joly & Voldoire 2009); Bahia is a warning from another continent, not a forecast for Soubré; and the newest crop model has West and Central African yields rising by 2060 on higher CO2 and wetter dry seasons, Côte d’Ivoire the exception (Asante et al. 2025). Buy the season, not the year.

You do not need a terminal to track this. Three readings tell you which way it broke:

One: NOAA’s ENSO update on the second Thursday of the month — 10 September, 8 October, 12 November. Very strong still on the page in November moves the call to likely; strong gone by then is the downgrade. Two: Ivorian arrivals in the weekly exporter tallies, printed by Reuters and Barchart — below 2025’s pace by 31 December confirms the call; level or above triggers the falsifier. Three: the ICCO daily price on the front page of icco.org, free and daily — above $7,500 is the call met; under $5,500 is the call missed.

The one thing

Buy the winter’s dark couverture this week at the bag step, hold it sealed below 18 °C, and let the El Niño main crop argue with your provisioner instead of with you — the one reading that changes it is the December arrivals count.

Tested at sea.
