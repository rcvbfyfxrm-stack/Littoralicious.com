The Source Standard: How Littoralicious Decides What Is True

Every food-science claim you read here now has to clear a named bar. This is that bar: the journals, the labs, the tells that separate a real source from a plausible one — and the eight tests we run before a sentence about your body is allowed to print.

What you'll get
  • The tier ladder we now grade every claim against — A, B, C, and reject — and what each one licenses you to say.
  • The journals and the laboratories that actually hold the food-science field up, named, by field.
  • Eight tests that separate a real source from a plausible one, runnable in about four minutes on a phone.

In February we published a sentence telling you that cocoa flavanols lower blood pressure, and we attached a citation to it: thirty-one randomised controlled trials, Circulation Research, 2022. It looked exactly like the sort of thing this publication should print. A named journal. A number. A study count.

That paper does not exist. We went looking for it this week and could not find it, because there is nothing to find. The claim itself is not fabricated — cocoa flavanols do lower blood pressure — but the evidence is a Cochrane review, and its answer is much smaller than the one we implied. Thirty-five trials, 1,804 people, a fall of about 1.8 mmHg. Real, measurable, and nothing like a treatment.

We got the direction right and the authority invented. That is worse than being wrong, because a reader cannot audit it. It is the failure this document exists to prevent.

A claim is not a fact because it is true. It is a fact because it is traceable. If you cannot follow a sentence back to the thing that supports it, it does not belong in a publication that asks tired people to change what they cook.

The ladder

The Evidence Grade Ladder

Four rungs. Every health, nutrition, physiology, chemistry or perception claim we print sits on one of them, and the rung decides how strongly we are allowed to phrase it.

The grade is not decoration. It governs the verb. Grade A evidence lets us write reduces. Grade B lets us write appears to reduce, in one good trial. Grade C lets us write the mechanism suggests, and only if we say in the same breath that it has not been shown in people. Nothing below C prints at all.

Grade What it is What we may write
A Systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Cochrane, PRISMA-compliant); large pre-registered randomised trials; assessments by EFSA, WHO, FAO/JECFA, FDA; composition data from USDA FoodData Central Plain declarative. “Cooking egg protein raises its digestibility from 51% to 91%.”
B One well-powered randomised trial; a strong prospective cohort; a consensus statement from a scientific society; named analytical chemistry in a first-quartile food journal Attributed and bounded. “In one trial of 7,447 people over 4.8 years…”
C Mechanistic, in vitro, cell-culture or animal work. The compound did something in a dish or in a mouse. Flagged as preliminary, every time. “Shown in cells, not in humans.”
Reject Press releases; supplement sellers; wellness sites; wikis; appeals to unnamed research; predatory journals; anything we cannot resolve to a real document Nothing. Cut it, or rewrite it as tradition and flavour rather than health.

The rule that does the most work: anything that cannot reach at least Grade B gets cut or reframed. Not softened — cut. A hedged bad claim is still a bad claim, and hedging is how most food writing smuggles nonsense past its own standards.

Where the work is published

The Journals That Count

Know the six or seven mastheads in your field and you can triage a citation in seconds, without reading a word of the paper.

Food science is not one field. It is at least four, and they publish in different places. A claim about protein digestibility, a claim about how a sauce holds, a claim about whether something lowers your cholesterol, and a claim about why lemon makes a dish taste brighter are four different literatures. Reaching for the wrong one is a common way to be confidently wrong.

Food chemistry and technology

This is where composition, reaction chemistry, texture, structure and processing live — the Maillard reaction, gelatinisation, emulsions, oxidative stability.

Taste, smell and sensory science

Where the claims about perception belong — umami synergy, bitterness suppression, why crystal shape changes perceived saltiness.

Chemical Senses, Food Quality and Preference, Journal of Sensory Studies, Appetite, and Physiology & Behavior carry nearly all of it. Add the International Journal of Gastronomy and Food Science for the specifically culinary work — it is the field's home for gastronomy research, and it is a smaller, less-cited journal than the titles above. That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to say so when we cite it, which is what we did with the umami piece.

Impact factor measures the journal, not the paper you are holding. A careful analytical study in a mid-tier food journal beats an underpowered trial in a famous one, every time. Use the masthead as a first filter and then judge the actual work. Anyone who cites the impact factor as the reason to believe a specific finding has told you they did not read it.

Who does the work

The Laboratories Doing The Work

Papers come from places, and the places have reputations you can learn once and use for years.

You will not visit any of these. Knowing them is still useful, because a name in a methods section tells you whether a result came from a group that has spent forty years on the question or from someone passing through.

InstitutionWhereWhat they own
Monell Chemical Senses Center Philadelphia Taste and smell. Founded 1968, and the only independent non-profit institute in the world dedicated to the chemical senses. This is where oleocanthal — the compound that makes good olive oil sting your throat — was identified.

The chefs' laboratories — the Fat Duck research kitchen, Modernist Cuisine, ChefSteps — are excellent trade sources and not peer-reviewed ones. They are superb on technique and unreviewed on physiology. We cite them for what happens in a pan. We do not cite them for what happens in a body.

The method

The Eight Tests

Run these in order. The first three kill most bad claims, and they take under a minute.

1. Does the document exist

Resolve the DOI. Paste the title into PubMed or Google Scholar. If a citation has a journal, a year and a volume but no findable paper, you have found a fabrication — and in an era when text is generated at scale, plausible-looking citations that resolve to nothing are the single most common failure mode in food writing. It is the one that caught us.

2. Does it say what the citation claims

Open the abstract. This sounds pedantic until you have watched a real paper get attached to the wrong claim. Our own acid piece credited sweet–sour suppression to Breslin and Beauchamp's 1997 Nature paper. That paper is real, excellent, and about salt suppressing bitterness. Right authors, right journal, wrong finding — and nothing about the citation looked wrong.

3. In humans, or in a dish

The most consequential question in nutrition. A compound that inhibits a pathway in cell culture has demonstrated almost nothing about what happens when you eat it: it has to survive cooking, digestion, absorption, and first-pass metabolism, and most of them do not. Our chicken soup article rested on a 2000 assay that slowed white-cell migration in a laboratory dish. It is a real and interesting result. It is not evidence that soup shortens a cold, and it now says so.

4. How many people, and for how long

Look for the n and the duration. Twenty people for four weeks is a pilot. Seven thousand people for five years is a finding. Neither is worthless; they license completely different sentences.

The two numbers that decide almost everything: how many and how long. Most of the nutrition claims that circulate as settled fact come from studies of a few dozen people over a few weeks — long enough to move a blood marker, nowhere near long enough to change anything you would notice.

5. Relative or absolute

“30% reduction” is meaningless until you know 30% of what. A drop from 6 cases per 1,000 to 4 is a 33% relative reduction and a 0.2 percentage-point absolute one. Relative figures are how modest findings are made to sound decisive — including by us, which is why the fat index now states its baselines.

6. Who paid

Read the funding and conflict-of-interest statements; they are at the bottom, and they are the most informative two lines in most papers. Industry funding does not invalidate a study. It does change what you should expect the study to have looked for. A cocoa trial funded by a chocolate manufacturer is worth reading and worth labelling.

7. Has anyone failed to replicate it

Search the claim alongside the words “failed to replicate” or “null”. Check the Retraction Watch database — since Crossref acquired it in September 2023 it has been free and public, and it takes ten seconds. This test is why our fat index now carries the STRENGTH trial next to REDUCE-IT: two large trials of concentrated omega-3, one positive, one null. Reporting only the positive one was a choice, and it was the wrong one.

8. Is this the best available source, or the first one you found

The escalation rule, and the one most often skipped. A decent primary study is not good enough if a systematic review of that same question exists. Always ask whether Cochrane, EFSA or WHO has already done the aggregation, because if they have, their answer supersedes yours.

Test eight is where most honest writers fail, because the first decent source you find is genuinely decent — it is just not the best one. Stopping there is how our cocoa claim ended up citing a journal that never published it, when a Cochrane review sat one search away with a smaller, truer number.

Resolve the DOI. Read the abstract. Ask whether it was humans. Ask whether a review already exists. If a claim survives those four, it is probably safe to write; if it fails any one of them, it is not a matter of softening the wording — the claim is not yet a claim.

The tells

The Tells Of A Bad Source

Learn the surface features and you can reject most of what circulates without opening it.

  • An appeal to research with no research named. The phrase does the work of a citation while committing to nothing — the single most reliable indicator that no paper is being described.
  • A journal you have never heard of publishing outside its stated field. Predatory journals publish anything for a fee and mimic legitimate titles closely. Check whether it is indexed in PubMed, Scopus or the Web of Science core collection.
  • A number with no denominator. “Increases absorption by 2,000%” means nothing without the dose it was measured at. That figure is real — it comes from ten volunteers given 20 mg of piperine with two grams of curcumin. A pinch of pepper is not that.
  • The mechanism given as the evidence. “It activates AMPK, which regulates metabolism” describes a pathway, not an outcome. Mechanism is how a thing would work if it worked.
  • A database cited as a journal. PubMed and PMC are libraries. Citing them tells the reader nothing about who did the work or where it was reviewed. Our own crew-welfare piece did this, and has been corrected.
  • Round, memorable, uncited numbers. “84 trace minerals.” “10,000 years.” “1,000 receptors.” These travel because they are quotable, and they are usually either unsourced or a misremembered version of something narrower.
  • A single research group producing all the positive results. Not fraud — but a finding that has never been reproduced by anyone else is a finding at half strength, and should be reported that way.
The argument

What Actually Makes The Difference

The line between a real source and a plausible one is not prestige. It is whether the claim can be checked and, in principle, proved wrong.

Every test above is a version of one question: can a reader who does not trust me go and find out whether I am right. That is the whole of it. A source is good to the degree that it makes your own disagreement possible.

This is why a Cochrane review outranks a famous trial, why a named analytical method outranks a confident assertion, and why a paper with a funding disclosure outranks one without. None of those are about authority. They are about exposure — each one hands the reader a way to catch the author out.

Prestige tells you who to believe. Traceability tells you how to check. Only one of those survives contact with a reader who is smarter than you.

There is a second reason, specific to this publication. We write for people who feed others for a living. A chef reading at eleven at night, mid-charter, cannot audit us. They will put the thing on a plate and hand it to twelve people, some of whom are on medication, some of whom are pregnant, some of whom believe what a chef tells them about food because a chef is supposed to know. The asymmetry between how easily we can write a sentence and how much weight it carries at the other end is the entire justification for a standard this heavy.

So the operating principle is not be accurate. It is stricter than that. Omit before you fabricate. A gap is honest. A confident wrong answer costs the reader something, and costs us the only thing we have.

The proof

The Standard, Applied To Ourselves First

A standard nobody has been held to is a wish. Here is what it cost us on its first run.

We ran every published article on this site through the ladder and the eight tests. Thirty-two articles, several hundred checkable claims. The recipe work came through nearly clean — it is built on McGee, Modernist Cuisine, Parks, Forkish, and it cites them properly. The health and nutrition work did not.

6numbers materially wrong
9claims overstated or missing a live dispute

The corrections that hurt most were the ones where we had been right about the direction and careless about the warrant:

  • The salt index gave sodium figures per tablespoon that were measured per teaspoon — a three-fold seasoning error in a piece whose whole argument is precision.
  • It also claimed a serving of spinach carries more iron than a year of pink salt. The real analysis (Fayet-Moore, Foods, 2020) says the opposite on the arithmetic, and makes the better point anyway: you would need about 30 g of pink salt a day, six times the WHO sodium ceiling, before its minerals mattered.
  • The mushroom piece said dried shiitake carries more free glutamate than Parmesan. It carries slightly less — and our own chicken soup article had the correct figure for Parmesan sitting in it the whole time. We contradicted ourselves across two pages and neither of us noticed.
  • The fire-and-cooking essay called Wrangham's cooking hypothesis settled. It is the best account available and it is genuinely contested: Roebroeks and Villa put habitual fire at 300,000–400,000 years ago, long after the anatomy it is meant to explain.
  • The flavour-pairing piece presented shared-compound pairing as a law. The paper it rests on found that Western cuisines follow it and East Asian cuisines deliberately do the reverse.

Every one of those is now fixed and marked. None of them made the articles worse to read. Several made them better, because a stated limit is more interesting than a smooth assertion — the shiitake sentence is more useful now that it names Parmesan's actual number, and the cooking-hypothesis section is far more alive now that it has an argument in it.

The rule

The Rule From Here

One paragraph, and it governs everything published under this masthead.

Every claim about health, nutrition, physiology, food chemistry, microbiology or perception is graded before it prints. It carries the highest-tier source available, named in full — author, title, journal, year — not a database, not a masthead alone, and never a study that has not been opened. Anything that cannot reach Grade B is cut or rewritten as tradition and flavour. Anything preliminary says so in the same sentence. Live disputes are surfaced, not hidden. Funding is disclosed. And where we have got something wrong, the correction is printed in the article rather than quietly swapped in.

The uncomfortable part of that last clause is that it applies retrospectively, which is how this article came to exist. We did not find these errors because a reader complained. We found them because we went looking, and we published what we found.

Cook from a source you can check. That is the entire standard, and everything above is just the long version.
