The Burger Grind: Three Cuts, Two Passes, Salt at the Pan

Chuck, brisket and short rib at 2:1:1, ground twice from near-frozen and salted only at the pan: an open, tender grain no tub of mince can give — and the honest line on doneness.

Cut one open and the grain should fall apart in loose, glossy threads — crumbly on the fork, wet in the mouth — not a disc that bounces back like a sausage. A tub of mince cannot get there: one muscle at one fat level, ground warm and fine, often salted at the plant. Three cuts, two cold passes, and salt withheld until the pan can have it. And because this is the one dish where the safe number and the good number disagree, that argument is here in the open, not in a footnote.

The one idea: salt the crust, never the mix. Salt in raw mince swells the muscle fibres and pulls myosin out of them, and myosin is glue — heated, it sets into the springy bind of a frankfurter. Season only the outside as it meets the pan and the inside stays exactly as loose as the grinder left it.

Where it comes from

The name is a port, and the dish began as cargo. Hannah Glasse’s Art of Cookery picked up a “Hamburgh sausage” between its 1755 and 1774 printings: a pound of beef minced with more than its own weight of suet, spiced, salted with bay-salt and common salt, stuffed tight into “the largest gut you can find” and smoked in the chimney for a week or ten days — “and they will keep a year”. Salted, fat-heavy, preserved: a thing built to travel, eaten sliced on toast.

By 1896 it was American, fresh and lean. Fannie Farmer’s Boston Cooking-School Cook Book gives “Hamburg Steaks” — “Chop finely one pound lean raw beef; season highly with salt, pepper, and a few drops onion juice” — and her table of cuts sends the neck and the lower round to it: the cheap, hard-working muscles. The Hamburg steak exists to make tough beef eat tender, which is why this grind is chuck, brisket and short rib and not fillet. White Castle put the small griddled patty in a bun at Wichita in 1921 and sold it by the sack; the thick pub patty and the thin smash have been the same grind travelling two ways ever since. Farmer salted her mince before she shaped it. Step four is where this recipe parts company with her.

Colour is not a thermometer. In one trial, patties were cooked to endpoints from 55 to 77 °C and cut open: some of the patties cooked to the low end were already brown right through, and the juices “never ran clear” at any temperature; the authors’ advice was “cook until juices lack redness”. Brown inside proves nothing, pink inside proves nothing; only a probe does. — Hague et al., Journal of Food Science 59 (1994) 465–470. DOI: 10.1111/j.1365-2621.1994.tb05539.x

That is the safety half of the argument, and why a probe thermometer sits in the kit before the grinder does. The other half is what fat and salt are doing inside the patty long before it meets the pan.

Fat is less a flavour here than a hedge against heat. Patties ground to 5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30% fat were cooked to 71 and 77 °C: cooking loss held at 24.7–26.0% all the way up to 20% fat, then climbed — 28.9% at 25%, 32.1% at 30% — while the 5 and 10% patties were firmer, crumblier and drier in the mouth than anything from 20% up, and the gap between lean and fat widened at the hotter endpoint. Twenty per cent is the knee of the curve: below it the patty eats dry, above it the fat runs off into the pan. This blend lands at 20–25% on purpose. — Troutt, Hunt, Johnson, Claus et al., Journal of Food Science 57 (1992) 25–29. DOI: 10.1111/j.1365-2621.1992.tb05416.x

Method

0/0 steps done

  1. Cold is not a precaution here. It is the recipe.

    Dice and chill everything. Cut the three cuts into 2–3 cm dice, gristle and silverskin out, fat left in. Spread on a tray and freeze 25–30 min, until the edges are firm and frosted and the centre still gives under a thumb. Auger, plate, blade and the catching bowl go into the freezer with it. Warm fat smears; cold fat cuts.

    Why
    Under a warm blade the fat does not cut, it spreads — a film over every strand of lean that cooks grey, weeps and never browns. Near-frozen fat shears into discrete chips that survive the grind as pockets of juice. Four degrees is also where the safety clock runs slow: USDA keeps raw mince at 4 °C (40 °F) or below. — J. Kenji López-Alt, The Food Lab (2015), on the cold grind; USDA FSIS, Ground Beef and Food Safety

  2. The first pass breaks the dice. It is not supposed to make mince.

    First grind, coarse. Fit the 10 mm (⅜ in) plate. Feed the frozen-edged dice steadily into the throat — never force it — and let the grind fall into the cold bowl set over ice. The three cuts go through mixed. If grey paste smears at the plate, the meat is warm: stop, and back to the freezer for 10 min.

    Why
    Two passes do what one cannot. The coarse pass opens the dice without working them; the finer pass sets the final grain and gives a thick patty just enough cohesion to survive a flip without salt. One fine pass through half-warm meat — the tub method — pastes fat and lean together.

  3. The second pass is where a thick patty decides whether it will hold.

    Re-chill, then the second grind, finer. Ten minutes in the freezer, bowl and all, then swap to the 4.5 mm (3/16 in) plate — or 6 mm for a looser, pub-style patty — and pass it once more over ice. Do not stir it afterwards. The fat should still show as separate white flecks; if it has gone pink and uniform, it was warm.

    Why
    Cohesion in a burger comes from one of two things: extracted myosin, which this recipe refuses, or mechanical entanglement of the strands, which the finer pass supplies. A 150 g, 2 cm patty needs some of the second to survive a flip and a swell; a smash needs none, which is why the smash in the fold below stops at one coarse pass.

  4. Fannie Farmer said “season highly with salt” before shaping. This is where we leave her.

    Portion, shape, dimple — no salt. Weigh 150 g a patty. Gather loosely, press to 2 cm thick and a thumb wider than the bun, and dimple the centre with two fingers. Tray, cover, fridge 30 min — or up to 24 h. The salt stays in its tin.

    Why
    In salt solutions like those used in meat processing, myofibrils swell to about twice their volume as the thick filaments — the myosin — begin to be extracted from them; that extracted protein is what binds a sausage, and 1.75% salt in lean meat is enough to set a heat-stable gel of it. Side by side, salt worked in before shaping gives a springy, sausage-like patty; salt on the surface at the pan gives a loose one. The dimple: the edge sets first and the disc contracts as it cooks, so a flat patty domes. — Offer & Trinick, Meat Science 8 (1983) 245–281, DOI: 10.1016/0309-1740(83)90013-X; Ruusunen & Puolanne, Meat Science 70 (2005) 531–541, DOI: 10.1016/j.meatsci.2004.07.016; López-Alt, The Food Lab (2015), the salt test

  5. A crust is a surface that has run out of water, and only then begun to cook.

    Sear hard, salt at the pan. A heavy pan or plancha with a film of dripping, heated until it just smokes — around 230 °C (450 °F) at the surface. Lay the patties in, not touching, and salt the top face now — a three-finger pinch, about 1 g — with the pepper. Leave them 2–3 min, until the crust releases on its own; flip once, salt the second face. Never press a thick patty; the smash is a different dish with its own rules.

    Why
    Browning is the Maillard reaction between the meat’s amino acids and sugars, and its rate climbs steeply with temperature — but a wet surface is pinned at 100 °C by its own steam until it dries. The pan’s first job is to dry the face of the patty in seconds; a loose, cold grind offers a dry face that browns fast, a warm, worked one steams itself grey. Pressing squeezes out the juice you ground the fat in to keep. — Martins, Jongen & van Boekel, Trends in Food Science & Technology 11 (2000) 364–373, DOI: 10.1016/S0924-2244(01)00022-X

  6. The honest step. The safe number and the good number are not the same number, and you should know which one you are serving.

    Probe, pull, rest. Probe from the side into the centre. USDA’s line for any ground beef is 71 °C (160 °F) — the number that protects children, pregnancy, the elderly and anyone immunocompromised. The chef’s medium-rare is a pull at 52–54 °C and a three-minute rest to 55–57 °C (131–135 °F): an informed risk, offered only to guests who choose it. Grinding folds whatever was on the surface of the muscle through the whole mass, so the centre of a patty is a surface. A grind you made yourself from intact whole cuts, kept cold and cooked within the day, lowers that risk. It does not remove it. Rest, toast the buns in the pan fat, build.

    Why
    The number moved for a reason. Between November 1992 and February 1993 more than 500 laboratory-confirmed E. coli O157:H7 infections and four deaths across four states were traced to regular-sized hamburger patties from one restaurant chain; the FDA’s interim response raised the recommended internal temperature for cooked hamburgers to 155 °F (68 °C), and USDA’s consumer figure has since settled at 71 °C. Why 71 eats drier than 57: myofibrillar proteins unfold from about 30 °C and gel by 45–50, collagen shrinks between 53 and 63 — each stage wrings water out of the network, and the blend’s 20–25% fat is what lets a 71 °C patty still eat well. — CDC, MMWR 42(14) (1993) 258–263; USDA FSIS; Tornberg, Meat Science 70 (2005) 493–508, DOI: 10.1016/j.meatsci.2004.11.021; Troutt et al. 1992

Burger night is the one the crew asks for by name. Guests get theirs plated, 55 °C, on the good bun; the crew get the same grind smashed on the plancha ten minutes later and eat it standing at the pass, with the onion smell still hanging in the galley. It is eaten with the hands, immediately, and without ceremony — the first one goes to whoever has the watch.

The same 1.8 kg makes twelve thick patties or twelve doubles. A smash needs no bind, so the grind can stop after the coarse pass — and it is the safe burger by geometry: a 5 mm patty passes 71 °C in the time it takes the edge to lace.

Once you own the ratio — two chuck, one brisket, one short rib; cold; two passes; salt at the pan — the burger is yours: dry-aged trim for funk, marrow for richness, a hand-chop when the grinder is ashore. Cook once, serve two tiers: the guests’ 150 g patty pulled to 55–57 °C on the good bun, and the same grind smashed thin for the crew — cooked through by its own geometry, and probably the better burger. Glasse’s sausage was salted and smoked to keep a year; yours is unsalted and cold to be eaten within the day. That is the whole distance the dish has travelled, and the whole recipe.
